# Book XI — Brother Ivan Fyodorovich
## VII. The Second Visit to Smerdyakov

<!-- B11-C07-P001 -->
By that time Smerdyakov had been discharged from the hospital. Ivan Fyodorovich knew his new lodgings: that very crooked little log house with two rooms separated by an entrance passage. Marya Kondratyevna and her mother occupied one room, and Smerdyakov the other, on his own. God knows on what terms he had settled there—whether he lived rent-free or paid for it. Later it was supposed that he had moved in as Marya Kondratyevna's fiancé and was living there free for the time being. Both mother and daughter respected him greatly and regarded him as a person superior to themselves. After knocking until someone answered, Ivan Fyodorovich entered the passage and, following Marya Kondratyevna's directions, went straight to the left, into the “white room” occupied by Smerdyakov. It had a tiled stove, which had been heated fiercely. The walls were adorned with blue wallpaper, though it was torn all over, and beneath it, in the cracks, small reddish cockroaches swarmed in such dreadful numbers that an unceasing rustle filled the room. The furniture was meager: two benches along the two walls, and two chairs beside the table. The table, although plain wood, was nevertheless covered with a cloth patterned in pink. On each of the two small windows stood a pot of geraniums. In the corner was an icon case with icons. On the table stood a small, badly dented brass samovar and a tray with two cups. But Smerdyakov had already finished his tea, and the samovar had gone out... He himself sat on the bench at the table, looking into a notebook and tracing something with a pen. A little bottle of ink stood beside him, as did a low cast-iron candlestick holding, however, a stearin candle. From Smerdyakov's face Ivan Fyodorovich immediately concluded that he had fully recovered from his illness. His face was fresher and fuller, his tuft brushed up, his hair smoothed at the temples. He wore a patterned, cotton-padded dressing gown, though it was very shabby and considerably frayed. On his nose were spectacles, which Ivan Fyodorovich had never seen him wear before. This utterly trivial detail suddenly seemed to double Ivan Fyodorovich's anger: “A creature like that, and spectacles too!” Smerdyakov slowly raised his head and looked steadily through his spectacles at the visitor; then he quietly took them off and half rose from the bench, but somehow not at all respectfully—lazily, even—solely to observe the barest necessary courtesy that could hardly be dispensed with. All this flashed before Ivan in an instant, and he grasped and noticed it all at once, above all Smerdyakov's look, decidedly malicious, unwelcoming, even haughty: as though to say, “What are you prowling about for? We settled everything then; why have you come again?” Ivan Fyodorovich could barely restrain himself.

“It's hot in here,” he said, still standing, and unbuttoned his coat.

<!-- B11-C07-P003 -->
“Take it off, sir,” Smerdyakov permitted him.

<!-- B11-C07-P004 -->
Ivan Fyodorovich took off his coat and threw it on the bench, seized a chair with trembling hands, pulled it quickly up to the table, and sat down. Smerdyakov had managed to settle back onto his bench before him.

<!-- B11-C07-P005 -->
“First of all, are we alone?” Ivan Fyodorovich asked sternly and rapidly. “They can't hear us from over there?”

<!-- B11-C07-P006 -->
“Nobody will hear anything, sir. You saw the passage yourself.”

<!-- B11-C07-P007 -->
“Listen, my dear fellow: what was that nonsense you came out with when I was leaving you at the hospital—that if I kept quiet about your being so good at pretending to have a fit, you wouldn't tell the investigator everything about our conversation at the gate? What does ‘everything’ mean? What could you have meant then? Were you threatening me? Had I entered into some sort of alliance with you, or what? Am I afraid of you, or what?”

<!-- B11-C07-P008 -->
Ivan Fyodorovich said this in a fury, plainly and deliberately making it known that he scorned all roundabout approaches and was playing with his cards on the table. Smerdyakov's eyes flashed with malice, his left eye began blinking, and he immediately gave his own answer, though with his customary restraint and deliberation: as if to say, “You want it plain? Then here is plain speaking for you.”

<!-- B11-C07-P009 -->
“What I meant then, and why I said it, was that, knowing beforehand about this murder of your own father, you left him to be sacrificed; and so that people should not afterward draw some bad conclusion about your feelings, or perhaps about something else besides—that was what I promised not to disclose to the authorities.”

<!-- B11-C07-P010 -->
Although Smerdyakov spoke unhurriedly and apparently had command of himself, something firm and insistent could now be heard even in his voice, something malicious and brazenly defiant. He stared boldly at Ivan Fyodorovich, whose vision actually swam for the first moment.

<!-- B11-C07-P011 -->
“How? What? Are you in your right mind or aren't you?”

<!-- B11-C07-P012 -->
“Perfectly in my right mind, sir.”

<!-- B11-C07-P013 -->
“Did I know about the murder then?” Ivan Fyodorovich finally cried, pounding the table hard with his fist. “What does ‘something else besides’ mean? Speak, you scoundrel!”

<!-- B11-C07-P014 -->
Smerdyakov remained silent and continued examining Ivan Fyodorovich with the same insolent stare.

<!-- B11-C07-P015 -->
“Speak, you stinking rogue! What ‘something else besides’?” the other man yelled.

<!-- B11-C07-P016 -->
“By ‘something else besides’ I meant just now that perhaps you yourself very much wished your father dead at the time.”

<!-- B11-C07-P017 -->
Ivan Fyodorovich leaped up and struck him on the shoulder with his fist as hard as he could, so that he reeled back against the wall. In an instant his whole face was flooded with tears, and, saying, “You ought to be ashamed, sir, to hit a weak man!” he suddenly covered his eyes with his cotton handkerchief, patterned in blue checks and thoroughly soiled with mucus, and sank into quiet, tearful weeping. About a minute passed.

<!-- B11-C07-P018 -->
“Enough! Stop it!” Ivan Fyodorovich said commandingly at last, sitting down again. “Don't exhaust what's left of my patience.”

<!-- B11-C07-P019 -->
Smerdyakov took his rag away from his eyes. Every line of his puckered face expressed the injury he had just suffered.

<!-- B11-C07-P020 -->
“So you thought then, you scoundrel, that I wanted to join Dmitri in killing Father?”

<!-- B11-C07-P021 -->
“I didn't know your thoughts at the time, sir,” Smerdyakov said resentfully, “and that's why I stopped you as you were coming through the gate, to test you on that very point.”

<!-- B11-C07-P022 -->
“Test what? What?”

<!-- B11-C07-P023 -->
“Precisely this circumstance: did you or did you not want your father to be killed as soon as possible?”

<!-- B11-C07-P024 -->
What outraged Ivan Fyodorovich most was this insistent, insolent tone, which Smerdyakov stubbornly refused to abandon.

<!-- B11-C07-P025 -->
“You killed him!” he suddenly exclaimed. Smerdyakov smiled contemptuously.

<!-- B11-C07-P026 -->
“You know perfectly well yourself that I didn't kill him. And I thought an intelligent man would have nothing more to say on the subject.”

<!-- B11-C07-P027 -->
“But why, why did you form such a suspicion of me then?”

<!-- B11-C07-P028 -->
“As you already know, out of sheer fear, sir. For my situation then was such that, shaking with fear, I suspected everyone. I decided to test you too, sir, for if you also, I thought, want the same thing as your brother, then that's the end of the whole business, and I'll perish along with it, like a fly.”

<!-- B11-C07-P029 -->
“Listen, that isn't what you said two weeks ago.”

<!-- B11-C07-P030 -->
“I meant exactly the same thing when I spoke to you in the hospital, only I supposed you'd understand without unnecessary words and would prefer not to speak openly yourself, being such a very intelligent man, sir.”

<!-- B11-C07-P031 -->
“Well, just listen to you! But answer me, answer, I insist: what was it exactly, what could I possibly have done then to plant in your vile soul such a degrading suspicion of me?”

<!-- B11-C07-P032 -->
“You could never have killed him yourself, sir, and didn't want to; but as for wanting someone else to kill him, you did want that.”

<!-- B11-C07-P033 -->
“And how calmly, how calmly he says it! But why should I want it? What the devil should I want it for?”

<!-- B11-C07-P034 -->
“What do you mean, what the devil for, sir? What about the inheritance?” Smerdyakov retorted venomously, even with a kind of vindictiveness. “After your father's death, each of you three brothers could have received nearly forty thousand, perhaps even more, sir. But if Fyodor Pavlovich had married that lady, Agrafena Alexandrovna, she'd have had the whole fortune transferred to her the minute they were married, for she's far from stupid, sir, so that you three brothers wouldn't have inherited even two rubles from your father. And how far off was the wedding then? A hair's breadth, sir: that lady only had to crook her little finger at him like this, and he'd have run straight to church after her with his tongue hanging out.”

<!-- B11-C07-P035 -->
Ivan Fyodorovich restrained himself with painful effort.

<!-- B11-C07-P036 -->
“Very well,” he said at last. “You see, I haven't jumped up, haven't beaten you, haven't killed you. Go on: according to you, then, I had my brother Dmitri marked out for it and was counting on him?”

<!-- B11-C07-P037 -->
“How could you not count on him, sir? If he killed him, he'd be stripped of all his rights of nobility, his ranks and property, and sent into exile. Then his share of your father's estate would go to you and your brother Alexei Fyodorovich, equally, sir. Which means you'd each get sixty thousand instead of forty. You were certainly counting on Dmitri Fyodorovich then!”

<!-- B11-C07-P038 -->
“What I put up with from you! Listen, you wretch: if I'd been counting on anyone then, it would certainly have been you, not Dmitri, and I swear I even had a presentiment of some vile act from you... then... I remember my impression!”

<!-- B11-C07-P039 -->
“And for one little moment I thought then that you were counting on me too,” Smerdyakov grinned mockingly. “Which only betrayed you to me all the more, for if you had that presentiment about me and were leaving at the same time, it was exactly as though you'd told me: you may kill my father, and I won't stand in your way.”

<!-- B11-C07-P040 -->
“Scoundrel! That's how you understood it!”

<!-- B11-C07-P041 -->
“And all because of that Chermashnya, sir. For pity's sake! You were setting off for Moscow and had refused all your father's requests to go to Chermashnya! Yet at one foolish word from me you suddenly agreed! And why did you need to agree to that Chermashnya then? If you went to Chermashnya instead of Moscow for no reason, solely at my word, then you must have been expecting something from me.”

<!-- B11-C07-P042 -->
“No, I swear, no!” Ivan yelled, grinding his teeth.

<!-- B11-C07-P043 -->
“How can you say no, sir? On the contrary, for those words of mine, you, as your father's son, ought first of all to have taken me to the police station and had me flogged... at the very least, beaten me about the face then and there. But instead, if you please, without getting angry in the slightest, you immediately and amicably do exactly as my very foolish words suggest and go away, which was quite absurd, sir, since you ought to have stayed to protect your father's life... How could I fail to draw that conclusion?”

<!-- B11-C07-P044 -->
Ivan sat scowling, both fists pressed convulsively against his knees.

<!-- B11-C07-P045 -->
“Yes, it's a pity I didn't beat you about the face,” he said with a bitter smile. “I couldn't have dragged you to the police station then: who would have believed me, and what could I have pointed to? But as for beating you... ah, a pity it didn't occur to me! Even if hitting people is forbidden, I'd have beaten that mug of yours to a pulp.”

<!-- B11-C07-P046 -->
Smerdyakov looked at him with something close to enjoyment.

<!-- B11-C07-P047 -->
“In the ordinary circumstances of life,” he said in the self-satisfied, doctrinaire tone with which he had once argued with Grigory Vasilyevich about religion and teased him while standing at Fyodor Pavlovich's table, “in the ordinary circumstances of life, blows to the face are indeed forbidden by law nowadays, and everyone's stopped beating people, sir. But in exceptional circumstances, not only here but all over the world—even in the most complete French republic—they go on beating people just as they did in the days of Adam and Eve, sir, and they'll never stop. Yet even in an exceptional circumstance you didn't dare, sir.”

<!-- B11-C07-P048 -->
“Why are you learning French words?” Ivan nodded toward the notebook on the table.

<!-- B11-C07-P049 -->
“And why shouldn't I learn them, sir, to further my education, thinking that perhaps one day I myself may find myself in those happy parts of Europe?”

<!-- B11-C07-P050 -->
“Listen, you monster,” Ivan said, his eyes flashing and his whole body shaking, “I'm not afraid of your accusations. Testify against me however you like, and if I haven't beaten you to death this minute, it's only because I suspect you of this crime and intend to bring you to trial. I'll expose you yet!”

<!-- B11-C07-P051 -->
“In my opinion you'd do better to keep quiet, sir. For what can you report against me, innocent as I am, and who will believe you? Only if you begin, I'll tell everything too, sir, for how could I fail to defend myself?”

<!-- B11-C07-P052 -->
“You think I'm afraid of you now?”

<!-- B11-C07-P053 -->
“They may not believe all these things I've just told you in court, sir, but the public will believe them, and you'll be disgraced, sir.”

<!-- B11-C07-P054 -->
“That means, once again, ‘It's always interesting talking to an intelligent man’—eh?” Ivan said through clenched teeth.

<!-- B11-C07-P055 -->
“Exactly right, sir. Then be intelligent.”

Ivan Fyodorovich rose, trembling all over with indignation, put on his coat, and, without answering Smerdyakov again or even looking at him, hurried out of the room. The fresh evening air revived him. The moon shone brightly in the sky. A terrible nightmare of thoughts and feelings seethed in his soul. “Go and report Smerdyakov at once? But report what? He's innocent all the same. On the contrary, he'll accuse me. Really, why did I go to Chermashnya then? Why, why?” Ivan Fyodorovich asked himself. “Yes, of course I was expecting something, and he's right...” And for the hundredth time he remembered how, on his last night at his father's, he had listened to him from the stairs; but now the memory hurt him so much that he stopped short as though pierced through. “Yes, that was what I was waiting for then, it's true! I wanted it, I specifically wanted the murder! Did I want the murder, did I?.. I must kill Smerdyakov!.. If I don't dare kill Smerdyakov now, life isn't worth living!..” Without going home, Ivan Fyodorovich went straight to Katerina Ivanovna's and frightened her by his appearance: he was like a madman. He told her his entire conversation with Smerdyakov, down to the last detail. He could not calm down, however much she tried to soothe him, but kept walking about the room and speaking in a strange, disjointed way. At last he sat down, rested his elbows on the table, held his head in both hands, and uttered a strange aphorism:

<!-- B11-C07-P057 -->
“If it wasn't Dmitri who killed him but Smerdyakov, then of course I share his guilt, because I put him up to it. Whether I put him up to it—I don't know yet. But if it was he who killed him and not Dmitri, then of course I'm a murderer too.”

After hearing this, Katerina Ivanovna rose silently, went to her writing desk, unlocked a small box that stood on it, took out a piece of paper, and laid it in front of Ivan. This paper was the very document Ivan Fyodorovich later described to Alyosha as “mathematical proof” that his brother Dmitri had killed their father. It was a letter Mitya had written drunkenly to Katerina Ivanovna on the same evening he had met Alyosha in the field on his way to the monastery, after the scene at Katerina Ivanovna's when Grushenka had insulted her. After parting from Alyosha, Mitya had rushed off to Grushenka; whether he saw her is unknown, but by night he had ended up at the Capital City tavern, where he got thoroughly drunk. Drunk, he demanded pen and paper and composed a weighty document against himself. It was a frenzied, wordy, incoherent letter, a “drunken” letter in the fullest sense. It was like a drunk coming home and telling his wife or someone in the household, with extraordinary heat, how he had just been insulted, what a scoundrel the man who insulted him was, what a fine fellow he himself was by contrast, and how he would give that scoundrel what for—all of it at great, great length, incoherently and excitedly, pounding his fists on the table and shedding drunken tears. The paper supplied to him at the tavern was a dirty scrap of ordinary, poor-quality writing paper with some sort of bill written on the back. His drunken verbosity had evidently run out of room, and Mitya had filled not only all the margins: even the last lines were written crosswise over the earlier writing. The letter ran as follows: “Fatal Katya! Tomorrow I'll get the money and pay you back your three thousand, and farewell—you woman of great wrath, but farewell also, my love! Let us end it! Tomorrow I'll try to get it from everyone, and if I can't get it from people, I give you my word of honor I'll go to Father and smash his head in and take it from under his pillow, if only Ivan goes away. I'll go to penal servitude, but I'll repay the three thousand. And you, farewell. I bow to the ground before you, for I've been a scoundrel to you. Forgive me. No, better not forgive me: it'll be easier for both of us! Better penal servitude than your love, because I love another, and today you came to know her all too well—how can you forgive me? I'll kill the man who robbed me! I'll go away from all of you to the East, to know no one. Not her either, for you're not the only tormentor; she is one too. Farewell!

“P. S. I write a curse, yet I adore you! I hear it in my breast. One string remains and it rings. Better to break my heart in two! I'll kill myself, but first the dog all the same. I'll wrench the three out of him and throw it to you. Though I've been a scoundrel to you, I'm no thief! Wait for the three thousand. Under the dog's mattress, a pink ribbon. I'm not the thief; I'll kill the man who robbed me. Katya, don't look down on me: Dimitri is no thief, but a murderer! Killed his father and ruined himself, so as to stand and not endure your pride. And not love you.

“PP. S. I kiss your feet, farewell!

“PP. SS. Katya, pray to God that people give me the money. Then I won't be covered in blood, but if they don't—blood! Kill me!

“Your slave and enemy,

“D. Karamazov.”

When Ivan had read the “document,” he rose convinced. So his brother had killed him, not Smerdyakov. If not Smerdyakov, then not he, Ivan, either. This letter suddenly acquired mathematical significance in his eyes. He could no longer have any doubts about Mitya's guilt. Incidentally, it had never occurred to Ivan that Mitya might have committed the murder together with Smerdyakov, nor would that have fitted the facts. Ivan was completely reassured. The next morning he recalled Smerdyakov and his mockery only with contempt. A few days later he was even astonished that he could have been so painfully hurt by the man's suspicions. He decided to despise him and forget him. A month passed like this. He no longer asked anyone about Smerdyakov, though once or twice he heard in passing that the man was very ill and not in his right mind. “He'll end up mad,” the young doctor Varvinsky once said of him, and Ivan remembered it. During the last week of that month Ivan himself began feeling very unwell. He had already gone for advice to the doctor from Moscow whom Katerina Ivanovna had sent for and who arrived just before the trial! And it was precisely at this time that his relations with Katerina Ivanovna grew strained to the utmost. They were something like two enemies in love with each other. Katerina Ivanovna's returns to Mitya, momentary but intense, now drove Ivan into complete frenzy. Strangely, until the very last scene we described at Katerina Ivanovna's, when Alyosha came to her from Mitya, he, Ivan, had not heard her express doubt of Mitya's guilt once during the whole month, despite all her “returns” to him, which Ivan hated so much. It is also remarkable that, while he felt his hatred of Mitya growing every day, he understood at the same time that he hated him not for Katya's “returns” to him, but precisely because he had killed their father! He felt and recognized this fully himself. Nevertheless, some ten days before the trial he went to see Mitya and proposed a plan of escape—a plan evidently conceived long before. Besides the chief reason that prompted him to take this step, something else was to blame: a scratch on his heart that would not heal, left by a little remark of Smerdyakov's, to the effect that it would benefit him, Ivan, to have his brother convicted, since his and Alyosha's inheritance from their father would then rise from forty to sixty thousand. He decided to sacrifice thirty thousand of his own money to arrange Mitya's escape. Returning from that visit, he felt terribly sad and troubled: he had suddenly begun to feel that he wanted the escape not merely in order to sacrifice thirty thousand on it and heal the scratch, but for some other reason too. “Is it because in my soul I'm just as much a murderer?” he began asking himself. Something remote but burning tormented his soul. Above all, his pride suffered terribly throughout that month, but more of that later... When, after his conversation with Alyosha, Ivan Fyodorovich grasped the bell at his lodgings and suddenly decided to go to Smerdyakov, he was obeying a particular indignation that had suddenly boiled up in his breast. He suddenly remembered how Katerina Ivanovna had just exclaimed in front of Alyosha: “It was you, only you, who convinced me he”—meaning Mitya—“was the murderer!” The memory left Ivan dumbfounded: never in his life had he tried to convince her that Mitya was the murderer. On the contrary, when he came back from Smerdyakov, he had voiced suspicions of himself in front of her. It was she, on the contrary, she who had produced the “document” and proved his brother's guilt to him! And now she herself suddenly exclaimed, “I've been to Smerdyakov myself!” When had she been? Ivan knew nothing about it. So she was not so certain of Mitya's guilt after all! And what could Smerdyakov have told her? What, what exactly had he told her? Terrible anger blazed in his heart. He could not understand how, half an hour before, he had let those words pass without shouting out then and there. He let go of the bell and set off for Smerdyakov. “Perhaps I'll kill him this time,” he thought on the way.
