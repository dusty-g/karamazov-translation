# Book IX: The Preliminary Investigation
## IX. They Take Mitya Away

<!-- B09-C09-P001 -->
When the record had been signed, Nikolai Parfenovich solemnly addressed the accused and read him a “Ruling” stating that on such-and-such a day in such-and-such a year, at such-and-such a place, the judicial investigator of such-and-such a district court, having questioned such-and-such a person—that is, Mitya—as the accused in such-and-such offenses, all the charges being carefully set out, and taking into consideration that the accused, while denying guilt in the crimes imputed to him, had presented nothing in his defense, whereas the witnesses—such and such—and the circumstances—such and such—fully incriminated him, pursuant to such-and-such articles of the Penal Code, and so forth, had ruled: to prevent such-and-such a person, Mitya, from evading investigation and trial, to confine him in such-and-such a prison; the accused to be informed of this, and a copy of the present ruling to be forwarded to the assistant prosecutor, and so on, and so on. In short, Mitya was told that from this moment he was a prisoner, and that he would now be taken to town and confined in a very unpleasant place. Mitya listened attentively and merely shrugged.

<!-- B09-C09-P002 -->
“Well, gentlemen, I don’t blame you, I’m ready... I understand there’s nothing else you can do.”

<!-- B09-C09-P003 -->
Nikolai Parfenovich gently explained that the local police officer, Mavriky Mavrikievich, who happened to be here just now, would take him immediately...

<!-- B09-C09-P004 -->
“Wait,” Mitya suddenly interrupted, and with a feeling he seemed unable to restrain, he addressed everyone in the room. “Gentlemen, we’re all cruel, we’re all monsters, we all make people weep, mothers and babies at the breast, but of us all—let that be settled now—I’m the vilest reptile of all! So be it! Every day of my life I beat my breast and promised to reform, and every day I did the same vile things. Now I understand that a man like me needs a blow, a blow from fate, to catch him as if in a noose and bind him by a force outside himself. Never, never would I have risen on my own! But the thunder has struck.[^b09-c09-n001] I accept the torment of the accusation and my public disgrace, I want to suffer, and through suffering I’ll be purified! Perhaps I really will be purified, gentlemen, eh? But hear me for the last time: I’m not guilty of my father’s blood! I accept punishment not because I killed him, but because I wanted to kill him and perhaps really would have killed him... All the same, I intend to fight you, and I’m telling you so. I’ll fight you to the very end, and then God will decide! Goodbye, gentlemen, don’t be angry that I shouted at you during the questioning; oh, I was still so foolish then... In a minute I’ll be a prisoner, and now, for the last time, Dmitri Karamazov, still a free man, offers you his hand. Saying goodbye to you, I’ll be saying goodbye to humankind!..”

<!-- B09-C09-P005 -->
His voice trembled, and he actually held out his hand, but Nikolai Parfenovich, who was nearest to him, suddenly concealed his hands behind his back with an almost convulsive movement. Mitya noticed instantly and shuddered. He immediately lowered his outstretched hand.

<!-- B09-C09-P006 -->
“The investigation isn’t over yet,” Nikolai Parfenovich began to stammer, somewhat embarrassed. “We’ll continue in town, and I, of course, for my part am prepared to wish you every success... toward your acquittal... Personally, Dmitri Fyodorovich, I’m always inclined to regard you as a man, so to speak, more unfortunate than guilty... All of us here, if I may venture to speak for everyone, are prepared to consider you a young man noble at heart, but alas, carried away by certain passions to a somewhat excessive degree...”

<!-- B09-C09-P007 -->
By the end of the speech Nikolai Parfenovich’s small figure had assumed the fullest official dignity. It suddenly flashed through Mitya’s mind that this “boy” was now going to take his arm, lead him into another corner, and resume their recent conversation about “girls.” But all sorts of utterly extraneous and irrelevant thoughts sometimes flash through the mind even of a criminal being led to execution.

<!-- B09-C09-P008 -->
“Gentlemen, you’re kind, you’re humane—may I see her, say goodbye for the last time?” Mitya asked.

<!-- B09-C09-P009 -->
“Certainly, but in view of... in a word, it must now be in the presence of...”

<!-- B09-C09-P010 -->
“Very well, be present!”

<!-- B09-C09-P011 -->
Grushenka was brought in, but the farewell was brief and few words were spoken, which disappointed Nikolai Parfenovich. Grushenka bowed deeply to Mitya.

<!-- B09-C09-P012 -->
“I told you I was yours, and I’ll be yours. I’ll go with you forever, wherever they sentence you to go. Goodbye, you innocent man who’s brought himself to ruin!”

<!-- B09-C09-P013 -->
Her lips trembled, and tears flowed from her eyes.

<!-- B09-C09-P014 -->
“Forgive me, Grusha, for my love, for ruining you too with my love!”

<!-- B09-C09-P015 -->
Mitya wanted to say something more, but suddenly broke off and went out. Immediately he was surrounded by people who never took their eyes off him. Down by the porch, where he had thundered up the night before in Andrei’s troika, two carts already stood ready. Mavriky Mavrikievich, a stocky, thickset man with a puffy face, was irritated by something, some sudden hitch in the arrangements, and was angrily shouting. He told Mitya to climb into the cart in an excessively harsh tone. “The man had quite a different face before, when I used to buy him drinks in the tavern,” Mitya thought as he climbed in. Trifon Borisovich came down the porch steps as well. People had gathered at the gate, peasant men and women, drivers, all staring at Mitya.

<!-- B09-C09-P016 -->
“Farewell, people of God!” Mitya suddenly called to them from the cart.

<!-- B09-C09-P017 -->
“And forgive us too,” two or three voices answered.

<!-- B09-C09-P018 -->
“Goodbye to you too, Trifon Borisych!”

<!-- B09-C09-P019 -->
But Trifon Borisych did not even turn around; perhaps he was very busy. He too was shouting something and bustling about. It turned out that everything was not yet in order with the second cart, in which two village constables were to accompany Mavriky Mavrikievich. The peasant who had been assigned to drive the second troika was pulling on his shabby coat and arguing vehemently that it was not his turn to go but Akim’s. But Akim was not there; someone ran to fetch him; the peasant insisted and begged them to wait.

<!-- B09-C09-P020 -->
“Our people have no shame at all, Mavriky Mavrikievich!” Trifon Borisych exclaimed. “Akim gave you twenty-five kopecks the day before yesterday, you drank it up, and now you’re shouting. I’m simply amazed at your kindness to our scoundrels, Mavriky Mavrikievich, that’s all I can say!”

<!-- B09-C09-P021 -->
“Why do we need a second troika?” Mitya tried to intervene. “Let’s go in one, Mavriky Mavrikich. Don’t worry, I won’t rebel, I won’t run away from you, what do we need an escort for?”

<!-- B09-C09-P022 -->
“You will kindly learn how to address me, sir, if you haven’t been taught already. I’m not your intimate, don’t address me so familiarly, and save your advice for another time...” Mavriky Mavrikievich suddenly snapped savagely at Mitya, as though glad of a chance to vent his anger.

<!-- B09-C09-P023 -->
Mitya fell silent. He flushed all over. A moment later he suddenly felt very cold. The rain had stopped, but the murky sky remained entirely overcast, and a sharp wind blew straight into his face. “Am I getting the chills?” Mitya thought, shivering. At last Mavriky Mavrikievich climbed into the cart too, settled himself heavily, taking up plenty of room, and, as though without noticing, crowded Mitya hard to one side. It was true that he was in a bad mood and strongly disliked the task assigned to him.

<!-- B09-C09-P024 -->
“Goodbye, Trifon Borisych!” Mitya called again, and felt himself that this time he had called out not from good nature but from spite, against his own will. But Trifon Borisych stood proudly, both hands behind his back, staring straight at Mitya, stern and angry, and gave him no answer.

<!-- B09-C09-P025 -->
“Goodbye, Dmitri Fyodorovich, goodbye!” Kalganov’s voice suddenly rang out, and he darted up from somewhere. Running to the cart, he held out his hand to Mitya. He was bareheaded. Mitya still had time to catch his hand and shake it.

<!-- B09-C09-P026 -->
“Goodbye, my dear fellow, I won’t forget your generosity!” he exclaimed fervently. But the cart started, and their hands parted. The little bell rang out—they had taken Mitya away.

<!-- B09-C09-P027 -->
Kalganov ran into the entryway, sat in a corner, bowed his head, covered his face with his hands, and wept. He sat weeping like that for a long time—weeping as though he were still a little boy and not a young man of twenty. Oh, he had come almost wholly to believe in Mitya’s guilt! “What sort of people are these, what can people be after this!” he cried incoherently in bitter despondency, almost despair. At that moment he did not even want to live in the world. “Is it worth it, is it worth it!” the distressed young man exclaimed.

[^b09-c09-n001]: An echo of the Russian proverb “Until thunder strikes, the peasant won’t cross himself.”
