# Book X: Boys
## IV. Zhuchka

<!-- B10-C04-P001 -->
With an important expression on his face, Kolya leaned against the fence and waited for Alyosha to appear. Yes, he had wanted to meet him for a long time. He had heard a great deal about him from the boys, but whenever they spoke of him, he had always maintained an outward air of contemptuous indifference; he had even “criticized” Alyosha as he listened to what they told him. Privately, though, he very, very much wanted to know him: there was something appealing and attractive in all these stories about Alyosha. So this was an important moment. Above all, he mustn't disgrace himself; he must show his independence. “Otherwise he'll think I'm thirteen and take me for a little boy like the rest of them. And what does he want with those boys? I'll ask him when we get to know each other. It's awful, though, being so short. Tuzikov is younger than I am and half a head taller. Still, I have an intelligent face. I'm not good-looking, I know my face is hideous, but it's intelligent. And I mustn't give too much away, or if I rush straight into his arms he'll think... Ugh, how disgusting if he thinks that!..”

<!-- B10-C04-P002 -->
Such were Kolya's anxious thoughts as he did his utmost to look completely independent. Above all, his shortness tormented him: his height bothered him more than his “hideous” face. At home, in a corner, there was a pencil mark on the wall where he had measured his height the previous year, and every two months since then he had anxiously gone back to measure himself again: how much had he grown? Alas, he grew terribly little, and at times this simply drove him to despair. As for his face, it was not “hideous” at all; on the contrary, it was rather pretty, fair and pale, with freckles. His small but lively gray eyes looked out boldly and often lit up with feeling. His cheekbones were rather broad; his lips were small, not very full but very red; his nose was small and decidedly turned up. “A snub nose, an absolute snub nose!” Kolya would mutter to himself in the mirror, and he always turned away from it indignantly. “And is my face even intelligent?” he would sometimes wonder, beginning to doubt even that. Still, it mustn't be supposed that his looks and height occupied his whole soul. Quite the opposite: however bitter those moments before the mirror, he quickly forgot them, and for long stretches too, “devoting himself entirely to ideas and real life,” as he himself described his activities.

<!-- B10-C04-P003 -->
Alyosha soon appeared and hurried toward Kolya. When he was still several paces away, Kolya could see that his face looked positively joyful. “Can he really be so glad to see me?” he thought with pleasure. Here we should mention that Alyosha had changed considerably since we last left him: he had discarded his cassock and now wore a beautifully tailored frock coat and a soft round hat, with his hair cut short. All this suited him very well, and he looked quite handsome. His pleasant face always wore a cheerful expression, but it was a quiet, peaceful kind of cheerfulness. To Kolya's surprise, Alyosha had come out just as he had been sitting indoors, without an overcoat: clearly he had been in a hurry. He held his hand straight out to Kolya.

<!-- B10-C04-P004 -->
“At last you've come! We've all been waiting for you so much.”

<!-- B10-C04-P005 -->
“There were reasons, which you'll hear in a moment. In any case, I'm glad to meet you. I've long been waiting for the opportunity, and I've heard a great deal,” Kolya mumbled, a little out of breath.

<!-- B10-C04-P006 -->
“We would have met in any case; I've heard a great deal about you myself. But here—it's coming here that you've left so late.”

<!-- B10-C04-P007 -->
“Tell me, how are things here?”

<!-- B10-C04-P008 -->
“Ilyusha is very ill. He's certainly going to die.”

<!-- B10-C04-P009 -->
“What are you saying! You must admit, medicine is a vile business, Karamazov!” Kolya exclaimed heatedly.

<!-- B10-C04-P010 -->
“Ilyusha has spoken of you often, very often, even, you know, in his sleep, in his delirium. You must have been very, very dear to him before... before that incident... with the knife. There's another reason too... Tell me, is this your dog?”

<!-- B10-C04-P011 -->
“Mine. Perezvon.”

<!-- B10-C04-P012 -->
“Not Zhuchka?” Alyosha looked pleadingly into Kolya's eyes. “Has she disappeared for good, then?”

<!-- B10-C04-P013 -->
“I know you'd all like Zhuchka; I've heard the whole story, sir,” Kolya said with a mysterious smile. “Listen, Karamazov, I'll explain the whole business. That's chiefly what I came for, and why I asked you to come out: to explain the whole affair beforehand, before we go in,” he began eagerly. “You see, Karamazov, Ilyusha entered the preparatory class in the spring. Well, you know our preparatory class: little boys, children. They started picking on Ilyusha at once. I'm two classes above them, and naturally I watched from a distance, from the sidelines. I saw a small, frail boy, but he wouldn't give in; he even fought them, a proud boy with flashing eyes. I like that sort. But they went at him all the harder. Above all, his clothes were shabby then, his trousers rode up his legs and his boots gaped at the toes. They went at him for that too. Humiliated him. No, I don't like that: I stepped in at once and gave them an extra dose of pepper. You know, I beat them and they adore me—did you know that, Karamazov?” Kolya boasted expansively. “And I like children in general. Even now I've got two fledglings at home hanging round my neck; they actually held me up today. So they stopped beating Ilyusha, and I took him under my protection. I could see he was proud, I'm telling you, a proud boy, but he ended up slavishly devoted to me, obeying my slightest command, listening to me as though I were a god, trying to imitate me. The moment there was a break between classes he would come to me, and we would walk together. On Sundays too. At our school they laugh when an older boy takes up with a little one like that, but that's prejudice. It's my fancy, and that's that, isn't it? I teach him, develop his mind—why, tell me, shouldn't I develop his mind if I like him? After all, you yourself, Karamazov, have made friends with all these fledglings, so you must want to influence the younger generation, develop them, be useful? And I confess, of all the things I'd heard about you, that trait in your character interested me most. But to get on with it: I began noticing a sort of sensitivity, a sentimentality developing in the boy, and I, you know, have been a resolute enemy of all that slobbering affection from the day I was born. And contradictions, too: he's proud, yet slavishly devoted to me—slavishly devoted, and then suddenly his eyes flash and he won't even agree with me; he argues, gets all worked up. Sometimes I put forward various ideas: it wasn't that he disagreed with the ideas, I could simply see that he was rebelling against me personally because I answered his affection with coolness. And so, to put him to the test, the more affectionate he was, the cooler I became; I did it deliberately, that was my conviction. My intention was to train his character, straighten it out, make a man of him... and so on... You understand what I mean, of course. Suddenly I noticed that for one day, two, three, he was troubled, grieving, but no longer over affection: over something else, something stronger and greater. What's the tragedy? I thought. I pressed him, and here's what I found out: somehow he'd got to know your late father's servant Smerdyakov—your father was still alive then—and Smerdyakov taught the little fool a stupid trick, a savage trick, a vile trick: take a piece of bread, the soft part, stick a pin in it, throw it to some yard dog, one of those so hungry they'll swallow anything without chewing, and see what happens. Well, they made up a piece like that and threw it to Zhuchka herself, the shaggy dog everyone is talking about now, a yard dog from a place where they simply didn't feed her, and she barked at the wind all day. (Do you like that stupid barking, Karamazov? I can't stand it.) She pounced on it, swallowed it, yelped, spun round and ran off, running and yelping all the way, and disappeared—that's how Ilyusha himself described it to me. He confessed to me and cried and cried, hugged me, shook all over. ‘She runs and yelps, she runs and yelps’—that was all he kept repeating; the sight had struck him to the heart. Well, I could see his conscience was tormenting him. I took it seriously. Chiefly, I wanted to teach him a lesson for the earlier business too, so I confess I played a trick: I pretended to be indignant to a degree I perhaps wasn't feeling at all. ‘You've done a vile thing,’ I told him. ‘You're a scoundrel. Of course I won't tell anyone, but for the present I'm breaking off relations with you. I'll think the matter over and let you know through Smurov’—that's the very boy who came with me today, who's always been devoted to me—‘whether I'll continue our relations in future or drop you forever as a scoundrel.’ It was a terrible blow to him. I confess, even then I felt I might have treated him too harshly, but what could I do? That was how I thought at the time. A day later I sent Smurov to tell him I was ‘not speaking’ to him anymore—that's our expression for two friends breaking off relations. The secret was, I only meant to keep him in disgrace for a few days, then, when I'd seen his repentance, hold out my hand to him again. That was my firm intention. But what do you think? He heard Smurov out, and suddenly his eyes flashed. ‘Tell Krasotkin from me,’ he shouted, ‘that now I'll throw bread with pins in it to every dog, every single one!’ ‘Aha,’ I thought, ‘a spirit of independence has taken up residence; we'll have to smoke it out.’ And I began showing him utter contempt, turning away whenever we met or smiling ironically. Then suddenly came that incident with his father—you remember, the scrubber? You see, he had already been worked up into a terrible state of irritation. The boys saw I'd abandoned him and fell on him, teasing him: ‘Scrubber, Scrubber!’ That's when their battles began, and I regret it terribly, because I think they gave him a very bad beating once. One day, in the yard as we were coming out of class, he flew at them all, and I happened to be standing ten paces away, watching him. And I swear I don't remember laughing then; on the contrary, I felt very, very sorry for him, and in another moment I would have rushed to defend him. But suddenly he caught my eye. What he imagined, I don't know, but he pulled out his penknife, rushed at me and stuck it in my thigh, here, in my right leg. I didn't move. I confess I can be brave sometimes, Karamazov. I just looked at him with contempt, as though my eyes were saying: ‘Would you like another go, in return for all my friendship? I'm at your service.’ But he didn't stab me again; he couldn't bear it, he was frightened himself, dropped the knife, burst out crying and ran away. Naturally I didn't tell on him, and I ordered everyone to keep quiet so the authorities wouldn't hear. I didn't even tell my mother until it had all healed, and it was a trifling wound anyway, a scratch. Then I heard that on the very same day he had been throwing stones and had bitten your finger—but you see what a state he was in! Well, what can I say? I behaved stupidly: when he fell ill I didn't go to forgive him, that is, to make peace, and now I'm sorry. But by then I had particular plans of my own. Well, that's the whole story... only I think I behaved stupidly...”

<!-- B10-C04-P014 -->
“Oh, what a pity,” Alyosha exclaimed with feeling, “that I didn't know earlier about this friendship between you! I'd have come to you long ago myself and asked you to visit him with me. Would you believe it, in his fever, in his illness, he kept talking about you in his delirium. I had no idea how dear you were to him! And can it be, can it really be that you haven't found this Zhuchka? His father and all the boys have searched the whole town. Would you believe it, three times already, while I've been there, he's said to his father, lying ill and in tears: ‘It's because I killed Zhuchka that I'm ill, Papa. God is punishing me.’ You can't shake him out of that idea! If only we could find Zhuchka now and show him she wasn't dead, that she was alive, I think he'd come back to life himself for joy. We were all counting on you.”

<!-- B10-C04-P015 -->
“Tell me, why on earth were you counting on me to find Zhuchka—I mean, on me in particular?” Kolya asked with extraordinary curiosity. “Why were you relying on me, rather than someone else?”

<!-- B10-C04-P016 -->
“There was some rumor that you were looking for her and would bring her when you'd found her. Smurov said something of the sort. Above all, we keep trying to persuade him that Zhuchka is alive, that someone has seen her somewhere. The boys got him a live little hare from somewhere, but he only looked at it, smiled a little and asked them to let it loose in the fields. So we did. Just now his father came back with a Medelyan mastiff puppy for him, got that from somewhere too. He thought it would comfort him, but it seems only to have made things worse...”

<!-- B10-C04-P017 -->
“Tell me something else, Karamazov: what sort of person is this father? I know him, but how would you define him: a buffoon, a clown?”

<!-- B10-C04-P018 -->
“Oh no. There are people who feel deeply but have somehow been crushed. Their buffoonery is a sort of bitter mockery of those they dare not tell the truth to, after years of humiliating timidity before them. Believe me, Krasotkin, buffoonery like that can sometimes be terribly tragic. Everything he has now, everything on earth, is bound up in Ilyusha, and if Ilyusha dies he will either go mad with grief or kill himself. I'm almost convinced of it when I look at him now!”

<!-- B10-C04-P019 -->
“I understand you, Karamazov. I see you understand human nature,” Kolya added earnestly.

<!-- B10-C04-P020 -->
“When I saw you with the dog, I thought you'd brought that very Zhuchka.”

<!-- B10-C04-P021 -->
“Wait, Karamazov, perhaps we'll find her yet. But this one is Perezvon. I'll let him into the room now, and perhaps I'll cheer Ilyusha up more than the Medelyan puppy did. Wait, Karamazov, you're about to learn something. Oh, good God, why am I keeping you here!” Kolya suddenly cried impulsively. “You're out in this cold in just your frock coat, and I'm keeping you standing here. You see, you see what an egoist I am! Oh, we're all egoists, Karamazov!”

<!-- B10-C04-P022 -->
“Don't worry. It is cold, but I don't catch cold easily. Still, let's go in. By the way, what's your name? I know it's Kolya, but what else?”

<!-- B10-C04-P023 -->
“Nikolai. Nikolai Ivanov Krasotkin—or ‘son Krasotkin,’ as they put it in official language.” Kolya laughed for some reason, then suddenly added: “Of course, I hate my name, Nikolai.”

<!-- B10-C04-P024 -->
“Why?”

<!-- B10-C04-P025 -->
“It's trivial, official...”

<!-- B10-C04-P026 -->
“Are you going on thirteen?” Alyosha asked.

<!-- B10-C04-P027 -->
“Fourteen, that is. I'll be fourteen in two weeks, very soon. I'll confess a weakness to you at the outset, Karamazov—just to you, as we're getting acquainted, so you'll see my whole nature at once: I hate being asked my age, I more than hate it... And finally... for instance, people are slandering me by saying I played robbers with the preparatory boys last week. It's a fact that I played, but to say I played for my own sake, for my own pleasure, is downright slander. I have reason to think you've heard it, but I wasn't playing for myself, I was playing for the children, because they couldn't think of anything without me. That's how they always spread nonsense here. It's a town of gossip, I assure you.”

<!-- B10-C04-P028 -->
“And what would be wrong with playing for your own pleasure?”

<!-- B10-C04-P029 -->
“Well, for myself... You wouldn't play horses, would you?”

<!-- B10-C04-P030 -->
“Look at it this way,” Alyosha said, smiling. “Grown-ups go to the theater, for example, and in the theater they act out the adventures of all sorts of heroes, sometimes with robbers and wars too. Isn't it the same thing, in its own way, of course? When young people play at wars or robbers in their recreation time, that's art beginning to emerge too, the need for art beginning to emerge in a young soul. And sometimes the stories in these games are even better put together than the plays at the theater. The only difference is that at the theater you go to watch actors, whereas here the young people are the actors themselves. But that's only natural.”

<!-- B10-C04-P031 -->
“You think so? That's your conviction?” Kolya looked at him intently. “You know, that's quite an interesting thought. When I get home I'll put my mind to it. I confess I was expecting to learn a thing or two from you. I've come to learn from you, Karamazov,” Kolya concluded in an earnest, expansive voice.

<!-- B10-C04-P032 -->
“And I from you,” Alyosha said, smiling and shaking his hand. Kolya was extremely pleased with Alyosha. What struck him was how completely they were on an equal footing, and how Alyosha spoke to him as though he were “quite grown-up.”

<!-- B10-C04-P033 -->
“I'm going to show you a little stunt now, Karamazov, another theatrical performance,” he said with a nervous laugh. “That's what I came for.”

<!-- B10-C04-P034 -->
“Let's go into the landlady's on the left first. That's where all your friends leave their overcoats, because the room is cramped and hot.”

<!-- B10-C04-P035 -->
“Oh, I'm only staying a moment. I'll go in and sit with my coat on. Perezvon will stay here in the entryway and die. ‘Ici, Perezvon, couche, and die!’ There, you see, he's dead. I'll go in first, look things over, and then when the time comes I'll whistle: ‘Ici, Perezvon!’ You'll see, he'll fly in like a mad thing. Only Smurov mustn't forget to open the door at that moment. I'll arrange it, and you'll see the stunt...”
