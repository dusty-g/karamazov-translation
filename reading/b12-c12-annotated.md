# Book XII: A Judicial Error

## XII. And There Was No Murder Either

<!-- B12-C12-P001 -->
“One moment, gentlemen of the jury: a human life is at stake here, and we must be more careful. We heard the prosecution itself testify that until the very last day, until today, the day of the trial, it hesitated to accuse the defendant of fully and completely premeditated murder, hesitated until that fatal ‘drunken’ letter was produced in court today. ‘It happened just as written!’ But once again I repeat: he ran to her, after her, solely to find out where she was. That is an indisputable fact. Had she been at home, he would not have run anywhere, but would have remained with her and not fulfilled the promise in his letter. He ran on impulse, all at once, and perhaps did not even remember his ‘drunken’ letter at the time. ‘He picked up the pestle,’ we are told—and remember how an entire psychology was deduced for us from that one pestle: why he must have taken the pestle for a weapon, seized it as a weapon, and so on and so forth. Here a very ordinary thought occurs to me: what if that pestle had not been in plain sight, on the shelf from which the defendant took it, but had been put away in a cupboard? It would not have caught his eye then, and he would have run off unarmed, empty-handed, and perhaps would have killed no one. How, then, can I regard the pestle as evidence of arming himself and of premeditation? Yes, but he shouted in the taverns that he would kill his father, while two days before, on the evening when he wrote his drunken letter, he was quiet and quarreled in the tavern with only one merchant’s clerk, ‘because Karamazov could not help quarreling.’ My answer is that if he had planned such a murder, and according to a program, according to what he had written, he would certainly not even have quarreled with the clerk, and perhaps would not have gone into the tavern at all, because a soul that has conceived such a deed seeks silence and obscurity, seeks to disappear, to be neither seen nor heard: ‘Forget about me if you can’—and not merely from calculation, but by instinct. Gentlemen of the jury, psychology has two ends, and we too know how to understand psychology. As for all those cries in the taverns throughout that month, how often do children or drunken revelers, coming out of taverns and quarreling with one another, shout: ‘I’ll kill you!’ Yet they do not kill. And this fatal letter itself—is it not drunken anger too, the cry of a man coming out of a tavern: I’ll kill you, I’ll kill you all! Why not, why could it not have been so? Why is this letter fatal, why, on the contrary, is it not ridiculous? Precisely because the body of the murdered father was found, because a witness saw the defendant in the garden, armed and running away, and was himself struck down by him; therefore everything happened as written, and therefore the letter is not ridiculous but fatal. Thank God, we have reached the point: ‘Since he was in the garden, he must have killed him.’ Those few words—since he was there, it must follow—exhaust everything, the entire accusation: ‘He was there, therefore he did it.’ And what if it does not follow, even though he was there? Oh, I agree that the accumulation of facts, the coincidence of facts, is indeed quite eloquent. But consider each of these facts separately, without letting their accumulation influence you: why, for example, does the prosecution absolutely refuse to admit the truth of the defendant’s testimony that he ran away from his father’s window? Remember even the sarcasm in which the prosecution indulges here about the respectfulness and ‘pious’ feelings that suddenly possessed the murderer. But what if something of that kind really did occur—not respectful feelings, perhaps, but pious ones? ‘My mother must have prayed for me at that moment,’ the defendant testified at the investigation; and so he ran away as soon as he had satisfied himself that Svetlova was not in his father’s house. ‘But he could not have satisfied himself through the window,’ the prosecution objects. Why could he not? The window was opened in response to the signals given by the defendant, after all. Fyodor Pavlovich might have spoken some single word, some cry might have escaped him, and the defendant could suddenly have become certain that Svetlova was not there. Why must we assume things happened as we imagine, as we have decided to imagine? In reality a thousand things may flash past that escape the observation of the subtlest novelist. ‘Yes, but Grigory saw the door open; therefore the defendant was certainly in the house, and therefore killed him.’ About that door, gentlemen of the jury… You see, only one person testifies to this open door, and he himself was in such a condition at the time that… But very well, let the door have been open, let the defendant have denied it, lied out of an instinct of self-defense so understandable in his position; let him, let him have entered the house, have been inside—well, what then? Why must his having been there necessarily mean that he killed him? He might have burst in, run through the rooms, pushed his father away, even struck his father, but, having satisfied himself that Svetlova was not there, run away, delighted that she was not there and that he had escaped without killing his father. Perhaps it was precisely for that reason that a minute later he jumped down from the fence to Grigory, whom he had struck down in the heat of the moment: because he was capable of a pure feeling, of compassion and pity, because he had escaped the temptation to kill his father, because he felt his heart was pure and rejoiced that he had not killed his father. The prosecutor describes for us with terrifying eloquence the defendant’s terrible state in the village of Mokroye, when love opened itself to him anew, calling him to a new life, and he could no longer love, because behind him lay his father’s bloodstained corpse, and beyond the corpse, punishment. Yet the prosecutor allowed for love all the same, explaining it by his psychology: ‘A drunken state, a criminal being taken to execution, a long wait still ahead,’ and so on and so forth. But have you not created a different person, Mr. Prosecutor, I ask again? Is the defendant so coarse, so soulless, that at that moment he could still think of love and of wriggling out of things before the court, if his father’s blood really were upon him? No, no, and no! The moment he discovered that she loved him, called him to go with her, promised him new happiness—oh, I swear, he must then have felt a double, a triple need to kill himself, and would certainly have killed himself if his father’s corpse had lain behind him! Oh no, he would not have forgotten where his pistols were! I know the defendant: the savage, wooden heartlessness attributed to him by the prosecution is incompatible with his character. He would have killed himself, that is certain; he did not kill himself precisely because ‘his mother had prayed for him,’ and his heart was innocent of his father’s blood. That night in Mokroye he suffered and grieved only over old Grigory, whom he had struck down, and silently prayed to God that the old man would rise and regain consciousness, that his blow might not prove fatal and the punishment for it might pass him by. Why not accept this interpretation of events? What firm proof have we that the defendant is lying to us? But there is his father’s corpse, they will immediately point out again: he ran away, he did not kill him—then who did kill the old man?

“I repeat, there is the prosecution’s entire logic: who killed him, if not he? There is no one, they say, to put in his place. Gentlemen of the jury, is that so? Is there truly, really, absolutely no one to put there? We heard the prosecution count on its fingers everyone who was in or passed through that house that night. There were five people. Three of them, I agree, are entirely beyond suspicion: the murdered man himself, old Grigory, and his wife. That leaves the defendant and Smerdyakov, and now the prosecutor exclaims with pathos that the defendant points to Smerdyakov because he has no one else to point to, that if there were some sixth person here, even the shadow of a sixth, the defendant himself would immediately stop accusing Smerdyakov out of shame and point to this sixth person instead. But, gentlemen of the jury, why could I not reach precisely the opposite conclusion? Two men stand before us: the defendant and Smerdyakov—why may I not say that you accuse my client solely because you have no one else to accuse? And you have no one else only because you excluded Smerdyakov from all suspicion beforehand, out of pure preconception. Yes, it is true that only the defendant himself, his two brothers, and Svetlova point to Smerdyakov, no one else. But there is another witness too: a certain, albeit vague, stirring in society of some question, some suspicion; an indistinct rumor can be heard, one senses that some expectation exists. Finally, a certain juxtaposition of facts testifies too, highly characteristic though admittedly inconclusive: first, this epileptic seizure on the very day of the catastrophe, a seizure which for some reason the prosecutor was compelled to defend and uphold so diligently. Then Smerdyakov’s sudden suicide on the eve of the trial. Then the no less sudden testimony today in court of the defendant’s elder brother, who until now believed in his brother’s guilt and suddenly brings money and once again names Smerdyakov as the murderer! Oh, I am fully convinced, with the court and the prosecution, that Ivan Karamazov is ill and feverish, that his testimony really could have been a desperate attempt, conceived moreover in delirium, to save his brother by putting the blame on a dead man. Yet Smerdyakov’s name was uttered all the same; once again one seems to hear something mysterious. Something seems not to have been fully said here, gentlemen of the jury, something unfinished. And perhaps it will yet be said. But let us leave that for the moment; that still lies ahead. The court decided earlier to continue the hearing, but now, while we wait, I could nevertheless make a few observations, for instance concerning the portrait of the late Smerdyakov, so subtly and so brilliantly drawn by the prosecutor. Yet while admiring the talent, I cannot entirely agree with the substance of the portrait. I visited Smerdyakov, I saw him and spoke with him; he made an entirely different impression on me. His health was weak, that is true, but in character, in heart—oh no, he was not at all as weak a man as the prosecution concluded. Above all, I did not find in him that timidity which the prosecutor described to us so characteristically. As for simplicity, there was none whatever; on the contrary, I found terrible mistrust concealed beneath naïveté, and a mind capable of contemplating a great deal. Oh, the prosecution was all too simple itself in judging him feeble-minded. He made a quite definite impression on me: I left convinced that this was a decidedly malicious creature, inordinately ambitious, vindictive, and burning with envy. I gathered some information: he hated his origins, was ashamed of them, and remembered with gnashing teeth that he was ‘born of Stinking Lizaveta.’ He was disrespectful to the servant Grigory and his wife, the benefactors of his childhood. He cursed Russia and mocked her. He dreamed of leaving for France to turn himself into a Frenchman. Previously he had often spoken at length about lacking the means for this. It seems to me that he loved no one but himself, while his esteem for himself was extraordinarily high. He saw enlightenment in fine clothes, clean shirtfronts, and polished boots. Considering himself—and there are facts to support this—Fyodor Pavlovich’s illegitimate son, he could have hated his position beside his master’s legitimate children: everything for them, nothing for him; all the rights for them, the inheritance for them, while he was only a cook. He told me that he himself had put the money in the packet together with Fyodor Pavlovich. The purpose for which this sum was intended—a sum that could have made his career—was of course hateful to him. Besides, he had seen three thousand rubles in bright, rainbow-colored banknotes; I specifically asked him about this. Oh, never show an envious, proud man a large sum of money all at once, and this was the first time he had seen such a sum in one hand. The impression of that rainbow-colored bundle might have affected his imagination morbidly, at first without any consequences whatever. The highly talented prosecutor outlined for us with extraordinary subtlety all the pros and cons of the supposition that Smerdyakov could be accused of the murder, and asked in particular: why should he have pretended to have epilepsy? Yes, but perhaps he did not pretend at all; the seizure could have come on quite naturally, but it could equally have passed quite naturally, and the sick man could have regained consciousness. Not recovered, let us say, but still at some point come to himself and regained consciousness, as happens in epilepsy. The prosecution asks: where is the moment at which Smerdyakov committed the murder? But it is extremely easy to identify that moment. He could have awakened and risen from deep sleep—for he was only asleep: epileptic seizures are always followed by deep sleep—at the very moment when old Grigory seized the fleeing defendant’s leg on the fence and shouted for the whole neighborhood to hear: ‘Parricide!’ That extraordinary cry, in the silence and darkness, could have awakened Smerdyakov, whose sleep by then might not have been very deep: naturally, he could already have begun waking an hour earlier. Getting out of bed, he goes almost unconsciously, without any intention, toward the cry to see what is happening. His head is in a sickly fog, his understanding still drowses, but now he is in the garden, approaches the lighted windows, and hears the terrible news from his master, who is naturally glad to see him. His understanding suddenly flares up. From his frightened master he learns all the details. And now, gradually, a thought takes shape in his disordered, sick brain—terrible, but tempting and irresistibly logical: kill him, take the three thousand rubles, and then blame it all on the young master. Whom will they suspect now but the young master, whom can they accuse but the young master, with all the evidence, when he was here? A terrible thirst for money, for booty, could have taken his breath away, together with the thought that he would go unpunished. Oh, such sudden, irresistible impulses so often arise when the opportunity presents itself, and above all arise suddenly in murderers who a minute earlier had no idea that they would want to kill! And so Smerdyakov could have gone in to his master and carried out his plan. With what, with what weapon? With the first stone he picked up in the garden. But why, for what purpose? Why, the three thousand—that is a career. Oh, I am not contradicting myself: the money could have been there, could have existed. And perhaps Smerdyakov alone even knew where to find it, exactly where his master kept it. ‘But what about the money’s wrapper, the torn packet on the floor?’ Earlier, when the prosecutor, speaking of this packet, presented his exceedingly subtle argument that only an inexperienced thief could have left it on the floor, precisely someone like Karamazov, and certainly not Smerdyakov, who would never have left such evidence against himself—earlier, gentlemen of the jury, as I listened, I suddenly felt I was hearing something exceedingly familiar. And imagine, this very argument, this conjecture about how Karamazov might have handled the packet, I had already heard exactly two days earlier from Smerdyakov himself. More than that, it had even struck me at the time: I specifically felt that he was putting on a false naïveté, running ahead, pressing this idea on me so that I would arrive at that very conclusion myself, as though prompting me. Might he not have prompted the investigators with this argument too? Might he not have pressed it upon the highly talented prosecutor as well? They will say: what about the old woman, Grigory’s wife? She heard the sick man groaning beside her all night long. Yes, she did, but that argument is exceedingly shaky. I knew a lady who complained bitterly that a little dog in the yard had woken her all night and would not let her sleep. Yet, as it emerged, the poor little dog had yapped only two or three times the entire night. And this is natural: a person is asleep and suddenly hears a groan, wakes in annoyance at having been disturbed, but immediately falls asleep again. Two hours later another groan, another awakening, and another return to sleep; finally one more groan, again two hours later—three times in all during the night. In the morning the sleeper rises and complains that someone groaned all night and kept waking him constantly. But that is inevitably how it must seem to him: he slept through the intervals, two hours each, and does not remember them; he remembered only the moments when he woke, so it seems to him that he was being awakened all night. But why, why, the prosecution exclaims, did Smerdyakov not confess in his suicide note? ‘He had enough conscience for one thing, but not the other.’ One moment: conscience already implies repentance, but the suicide might have felt no repentance, only despair. Despair and repentance are two entirely different things. Despair can be malicious and implacable, and a suicide, laying hands on himself, might at that moment hate doubly those whom he had envied all his life. Gentlemen of the jury, beware of a judicial error! What, what is improbable in everything I have just presented and described to you? Find the error in my account, find the impossibility, the absurdity. But if there is even a shadow of possibility, a shadow of plausibility in my suppositions, withhold your verdict. And is there only a shadow here? I swear by everything sacred that I fully believe in my interpretation of the murder, the one I have just presented to you. Above all, above all, I am troubled and driven to distraction by the same thought: that out of the entire mass of facts heaped upon the defendant by the prosecution, there is not one that is even remotely precise and irrefutable, and that the unfortunate man is being destroyed solely by the accumulation of these facts. Yes, that accumulation is terrible: this blood, this blood flowing from his fingers, the linen covered in blood, the dark night ringing with the cry ‘Parricide!’ and the man shouting it falling with his skull broken, and then this mass of sayings, testimony, gestures, cries—oh, it has such an effect, it can so sway a person’s conviction, but can it sway yours, gentlemen of the jury, your conviction? Remember, you have been given immense power, the power to bind and to loose. But the greater the power, the more terrible its exercise! I do not retreat one iota from what I have just said, but very well, let it be so: let me too agree for a moment with the prosecution that my unfortunate client stained his hands with his father’s blood. This is only a supposition, I repeat; I do not doubt his innocence for a moment, but very well, I shall suppose that my defendant is guilty of parricide. Listen, however, to what I have to say even if I were to admit such a supposition. There is still something on my heart that I must tell you, for I sense a great struggle in your hearts and minds as well… Forgive these words, gentlemen of the jury, about your hearts and minds. But I want to be truthful and sincere to the end. Let us all be sincere!…”

At this point fairly loud applause interrupted the defense counsel. Indeed, his last words had sounded so sincere that everyone felt that perhaps he really did have something to say, and that what he was about to say was the most important thing of all. But on hearing the applause, the presiding judge loudly threatened to “clear” the courtroom if “such an incident” occurred again. Everyone fell silent, and Fetyukovich began in a new, penetrating voice, quite unlike the one in which he had spoken until then.
