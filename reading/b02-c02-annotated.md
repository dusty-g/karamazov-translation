# Chapter II — The Old Buffoon

*Part I · Book II: An Inappropriate Gathering*

<!-- B02-C02-P001 -->

They entered the room almost at the same moment as the elder, who appeared from his little bedroom
as soon as they arrived. Two hieromonks from the hermitage were already in the cell, waiting for him
to come out: one was Father Librarian, the other Father Paisius, a sickly man, though not an old one,
and, people said, very learned. Also waiting, standing in a corner—and he remained standing
throughout—was a young fellow who looked about twenty-two, wearing a civilian frock coat: a
seminarian and future theologian whom the monastery and its brethren, for some reason, had taken
under their protection. He was fairly tall, with a fresh face, broad cheekbones, and narrow brown
eyes, intelligent and watchful. His face expressed perfect respect, but within proper bounds, with
no visible attempt to ingratiate himself. He did not even bow to greet the arriving visitors, being
someone who was not their equal but, on the contrary, subordinate and dependent.

<!-- B02-C02-P002 -->

The elder Zosima came out accompanied by a novice and Alyosha. The hieromonks rose and greeted him
with the deepest of bows, touching the floor with their fingers; then, after receiving his blessing,
they kissed his hand. Having blessed them, the elder returned an equally deep bow to each, touching
the floor with his fingers, and asked each of them to bless him in turn. The whole ceremony was
performed in great earnest, not at all like some everyday ritual, but almost with a kind of feeling.
To Miusov, however, it seemed that all this was deliberately meant to impress. He stood in front of
everyone who had come in with him. Whatever his ideas, he ought—he had even thought it over the night
before—to approach the elder and ask his blessing out of simple courtesy, since that was the custom
here; at least ask his blessing, even if he did not kiss his hand. But at the sight of all the
hieromonks' bows and kisses, he changed his mind in an instant: with a grave and dignified air, he
made a fairly deep bow in the secular manner and stepped over to a chair. Fyodor Pavlovich did
exactly the same, this time mimicking Miusov to perfection, like a monkey. Ivan Fyodorovich bowed
with great dignity and politeness, but he too kept his hands at his sides, while Kalganov was so
embarrassed that he did not bow at all. The elder lowered the hand he had begun to raise in
blessing, bowed to them once more, and asked them all to sit down. Blood flooded Alyosha's cheeks;
he felt ashamed. His forebodings were coming true.

<!-- B02-C02-P003 -->

The elder sat down on a small leather-covered mahogany sofa of very old-fashioned design and seated
his guests, apart from the two hieromonks, along the opposite wall, all four side by side on four
mahogany chairs upholstered in badly worn black leather. The hieromonks sat to either side, one by
the door and the other by the window. The seminarian, Alyosha, and the novice remained standing. The
cell was quite small and had a somehow listless look. Its furnishings were rough and poor, confined
to the bare necessities. Two pots of flowers stood on the windowsill, and in the corner were many
icons—one of the Mother of God, enormous in size and probably painted long before the
seventeenth-century church schism. A little lamp glowed before it. Beside it were two other icons in
gleaming metal coverings, and around these, little ornamental cherubs, porcelain eggs, an ivory
Catholic cross embraced by a *Mater Dolorosa*,[^b02-c02-n001] and several foreign engravings after
the great Italian painters of past centuries. Beside these elegant, expensive engravings, a few
sheets of the most ordinary Russian popular lithographs held pride of place: saints, martyrs, holy
bishops, and so forth, the kind sold for kopecks at every fair. There were also a few lithographic
portraits of Russian bishops, past and present, but these hung on the other walls. Miusov gave all
this “official-looking clutter” a cursory glance, then fixed his intent gaze on the elder. He valued
his own gaze; that was a weakness of his, a forgivable one, at any rate, considering that he was
already fifty—an age at which an intelligent, worldly, well-to-do man invariably comes to regard
himself with greater respect, sometimes even despite himself.

<!-- B02-C02-P004 -->

He disliked the elder from the first instant. Indeed, there was something about the elder's face
that many people besides Miusov would have disliked. He was a short, stooped little man with very
weak legs, only sixty-five, but made to look much older by illness, at least ten years older. His
whole face, which was very gaunt, was sprinkled with fine wrinkles, especially numerous around the
eyes. The eyes themselves were small and light-colored, quick and bright, almost like two shining
points. A few wisps of gray hair survived only at the temples; his beard was tiny and sparse,
tapering to a point, and his lips, which often curved into a smile, were as thin as two bits of
string. His nose was not so much long as sharp, like a little bird's.

<!-- B02-C02-P005 -->

“Every sign of a spiteful, petty, conceited little soul,” flashed through Miusov's mind. He was, in
general, very dissatisfied with himself.

<!-- B02-C02-P006 -->

The striking of a clock helped start the conversation. A cheap little wall clock with hanging
weights struck a brisk twelve, precisely.

<!-- B02-C02-P007 -->

“Right on the dot!” cried Fyodor Pavlovich. “And my son Dmitri Fyodorovich still isn't here. I
apologize for him, sacred elder!” (Alyosha shuddered all over at “sacred elder.”) “For my own part,
I'm always punctual, to the very minute, remembering that punctuality is the politeness of kings...”

<!-- B02-C02-P008 -->

“But you, at any rate, aren't a king,” Miusov muttered, already unable to restrain himself.

<!-- B02-C02-P009 -->

“Yes, that's true, I'm no king. And would you believe it, Pyotr Alexandrovich, I knew that myself, I
swear to God! And there I go, always saying things at the wrong moment! Your Reverence!” he
exclaimed with some sudden theatrical fervor. “You see a buffoon before you, a buffoon in truth!
That's how I introduce myself. An old habit, alas! And when I sometimes tell lies at the wrong
moment, I even do it on purpose, on purpose to make people laugh and make myself agreeable. One must
be agreeable, mustn't one? About seven years ago I arrive in a little town—had a bit of business
there, and I'd formed a little partnership with some merchant fellows or other. We go to see the
*ispravnik*,[^b02-c02-n002] because we need to ask him for something and invite him to dine with us.
Out comes the *ispravnik*: a tall, fat, fair-haired, gloomy man—the most dangerous sort in such
circumstances: their livers, their livers. I go straight up to him, with the free-and-easy manner of
a man of the world, you understand. ‘Mr. Ispravnik,’ I say, ‘be our Nápravník, so to speak!’ ‘What
do you mean, Nápravník?’ he says. I can see in the first half-second that it hasn't come off. There
he stands, dead serious, digging his heels in. ‘I meant to make a joke,’ I say, ‘for everyone's
amusement, since Mr. Nápravník is our well-known Russian conductor, and what we need, precisely for
the harmony of our enterprise, is something like a conductor too...’ A perfectly reasonable
explanation and comparison, wasn't it? ‘Excuse me,’ he says, ‘I'm the *ispravnik*, and I won't allow
puns to be made out of my rank.’ He turns and walks away. I'm after him, shouting, ‘Yes, yes, you're
the *ispravnik*, not Nápravník!’ ‘No,’ he says, ‘once it's been said that way, that means I am
Nápravník.’ And would you believe it, our business fell apart! And I'm always like this, always like
this. I'm sure to do myself harm with my very own affability! Once, many years ago now, I say to a
man of influence, no less: ‘Your wife is a ticklish lady, sir’—meaning in matters of honor, moral
qualities, so to speak—and all of a sudden he comes back with: ‘Have you tickled her?’ I couldn't
help myself; all at once I think, let me be agreeable: ‘Yes,’ I say, ‘I've tickled her, sir’—well,
then he tickled me... Only it happened a long time ago, so there's no shame in telling it now; I'm
forever doing myself harm like that!”

<!-- B02-C02-P010 -->

“You're doing it now,” Miusov muttered in disgust.

<!-- B02-C02-P011 -->

The elder silently studied them both.

<!-- B02-C02-P012 -->

“Really! Would you believe it, I knew that too, Pyotr Alexandrovich, and I even had a premonition,
you know, that I was doing it the moment I began to speak; and I even had a premonition, you know,
that you would be the first to point it out. In those seconds when I see my joke isn't working, Your
Reverence, both my cheeks begin to dry up and stick to my lower gums, almost like a cramp; I've had
that since I was young, when I lived as a hanger-on in noblemen's houses and earned my bread by
hanging on. I'm a buffoon through and through, from birth, Your Reverence, as good as a holy fool; I
won't deny that there may be an unclean spirit lodged in me too, though only a small-caliber one; a
more important one would have chosen other lodgings—though not yours, Pyotr Alexandrovich, you're
not much as lodgings either. But I do believe, I believe in God. Only lately have I had my doubts,
but now I sit here awaiting great words. I'm like the philosopher Diderot, Your Reverence. Do you
know, most holy father, how Diderot the philosopher went to see Metropolitan Platon in the days of
Empress Catherine? He comes in and says straight out: ‘There is no God.’ To which the great prelate
raises a finger and replies: ‘The fool hath said in his heart, There is no God!’ Down he falls at
his feet on the spot. ‘I believe!’ he cries. ‘I accept baptism!’ So they baptized him then and
there. Princess Dashkova stood as godmother, and Potemkin as godfather...”

<!-- B02-C02-P013 -->

“Fyodor Pavlovich, this is intolerable! You know perfectly well that you're lying and that this
stupid story isn't true. Why all this playacting?” said Miusov in a trembling voice, no longer
restraining himself at all.

<!-- B02-C02-P014 -->

“All my life I've had a premonition that it wasn't true!” Fyodor Pavlovich exclaimed
enthusiastically. “But I'll tell you the whole truth, gentlemen: great elder! Forgive me, I made up
that last part, about Diderot's baptism, just now, this very minute, as I was telling it; it had never
entered my head before. I added it for spice. That's why I playact, Pyotr Alexandrovich, to make
myself more charming. Though sometimes I don't know why myself. As for Diderot, I must have heard
that ‘fool hath said’ twenty times from our own local landowners when I was young and lived in their
houses; from your aunt too, Pyotr Alexandrovich, Mavra Fominishna, as it happens. To this day they're
all convinced that Diderot the atheist went to Metropolitan Platon to argue about God...”

<!-- B02-C02-P015 -->

Miusov stood up, not merely having lost patience, but almost beside himself. He was furious and knew
that his fury was making him ridiculous. Something utterly unthinkable was indeed taking place in
the cell. Visitors had been gathering in this very cell for perhaps forty or fifty years, as far
back as the previous elders, but always in the deepest reverence, never otherwise. Almost all who
were admitted understood, on entering the cell, that they were being shown a great favor. Many fell
to their knees and stayed on their knees throughout the visit. Many even of the “highest”
personages, and even of the most learned—more than that, even some freethinkers—who came out of
curiosity or for some other reason, whether entering the cell with everyone else or being granted a
private audience, regarded the deepest respect and consideration throughout their visit as their
first duty, every one of them; all the more so since no money was expected here, only love and mercy
on the one side, and on the other, repentance and a longing to resolve some difficult question of
the soul or some difficult moment in the life of one's own heart. So the sudden eruption of such
buffoonery as Fyodor Pavlovich displayed, showing no respect for the place he was in, left those who
witnessed it—or at least some of them—bewildered and astonished. The hieromonks, whose expressions
had not changed in the slightest, nevertheless watched with grave attention for what the elder would
say, though they too seemed to be preparing to rise, like Miusov. Alyosha stood with his head bowed,
on the verge of tears. Strangest of all to him was that his brother Ivan Fyodorovich, the only person
he had counted on, and the only one with enough influence over their father to stop him, now sat
absolutely motionless in his chair, his eyes lowered, apparently waiting with a kind of inquisitive
curiosity to see how it would all end, as though he were a complete stranger here. Alyosha could not
even look at Rakitin, the seminarian, whom he also knew very well and was almost close to: he knew
what Rakitin was thinking, though Alyosha alone in the whole monastery knew it.

<!-- B02-C02-P016 -->

“Forgive me...” Miusov began, addressing the elder, “if I too perhaps seem to you to be taking part
in this unworthy joke. My mistake was to believe that even a man like Fyodor Pavlovich, on visiting
someone so worthy of respect, would wish to understand his obligations... It did not occur to me
that I would have to apologize simply for coming in with him...”

<!-- B02-C02-P017 -->

Pyotr Alexandrovich broke off and, thoroughly embarrassed, was about to leave the room.

<!-- B02-C02-P018 -->

“Please, don't distress yourself.” The elder suddenly rose on his frail legs and, taking Pyotr
Alexandrovich by both hands, seated him in his chair again. “Be at ease, I beg you. I particularly
ask you to remain my guest.” With a bow, he turned and sat down again on his little sofa.

<!-- B02-C02-P019 -->

“Great elder, pronounce: does my liveliness offend you or not?” Fyodor Pavlovich suddenly cried,
clutching the arms of his chair with both hands, as though preparing to spring out of it according
to the answer.

<!-- B02-C02-P020 -->

“And I earnestly ask you too not to be anxious or ill at ease,” the elder told him firmly... “Don't
feel constrained; make yourself entirely at home. Above all, don't be so ashamed of yourself, for
that alone is the source of it all.”

<!-- B02-C02-P021 -->

“Entirely at home? You mean in my natural state? Oh, that's a great deal, too much, but—I accept,
with a heart full of gratitude! You know, blessed father, don't challenge me to show my natural
state, don't take the risk... I won't go so far as my natural state myself. I'm warning you for your
own protection. Well, sir, all the rest is still shrouded in the darkness of the unknown, however
much certain people might like to paint my portrait. That's addressed to you, Pyotr Alexandrovich,
but to you, most holy being, here is what I say: I pour out my rapture!” He half rose, lifted his
arms, and declared: “Blessed is the womb that bore thee, and the breasts that gave thee suck—the
breasts especially! Just now, with your remark, ‘Don't be so ashamed of yourself, for that alone is
the source of it all’—with that remark you seemed to pierce right through me and read what was
inside. That's exactly how I feel whenever I go among people: that I'm baser than anyone and
everyone takes me for a buffoon, so then, ‘Very well, I'll play the buffoon in earnest; I'm not
afraid of your opinions, because every last one of you is baser than I am!’ That's why I'm a
buffoon, a buffoon out of shame, great elder, out of shame. It's nothing but my touchy self-doubt
that makes me act up. If only I were sure, when I came in, that everyone would immediately take me
for the most charming and intelligent of men—Lord! What a good man I'd be then! Teacher!” He
suddenly fell to his knees. “What must I do to inherit eternal life?” Even now it was hard to decide
whether he was joking or truly so moved.

<!-- B02-C02-P022 -->

The elder raised his eyes to him and said with a smile:

<!-- B02-C02-P023 -->

“You've known for a long time what you must do; you have intelligence enough. Don't give yourself
over to drunkenness or to an unbridled tongue; don't give yourself over to lust, and especially not
to the worship of money. And close your taverns—if you can't close them all, then at least two or
three. Above all, most of all—don't lie.”

<!-- B02-C02-P024 -->

“You mean about Diderot, is that it?”

<!-- B02-C02-P025 -->

“No, I don't mean about Diderot. Above all, don't lie to yourself. A man who lies to himself and
listens to his own lies reaches a point where he can no longer distinguish any truth, within himself
or around him, and so loses respect both for himself and for others. Having no respect for anyone,
he ceases to love, and without love, to keep himself occupied and entertained, he gives himself over
to passions and coarse pleasures, until his vices reduce him to an utter beast—all from lying
continually to other people and to himself. A man who lies to himself can also be the first to take
offense. Sometimes taking offense is very pleasant, isn't it? A man knows that no one has offended
him, that he has invented the offense himself and lied to embellish it, exaggerated it himself to
make a picture, seized on a word and made a mountain out of a molehill—he knows all this himself,
and yet he is the first to take offense, taking offense to the point of enjoyment, of feeling great
pleasure, and so he comes to feel real enmity... But do get up and sit down, I beg you; these too are
all false gestures...”

<!-- B02-C02-P026 -->

“Blessed man! Let me kiss your hand.” Fyodor Pavlovich sprang up and quickly planted a smacking kiss
on the elder's thin hand. “Exactly, exactly, taking offense is pleasant. You've put it so well,
better than I've ever heard. Exactly, exactly, all my life I've taken offense for the pleasure of
it, taken offense for aesthetic reasons, for it is not merely pleasant, but sometimes beautiful to
be offended—that's what you forgot, great elder: beautiful! I shall write that down in a little
book! And I have lied, lied, absolutely all my life, every day and every hour. Verily, I am a lie and
the father of lies! Though perhaps not the father of lies; I keep getting the texts wrong. Well, the
son of lies, then, that will do. Only... my angel... a little Diderot is allowed sometimes! Diderot
won't do any harm, but some other little word might. Great elder, that reminds me, I nearly forgot,
though for the past three years I've meant to make inquiries here, to come here expressly and insist
on finding out, on asking: only tell Pyotr Alexandrovich not to interrupt. Here's my question: is it
true, great father, what is told somewhere in the *Chet'i-Minei*[^b02-c02-n003] about some holy
wonder-worker who was tortured for his faith, and when they finally cut off his head, he stood up,
picked up his head, and ‘lovingly did kiss it,’ and walked a long way carrying it in his hands, and
‘lovingly did kiss it.’ Is that true or not, reverend fathers?”

<!-- B02-C02-P027 -->

“No, it isn't true,” said the elder.

<!-- B02-C02-P028 -->

“There is nothing of the kind anywhere in the *Chet'i-Minei*. Which saint do you say this was
written about?” asked the hieromonk, Father Librarian.

<!-- B02-C02-P029 -->

“I don't know which one myself. I don't know—I know not. I was deceived, that is what I was told. I
heard it, and do you know who told the story? Why, Pyotr Alexandrovich Miusov here, the very man who
was so angry just now about Diderot—he's the one who told it.”

<!-- B02-C02-P030 -->

“I never told you anything of the kind. I never even speak to you at all.”

<!-- B02-C02-P031 -->

“True, you weren't telling it to me; but you told it in company, and I was there. That was three
years ago. I only mentioned it because with this amusing tale you shook my faith, Pyotr
Alexandrovich. You did not know, you were not aware of it, but I returned home with my faith shaken,
and ever since I've been shaking more and more. Yes, Pyotr Alexandrovich, you were the cause of a
great fall! This is no Diderot, sir!”

<!-- B02-C02-P032 -->

Fyodor Pavlovich worked himself up into a fervent display of emotion, although by now it was
perfectly clear to everyone that he was playacting again. Miusov was nevertheless deeply stung.

<!-- B02-C02-P033 -->

“What nonsense, it's all nonsense,” he muttered. “I may indeed have said something once... only not
to you. Someone told me. I heard it in Paris, from a Frenchman, that supposedly it's in our
*Chet'i-Minei* and is read out at the liturgy... He's a very learned man who made a special study of
Russian statistics... lived in Russia a long time... I haven't read the *Chet'i-Minei* myself... and
I don't intend to... People say all sorts of things over dinner... We were having dinner at the
time...”

<!-- B02-C02-P034 -->

“Yes, you were having dinner, and I was losing my faith!” Fyodor Pavlovich teased him.

<!-- B02-C02-P035 -->

“What do I care about your faith!” Miusov began to shout, but suddenly checked himself and said
contemptuously: “You literally soil everything you touch.”

<!-- B02-C02-P036 -->

The elder suddenly rose from his seat.

<!-- B02-C02-P037 -->

“Forgive me, gentlemen, for leaving you for just a few minutes,” he said, addressing all his
visitors, “but there are others waiting who arrived before you. But still—don't lie,” he added,
turning to Fyodor Pavlovich with a cheerful face.

<!-- B02-C02-P038 -->

He moved toward the door of the cell, and Alyosha and the novice hurried to help him down the steps.
Alyosha was breathless; he was glad to get away, but glad too that the elder was not offended and
was cheerful. The elder set off toward the gallery to bless those waiting for him. But Fyodor
Pavlovich detained him after all in the doorway of the cell.

<!-- B02-C02-P039 -->

“Most blessed of men!” he cried with feeling. “Allow me once more to kiss your hand! No, you're
someone one can talk to, someone one can live with! Do you think I always lie like this and play the
buffoon? Then let me tell you, I've been putting on this performance deliberately all along, to test
you. All along I've been feeling you out: can one live with you? Is there room for my humility
alongside your pride? I award you a certificate of merit: one can live with you! And now I'll be
quiet, I'll say nothing for the rest of the time. I'll sit in my chair and keep quiet. It's your turn
to speak now, Pyotr Alexandrovich; you're the most important person left here now—for ten minutes.”

[^b02-c02-n001]: Latin: “Sorrowful Mother,” an image of Mary grieving over Christ.

[^b02-c02-n002]: An *ispravnik* headed a district police administration in imperial Russia. Fyodor’s joke pairs the title with the name of the composer and conductor Eduard Nápravník.

[^b02-c02-n003]: The *Chet'i-Minei* is a traditional collection of saints' lives and teachings arranged for reading by feast day through the year.
