# Book III: The Lustful
## V. Confession of a Passionate Heart: “Heels Up”

<!-- B03-C05-P001 -->
“Now,” said Alyosha, “I know the first half of the story.”

<!-- B03-C05-P002 -->
“You understand the first half: it's a drama, and it happened there. The second half is a tragedy, and it will happen here.”

<!-- B03-C05-P003 -->
“So far I don't understand any of the second half,” said Alyosha.

<!-- B03-C05-P004 -->
“And I do? Do you think I understand it?”

<!-- B03-C05-P005 -->
“Wait, Dmitri, there's one crucial word here. Tell me: you're engaged, you're still engaged, aren't you?”

<!-- B03-C05-P006 -->
“I didn't get engaged straightaway, only three months after all that happened. The very next day I told myself the affair was over and done with; there would be nothing more. Going to her and offering her my hand seemed a vile thing to do. And for her part, through the six weeks she stayed in our town afterward, she never sent me a word. Except on one occasion, it's true: the day after her visit their maid slipped in and handed me an envelope without saying a thing. The address on it: to so-and-so. I open it—change from the five-thousand-ruble bond. They only needed four thousand five hundred, and selling the five-thousand-ruble bond had meant a loss of two hundred and something. She sent me two hundred and sixty rubles altogether, I think, I don't quite remember, and only the money—no note, not a word, no explanation. I searched the envelope for some sort of pencil mark—n-nothing! Well, I went on a spree with the rubles I had left, so much so that even the new major was finally forced to reprimand me. Meanwhile the lieutenant colonel handed over the government money—safely, to everyone's astonishment, because by then no one believed he still had the whole sum. Handed it over and fell ill, took to his bed, lay there for three weeks or so, then suddenly developed softening of the brain and died within five days. They buried him with military honors; his retirement hadn't come through yet. Having buried their father, Katerina Ivanovna and her sister set off for Moscow with their aunt about ten days later. And just before they left, on the very day of their departure—I didn't see them or go to see them off—I received a tiny blue envelope, a bit of lace-edged paper inside, with just one line in pencil: ‘I will write to you. Wait. K.’ That was all.

<!-- B03-C05-P007 -->
“Now let me explain briefly. In Moscow their fortunes changed with the speed of lightning and the surprises of an Arabian tale. This general's wife, her most important relative, suddenly lost her two closest heirs, her two closest nieces—both died of smallpox in the same week. The old woman, shattered, welcomed Katya as her own daughter, as a star of salvation, seized on her and immediately changed her will in her favor. But that was for the future; for the present, straight into her hands—eighty thousand. Here's your dowry, she said, do what you like with it. A hysterical woman; I observed her in Moscow later. And then, all of a sudden, I receive four thousand five hundred rubles in the mail; naturally, I'm bewildered, struck dumb with astonishment. Three days later the promised letter arrives. I still have it, I always have it with me, and I'll die with it—shall I show you? You must read it: she's offering to be my bride, offering herself. ‘I love you madly,’ she says. ‘You needn't love me—it makes no difference, only be my husband. Don't be afraid, I won't restrict you in anything; I'll be your furniture, the carpet you walk on… I want to love you forever, I want to save you from yourself…’ Alyosha, I'm not worthy even to repeat those lines in my vile words and my vile tone, that vile tone of mine I've never been able to cure myself of! That letter pierced me, and the wound is still there today. Do you think it's easy for me now, easy for me today? I wrote back at once—I couldn't possibly get to Moscow myself. I wrote through tears. One thing I shall be ashamed of forever: I mentioned that she was rich now, had a dowry, while I was only a penniless army brute—I mentioned the money! I ought to have borne it, but it slipped from my pen. At the same time, straightaway, I wrote to Ivan in Moscow and explained everything as best I could—the letter ran to six sheets—and sent him to her. What are you looking at, why are you staring at me? Yes, Ivan fell in love with her, he's in love with her now, I know that. I did something foolish by your standards, by worldly standards, but perhaps that one foolish thing will save us all now! Ah! Don't you see how highly she thinks of him, how she respects him? Once she's compared the two of us, how can she love someone like me, especially after everything that's happened here?”

<!-- B03-C05-P008 -->
“But I'm sure she loves someone like you, not someone like him.”

<!-- B03-C05-P009 -->
“It's her own virtue she loves, not me,” Dmitri Fyodorovich suddenly blurted out, involuntarily but almost spitefully. He laughed, but a moment later his eyes flashed, his whole face flushed, and he pounded the table with his fist.

<!-- B03-C05-P010 -->
“I swear, Alyosha,” he cried, with fierce, sincere anger at himself, “believe me or not, but as God is holy and Christ is the Lord, I swear that even though I just sneered at her noblest feelings, I know my soul is a million times more worthless than hers, and those finest feelings of hers are as sincere as a heavenly angel's! That's the tragedy: I know it for certain. What does it matter if someone declaims a little? Don't I declaim? And yet I'm sincere, sincere. As for Ivan, I understand with what a curse he must look on nature now, especially with that mind of his! Who, what has been preferred to him? A monster who couldn't restrain his drunken outrages even here, when he was already engaged and all eyes were on him—and with his fiancée here, his fiancée! Someone like me is preferred, and he's rejected. But why? Because a young lady wants to do violence to her own life and destiny out of gratitude! Absurd! I've never said anything of the kind to Ivan, and of course Ivan has never said half a word to me about it either, never given the slightest hint; but destiny will be fulfilled, the worthy man will take his place, and the unworthy man will disappear into a back alley forever—his filthy back alley, the alley he loves and belongs in—and there, in the filth and stench, he'll perish of his own free will and with delight. I've started spouting lies somehow, all my words are worn out, I seem to be putting them down at random, but it will be just as I've laid it out. I'll drown in the back alley, and she'll marry Ivan.”

<!-- B03-C05-P011 -->
“Brother, wait,” Alyosha interrupted again, deeply troubled. “There's still one thing you haven't explained to me: you're engaged, you're still engaged, aren't you? How can you mean to break it off if she, your fiancée, doesn't want to?”

<!-- B03-C05-P012 -->
“I'm engaged, formally engaged and blessed. It all happened in Moscow when I arrived, with ceremony, with icons, everything done in the finest style. The general's wife gave us her blessing and—would you believe it—even congratulated Katya: ‘You've chosen well,’ she said. ‘I can see right through him.’ And would you believe it, she took a dislike to Ivan and didn't congratulate him. I had a great many talks with Katya in Moscow too. I gave her a complete picture of myself, honorably, accurately, sincerely. She heard it all:

> There was sweet confusion,  
> There were tender words…

“Well, there were proud words too. She wrung a solemn promise from me then that I would reform. I gave her my promise. And now…”

<!-- B03-C05-P013 -->
“What now?”

<!-- B03-C05-P014 -->
“And now I've called you over and dragged you in here today, this very day—remember that!—so I can send you, again today, to Katerina Ivanovna, and…”

<!-- B03-C05-P015 -->
“What?”

<!-- B03-C05-P016 -->
“Tell her I'm never coming to see her again. Say: ‘He told me to pay you his respects.’”

<!-- B03-C05-P017 -->
“How can you possibly do that?”

<!-- B03-C05-P018 -->
“That's exactly why I'm sending you instead: because it's impossible. How could I tell her myself?”

<!-- B03-C05-P019 -->
“But where will you go?”

<!-- B03-C05-P020 -->
“Into the back alley.”

<!-- B03-C05-P021 -->
“Then you mean to Grushenka!” Alyosha exclaimed in distress, throwing up his hands. “Could Rakitin really have been telling the truth? I thought you'd only been going there casually, and it was over.”

<!-- B03-C05-P022 -->
“An engaged man, going there? How could that be possible, with a fiancée like that, and in front of everyone? I do have some honor, after all. The moment I started going to Grushenka I stopped being an engaged man or an honorable man—I understand that much. What are you staring at? You see, I first went there to beat her. I'd found out, and now I know for certain, that this staff captain, Father's agent, had handed a promissory note of mine over to this Grushenka so she could collect on it and make me settle down and stop. They meant to frighten me. So off I went to beat Grushenka. I'd caught glimpses of her before. She doesn't strike you at first. I knew about the old merchant, who's ill now into the bargain, laid up and helpless, but he'll still leave her a tidy sum. I also knew she liked making money, and was making it, lending at vicious rates—a swindler, a rogue, without pity. I went to beat her and stayed. The storm broke, the plague struck, I caught it and I'm infected to this day, and I know everything's over, there will never be anything else. The cycle of the ages is complete. That's my story. And just then, as if on purpose, I suddenly found three thousand in my pocket—mine, a beggar's. She and I went from here to Mokroye, twenty-five versts away; I got gypsy men and women out there, champagne, got all the peasant men drunk on champagne, all the women and girls, flung my thousands about. Three days later, bare as a falcon, but a falcon all the same. And do you think the falcon got anywhere? She wouldn't show me what I wanted, not even from afar. I'm telling you: a curve. That rogue Grushenka has a certain curve to her body, and it carries through into her foot, there's even an echo of it in the little toe of her left foot. I've seen it and kissed it, but that's all—I swear! She says, ‘I'll marry you if you like, poor as you are. Say you won't beat me and you'll let me do whatever I want, and then perhaps I will’—and she laughs. And she's laughing still!”

<!-- B03-C05-P023 -->
Dmitri Fyodorovich rose from his seat with something almost like fury; suddenly he seemed drunk. His eyes suddenly grew bloodshot.

<!-- B03-C05-P024 -->
“And do you really want to marry her?”

<!-- B03-C05-P025 -->
“If she'll have me, at once; if she won't, I'll stay anyway. I'll sweep her yard. You… you, Alyosha…” He suddenly stopped in front of him, caught him by the shoulders and began shaking him hard. “Do you know, you innocent boy, that all this is delirium, inconceivable delirium, because this is a tragedy! Then learn this, Alexei: I may be a base man, with base and ruinous passions, but a thief, a pickpocket, a sneak thief in people's entrance halls—Dmitri Karamazov can never be that. Well, then, learn this now: I am a sneak thief, I steal from pockets and entrance halls! Just before I went to beat Grushenka, on that very morning, Katerina Ivanovna sent for me and, in the utmost secrecy, so no one would know for the time being—I don't know why, apparently she needed it that way—asked me to go to the provincial capital and send three thousand from the post office there to Agafya Ivanovna in Moscow. To the capital, so no one here would know. And it was with those three thousand in my pocket that I ended up at Grushenka's, and that's the money we went to Mokroye on. Afterward I pretended I'd dashed off to the capital, but I didn't produce the postal receipt; I said I'd sent the money and would bring the receipt, and I still haven't brought it—forgot, ma'am. Now, what do you think: you'll go there today and say, ‘He told me to pay you his respects,’ and she'll say, ‘And the money?’ You could still tell her, ‘He's a vile, lustful creature, a contemptible creature who can't control his feelings. He didn't send your money then, he squandered it, because he couldn't restrain himself, like an animal’—but you could still add, ‘At least he's not a thief. Here are your three thousand, he's sending them back; send them to Agafya Ivanovna yourself. And he told me to pay you his respects.’ But now, suppose she suddenly says, ‘Where's the money?’”

<!-- B03-C05-P026 -->
“Mitya, you're unhappy, yes! But still not as much as you think—don't destroy yourself with despair, don't!”

<!-- B03-C05-P027 -->
“What, do you think I'll shoot myself if I can't get the three thousand to pay her back? That's just it: I won't shoot myself. I haven't the strength now; later, perhaps, but now I'll go to Grushenka… Let it all go to the devil!”

<!-- B03-C05-P028 -->
“And once you're there?”

<!-- B03-C05-P029 -->
“I'll be her husband, I'll be granted that honor, and if a lover comes, I'll go into the other room. I'll clean her friends' muddy galoshes, blow up the fire in the samovar, run their errands…”

<!-- B03-C05-P030 -->
“Katerina Ivanovna will understand everything,” Alyosha suddenly said solemnly. “She'll understand the full depth of all this misery and accept it. She has a superior mind, and no one could be unhappier than you are; she'll see that for herself.”

<!-- B03-C05-P031 -->
“She won't accept everything,” Mitya said with a grin. “There's something here, brother, that no woman could accept. But do you know what would be the best thing to do?”

<!-- B03-C05-P032 -->
“What?”

<!-- B03-C05-P033 -->
“Give her back the three thousand.”

<!-- B03-C05-P034 -->
“But where can we get it? Listen, I have two thousand, and Ivan will give another thousand; that makes three. Take it and pay her back.”

<!-- B03-C05-P035 -->
“And when will those three thousand of yours arrive? You're not even of age yet, besides. And you must, you absolutely must, take leave of her for me today, with the money or without it, because I can't drag it out any longer, that's the point things have reached. Tomorrow will be too late, too late. I'll send you to Father.”

<!-- B03-C05-P036 -->
“To Father?”

<!-- B03-C05-P037 -->
“Yes, to Father before you go to her. Ask him for the three thousand.”

<!-- B03-C05-P038 -->
“But he won't give it, Mitya.”

<!-- B03-C05-P039 -->
“As if he would! I know he won't. Do you know what despair means, Alexei?”

<!-- B03-C05-P040 -->
“Yes.”

<!-- B03-C05-P041 -->
“Listen: legally he owes me nothing. I've had it all out of him, all of it, I know that. But morally he owes me, doesn't he? He started with Mother's twenty-eight thousand and made a hundred thousand. Let him give me just three thousand out of the twenty-eight, just three, and he'll draw my soul out of hell, and it will be credited to him against his many sins! With these three thousand I'll call it quits—I give you my solemn word—and he'll never hear another thing about me. I'm giving him one last chance to be a father. Tell him God himself is sending him this chance.”

<!-- B03-C05-P042 -->
“Mitya, nothing will make him give it.”

<!-- B03-C05-P043 -->
“I know he won't, I know perfectly well. Especially now. More than that, here's something else I know: only just now, the other day, perhaps only yesterday, he realized for the first time, seriously—underline that: seriously—that Grushenka might really mean it and might take it into her head to marry me. He knows her nature, he knows that cat. So is he really going to give me money as well, to help such a thing happen, when he's out of his mind over her himself? But even that isn't all, I can give you more: I know that about five days ago he took out three thousand rubles, had them changed into hundred-ruble notes, and packed them into a big envelope with five seals and a little red ribbon tied crosswise around it. You see how much detail I know! And on the envelope it says, ‘For my angel Grushenka, if she cares to come.’ He scrawled it himself, quietly and secretly, and no one knows he has the money there except his valet Smerdyakov, whose honesty he trusts as he trusts himself. He's been waiting three or four days now for Grushenka, hoping she'll come for the envelope. He sent word to her, and she sent word back: ‘Perhaps I will come.’ But if she goes to the old man, how can I marry her then? Do you understand now why I'm sitting here in hiding, and just what I'm watching for?”

<!-- B03-C05-P044 -->
“Her?”

<!-- B03-C05-P045 -->
“Her. Foma rents a little room from these sluts, the landladies here. Foma's from our part of the country, a former soldier of ours. He does chores for them, keeps watch at night, and goes out shooting black grouse in the daytime; that's how he lives. I've holed up here with him. Neither he nor the women know the secret—that is, what I'm watching for.”

<!-- B03-C05-P046 -->
“Only Smerdyakov knows?”

<!-- B03-C05-P047 -->
“Only him. He'll let me know if she goes to the old man.”

<!-- B03-C05-P048 -->
“Was he the one who told you about the envelope?”

<!-- B03-C05-P049 -->
“He was. The deepest secret. Even Ivan knows nothing about the money or any of it. And the old man is trying to send Ivan off to Chermashnya for two or three days: a buyer has turned up who'll pay eight thousand for the grove, to cut it down, so the old man keeps begging Ivan, ‘Help me out, go yourself’—for two or three days, that is. He wants Grushenka to come while Ivan's away.”

<!-- B03-C05-P050 -->
“So he's expecting Grushenka today too?”

<!-- B03-C05-P051 -->
“No, she won't come today, there are signs. She definitely won't!” Mitya suddenly shouted. “That's what Smerdyakov thinks too. Father's drinking now, sitting at the table with our brother Ivan. Go, Alexei, ask him for those three thousand…”

<!-- B03-C05-P052 -->
“Mitya, dear, what's happened to you!” cried Alyosha, jumping up and peering at the frantic Dmitri Fyodorovich. For an instant he thought his brother had gone mad.

<!-- B03-C05-P053 -->
“What do you mean? I'm not mad,” said Dmitri Fyodorovich, looking at him intently, even with a kind of solemnity. “I'm sending you to Father, aren't I, and I know what I'm saying: I believe in a miracle.”

<!-- B03-C05-P054 -->
“A miracle?”

<!-- B03-C05-P055 -->
“A miracle of God's providence. God knows my heart, he sees all my despair. He sees the whole picture. Would he really let the horror happen? Alyosha, I believe in a miracle. Go!”

<!-- B03-C05-P056 -->
“I'll go. Tell me, will you wait here?”

<!-- B03-C05-P057 -->
“I will. I understand it'll take time, that you can't just walk in and blurt it out! He's drunk now. I'll wait three hours, four, five, six, seven, but remember: today, even if it's midnight, you're to go to Katerina Ivanovna, with the money or without it, and say, ‘He told me to pay you his respects.’ I specifically want you to say it this way: ‘He told me, he said, to pay you his respects.’”

<!-- B03-C05-P058 -->
“Mitya! What if Grushenka comes today… or if not today, tomorrow or the day after?”

<!-- B03-C05-P059 -->
“Grushenka? I'll be watching, I'll burst in and stop them…”

<!-- B03-C05-P060 -->
“But if…”

<!-- B03-C05-P061 -->
“If it comes to ‘if,’ I'll kill. I couldn't live through that.”

<!-- B03-C05-P062 -->
“Kill whom?”

<!-- B03-C05-P063 -->
“The old man. I won't kill her.”

<!-- B03-C05-P064 -->
“Brother, what are you saying!”

<!-- B03-C05-P065 -->
“I don't know, I don't know… Perhaps I won't kill him, or perhaps I will. I'm afraid that at that very moment the sight of his face will suddenly fill me with hatred. I hate his Adam's apple, his nose, his eyes, his shameless sneer. I feel a personal revulsion. That's what I'm afraid of. That's when I won't be able to stop myself…”

<!-- B03-C05-P066 -->
“I'll go, Mitya. I believe God will arrange things as he knows best, so the horror won't happen.”

<!-- B03-C05-P067 -->
“And I'll sit here waiting for a miracle. But if it doesn't happen, then…”

<!-- B03-C05-P068 -->
Alyosha set off for his father's, deep in thought.
