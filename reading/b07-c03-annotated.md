# Book VII: Alyosha

## III. An Onion

<!-- B07-C03-P001 -->
Grushenka lived in the busiest part of town, near Cathedral Square, on the property of a merchant’s widow, Morozova, from whom she rented a small wooden annex in the courtyard. Morozova’s own house was large, stone-built, two stories high, old and very uninviting to look at; its owner, an old woman, lived there in seclusion with her two nieces, both themselves unmarried women of quite advanced years. She had no need to let the annex in her courtyard, but everyone knew that she had taken Grushenka in as a tenant—some four years before—solely to oblige her relative, the merchant Samsonov, Grushenka’s acknowledged protector. People said that when the jealous old man installed his “favorite” at Morozova’s, his original intention was to use the old woman’s watchful eye to keep track of his new tenant’s behavior. But that watchful eye soon proved unnecessary, and in the end Morozova rarely even saw Grushenka and had long since stopped troubling her with any supervision. Admittedly, four years had passed since the old man brought an eighteen-year-old girl to this house from the provincial capital: timid, shy, slender, thin, pensive and sad; much water had flowed under the bridge since then. In our town, however, little was known of this girl’s history, and what was known was confused. No more had come to light even lately, when so many people had begun taking an interest in the “ravishing beauty” Agrafena Alexandrovna had become in those four years. There were only rumors that, while still a girl of seventeen, she had been seduced by someone, supposedly an officer, who had immediately abandoned her. The officer, they said, had gone away and later married somewhere, leaving Grushenka in disgrace and poverty. It was said, however, that although her old man had indeed rescued her from poverty, she came of an honest family and had some connection with the clergy, being the daughter of a deacon without a post, or something of that kind. And so, in four years, the sensitive, injured, pitiful little orphan had become a rosy, full-bodied Russian beauty, a woman of bold, determined character, proud and brazen, who understood money, acquisitive, tightfisted and cautious, and who by fair means or foul had already, so people said, managed to put together a little capital of her own. Of one thing everyone was certain: access to Grushenka was difficult, and apart from the old man, her protector, not a single person in all those four years could boast of having enjoyed her favors. This was a solid fact, since quite a few would-be suitors had come forward to seek those favors, especially in the past two years. But every attempt had failed, and some aspirants had even been forced into a comic and humiliating retreat, thanks to the spirited young woman’s firm, mocking rebuffs. It was also known that the young woman, especially in the past year, had taken up what is called “gesheft”—business dealing—and had shown such exceptional abilities in that line that many people ended by sneeringly calling her “a regular Jewess.” It was not that she lent money at interest, but it was known, for example, that in partnership with Fyodor Pavlovich Karamazov she had for a time actually bought up promissory notes for next to nothing, at ten kopecks to the ruble, and had then turned some of those notes into a ruble for every ten kopecks. Samsonov, an ailing widower who in the past year had lost the use of his swollen legs, a tyrant to his grown sons, a man worth hundreds of thousands, miserly and implacable, had nevertheless fallen strongly under the influence of his protégée. At first he had kept her on a very tight rein and short rations—“on Lenten oil,” as the wags used to say. But Grushenka had managed to free herself, while still inspiring in him an unlimited trust in her fidelity. This old man, a considerable businessman, now long dead, also had a remarkable character: above all he was stingy and hard as flint, and although Grushenka had so captivated him that he could not live without her—as was indeed the case in the last two years, for example—he never settled any large, substantial sum on her. Even if she had threatened to leave him altogether, he would still have remained unmoved. He did, however, settle a small sum on her, and when this became known, even that astonished everyone. “You’re a shrewd woman yourself,” he told her, giving her about eight thousand. “Make your own way. But understand that, apart from your yearly allowance, which will continue as before until I die, you’ll get nothing more from me, and I won’t leave you anything more in my will either.” And he kept his word: he died and left everything to his sons, whom, with their wives and children, he had kept beside him all his life as if they were servants; Grushenka was not so much as mentioned in his will. All this became known afterward. He did, however, give Grushenka considerable help with advice on managing “her own capital,” and pointed out “opportunities” to her. When Fyodor Pavlovich Karamazov, who had first become involved with Grushenka through some chance piece of “gesheft,” ended, quite unexpectedly to himself, by falling madly in love with her, seemingly even losing his mind, old Samsonov, already on his last legs, was greatly amused. It is worth observing that throughout their acquaintance Grushenka was entirely frank with her old man, with what even seemed an affectionate openness—and apparently he was the only person in the world she treated that way. Very recently, when Dmitri Fyodorovich suddenly appeared with his love as well, the old man stopped laughing. Indeed, one day he gave Grushenka this serious, stern advice: “If you’re going to choose between those two, the father or the son, choose the old man, but only on condition that the old scoundrel marries you, and signs over at least some capital beforehand. Don’t get mixed up with the captain: nothing good will come of it.” Those were the very words of the old sensualist to Grushenka; he already felt his death approaching, and did in fact die five months after giving this advice. I will also note in passing that although many people in our town knew of the absurd and ugly rivalry between the two Karamazovs, father and son, with Grushenka as its object, few understood the true nature of her relations with either the old man or the son. Even both her servants later testified in court, after the catastrophe of which we have yet to speak, that Agrafena Alexandrovna received Dmitri Fyodorovich solely out of fear, allegedly because he “threatened to kill her.” She had two servants: a very old cook who had been with her parents’ family, a sick woman, almost deaf, and the cook’s granddaughter, a lively young girl of about twenty, who was Grushenka’s maid. Grushenka lived very frugally, in far from luxurious surroundings. Her annex had only three rooms, furnished by the landlady with ancient mahogany furniture in the style of the twenties. By the time Rakitin and Alyosha came in it was quite dusk, but the rooms had not yet been lit. Grushenka was lying in her sitting room on her large, ungainly sofa, a hard sofa with a back finished to look like mahogany and leather upholstery long since worn through and full of holes. Beneath her head were two white down pillows from her bed. She lay motionless on her back, stretched out, with both hands behind her head. She was dressed up as if expecting someone, in a black silk dress and a light lace headpiece that suited her very well; a lace kerchief was draped over her shoulders and fastened with a heavy gold brooch. She was indeed expecting someone, and lay seemingly anxious and impatient, her face a little pale, her lips and eyes burning, tapping the toe of her right foot impatiently against the arm of the sofa. The moment Rakitin and Alyosha appeared there was a small commotion: from the entrance hall they heard Grushenka spring up from the sofa and suddenly cry out in alarm, “Who’s there?” But the maid had met the visitors and immediately called back to her mistress.

“It’s not him, ma’am. It’s other people. These are all right.”

“Oh, it’s you, Rakitka? You gave me such a fright. Who have you brought? Who’s that with you? Lord, look who he’s brought!” she exclaimed, making out Alyosha.

“Have I got it wrong, then?” asked Rakitin, almost instantly offended.

I told him I was going to Kuzma Kuzmich, my old man, for the whole evening, and would be counting money with him till late. I go to him every week for a whole evening to balance the accounts. We lock ourselves in: he clicks away on the abacus and I sit entering it in the books—he trusts only me. So Mitya believed I was there, but here I am, locked up at home, waiting for some news. How could Fenya have let you in! Fenya, Fenya! Run to the gate, open it and look all around. Is the captain anywhere out there? Perhaps he’s hiding and watching. I’m frightened to death!”

<!-- B07-C03-P009 -->
“There’s no one, Agrafena Alexandrovna. I’ve just looked everywhere, and I keep going to peep through the crack every minute. I’m shaking with fear myself.”

<!-- B07-C03-P010 -->
“Are the shutters fastened, Fenya? And the curtains ought to be drawn—there!” She drew the heavy curtains herself. “Otherwise he’ll make straight for the light. I’m afraid of Mitya today, Alyosha, your brother.” Grushenka spoke loudly; although anxious, she seemed almost elated as well.

<!-- B07-C03-P011 -->
“Why are you so afraid of Mitenka today?” Rakitin inquired. “You don’t usually seem scared of him. He dances to your tune.”

<!-- B07-C03-P012 -->
“I tell you, I’m waiting for news, a precious little piece of news, so I could do altogether without Mitenka just now. Besides, he didn’t believe I was going to Kuzma Kuzmich—I can feel it. He’s probably sitting in his usual place now, at the back of Fyodor Pavlovich’s garden, keeping watch for me. And if he’s settled in there, he won’t come here, so much the better! But I really did go to Kuzma Kuzmich; Mitya walked me there himself. I told him I’d stay till midnight and made sure he would come at midnight to walk me home. He went away, and I sat ten minutes or so with the old man, then came back here. Oh, I was frightened—I ran, so I wouldn’t meet him.”

<!-- B07-C03-P013 -->
“And where are you going, all dressed up? What a curious cap you’ve got on!”

<!-- B07-C03-P014 -->
“And what a curious fellow you are yourself, Rakitin! I tell you, I’m waiting for a certain bit of news. Once it comes I’ll jump up and fly away, and that’ll be the last you see of me here. That’s why I’m dressed up, so I’ll be ready.”

<!-- B07-C03-P015 -->
“Where will you fly off to?”

<!-- B07-C03-P016 -->
“Know too much and you’ll grow old too soon.”

<!-- B07-C03-P017 -->
“Well, look at you. All happiness… I’ve never seen you like this. Dressed up for a ball.” Rakitin looked her over.

<!-- B07-C03-P018 -->
“As if you know anything about balls.”

<!-- B07-C03-P019 -->
“And you do?”

<!-- B07-C03-P020 -->
“I’ve seen a ball. Two years ago, when Kuzma Kuzmich married off his son, I watched from the gallery. Why should I waste time talking to you, Rakitka, with such a prince standing here? What a guest! Sit on the sofa, here, there you are, my young moon. Truly, I still can’t quite take it in… Oh, Rakitka, if only you’d brought him yesterday or the day before!… Still, I’m glad anyway. <!-- B07-C03-P021 -->
She briskly sat down beside Alyosha on the sofa and looked at him with unmistakable delight. And she really was glad; she had not lied when she said so. Her eyes shone, her lips were smiling, but with a good-natured, cheerful smile. Alyosha had not even expected such a kind expression on her face. Before yesterday he had rarely encountered her and had formed a terrifying impression of her; yesterday he had been so badly shaken by her malicious, treacherous treatment of Katerina Ivanovna that he was astonished to discover in her now what seemed an entirely different, unexpected being. Crushed though he was by his own grief, his eyes involuntarily rested on her with interest. Her whole manner, too, seemed to have changed for the better since yesterday: almost none of that sugary way of speaking remained, none of those languid, affected movements. Everything was simple and artless; her movements were quick, direct, trusting, but she was intensely excited.

<!-- B07-C03-P022 -->
“Lord, what things are happening today, really!” she began chattering again. “And why I’m so glad to see you, Alyosha, I don’t know myself. You can ask me, but I don’t know.”

<!-- B07-C03-P023 -->
“So you don’t know what you’re glad about?” Rakitin smirked. “You must have had some reason for pestering me before: bring him, bring him. You had something in mind.”

<!-- B07-C03-P024 -->

There he is, Alyosha, sitting opposite us, taking offense because I didn’t ask him to sit before you. Oh, my Rakitka’s touchy, so touchy!” Grushenka laughed. “Don’t be angry, Rakitka. I’m kind today. But why are you sitting there so sadly, Alyoshechka? Are you afraid of me?” she asked, looking into his eyes with cheerful mockery.

<!-- B07-C03-P026 -->
“What promotion?”

<!-- B07-C03-P027 -->

<!-- B07-C03-P028 -->

Will you let me sit on your lap, Alyosha, like this?” Suddenly she sprang up and, laughing, jumped onto his lap like an affectionate kitten, gently slipping her right arm around his neck. “I’ll cheer you up, my little man of prayer! No, really, will you let me sit on your lap? You won’t be angry? Tell me to and I’ll jump down.”

<!-- B07-C03-P029 -->
Alyosha said nothing. He sat afraid to move; he heard her say, “Tell me to and I’ll jump down,” but did not answer, as if he had frozen. Yet what was happening in him was not what, for example, Rakitin, watching greedily from his seat, might have expected or imagined. The great grief in his soul swallowed up every feeling that could arise in his heart, and had he been able at that moment to understand his own condition fully, he would have realized that he was protected by the strongest armor against every seduction and temptation. Nevertheless, confused and uncomprehending as his state of mind was, and despite all the grief weighing on him, he could not help wondering at one new, strange feeling arising in his heart. This woman, this “terrible” woman, not only failed to frighten him now with his old fear, the fear that used to stir whenever a dream of woman flashed through his soul; on the contrary, this woman whom he had feared more than any other, sitting on his lap with her arm around him, was suddenly arousing an entirely different, unexpected and peculiar feeling in him: a quite extraordinary, immense and wholly sincere curiosity about her. And all this without any fear, without the slightest trace of his former terror—that was the main thing, and what involuntarily astonished him.

Until that moment he had thought it politic not to contradict Grushenka too much, despite all her digs, for she evidently held some power over him. But now he too was angry:

<!-- B07-C03-P075 -->
“You love people for something. What have either of you done for me?”

<!-- B07-C03-P076 -->
“Then love us for nothing, the way Alyosha loves.”

<!-- B07-C03-P077 -->
“How does he love you? What has he shown you that’s got you so carried away?”

<!-- B07-C03-P078 -->
Grushenka stood in the middle of the room, speaking heatedly, and hysterical notes could be heard in her voice:

<!-- B07-C03-P079 -->
“Be quiet, Rakitka, you understand nothing about us! And don’t you dare address me so familiarly again. I won’t allow it. Where did you get the nerve, anyway! Sit in the corner and be quiet, like my footman. And now, Alyosha, I’ll tell you alone the whole pure truth, so you can see what a creature I am! I’m speaking to you, not Rakitka. I wanted to ruin you, Alyosha, that’s the absolute truth. I’d made up my mind; I wanted it so much I bribed Rakitka to bring you. And what made me want it so? You knew nothing of it, Alyosha; you turned away from me, you’d walk past with your eyes lowered, but I’d looked at you a hundred times before and begun asking everyone about you. Your face stayed in my heart. ‘He despises me,’ I thought. ‘He won’t even want to look at me.’ And in the end such a feeling came over me that I wondered at myself: why am I afraid of a boy like that? I’ll swallow him whole and laugh. I grew quite vicious. Would you believe it: no one here dares say or even think of coming to Agrafena Alexandrovna for that dirty business. There’s only the old man here with me; I’m bound and sold to him, Satan married us, but of the others—not one. Yet when I looked at you I made up my mind: I’ll swallow him. I’ll swallow him and laugh. You see what a wicked dog you called your sister! And now this man who wronged me has come; I’m sitting here waiting for news. Do you know what that man meant to me? When Kuzma brought me here five years ago, I used to sit hiding from people so they wouldn’t see or hear me, thin and foolish, sitting and sobbing, not sleeping all night, thinking: ‘Where is he now, the man who wronged me? Laughing at me with another woman, I suppose. Oh, if only I could see him, meet him sometime, I’d pay him back, I’d pay him back!’ At night in the dark I’d sob into my pillow and think it all over, tear my heart apart on purpose and soothe it with spite: ‘I’ll pay him back, I’ll pay him back!’ I’d even cry it out in the dark. Then I’d suddenly remember that I couldn’t do anything to him, while he was laughing at me now, or perhaps had forgotten me altogether and didn’t even remember, and I’d fling myself out of bed onto the floor, burst into helpless tears and shake and shake till dawn. In the morning I’d get up meaner than a dog, ready to swallow the whole world. And then what do you think? I began saving money, grew merciless, grew fat—you think I’d grown wiser, eh? Well, no. Nobody in the whole universe sees or knows it, but when the darkness of night comes, sometimes I still lie there as I did when I was a girl five years ago, grinding my teeth and crying all night: ‘Oh, I’ll get him, I’ll get him!’ Have you heard all that? Then how do you understand me now? A month ago that letter suddenly comes: he’s coming, he’s a widower, he wants to see me. It quite took my breath away, Lord, and suddenly I thought: he’ll come and whistle for me, call me, and I’ll crawl to him like a beaten little dog, guilty! I thought it and couldn’t believe myself: ‘Am I base or am I not? Will I run to him or won’t I?’ And all this month I’ve been so angry at myself, worse even than five years ago. Do you see now, Alyosha, how wild I am, how full of rage? I’ve told you the whole truth! I played with Mitya so I wouldn’t run to him. Be quiet, Rakitka, you’re not the one to judge me, I wasn’t speaking to you. Until you came I lay here waiting, thinking, deciding my whole fate, and you’ll never know what was in my heart. No, Alyosha, tell your young lady not to be angry about the other day!… Nobody in the whole world knows what I’m going through now, and nobody can know… Because perhaps I’ll take a knife with me there tonight. I haven’t decided yet…”

<!-- B07-C03-P080 -->
And with this “pitiful” utterance Grushenka suddenly broke down. Unable to finish, she covered her face with her hands, flung herself into the pillows on the sofa and sobbed like a small child. Alyosha rose and went over to Rakitin.

<!-- B07-C03-P081 -->
“Misha,” he said, “don’t be angry. She’s hurt you, but don’t be angry. Did you hear her just now? You can’t demand so much of a human soul. We must be more merciful…”

<!-- B07-C03-P082 -->
Alyosha said this in an irresistible rush of feeling. He needed to speak and turned to Rakitin. Had Rakitin not been there, he would have begun exclaiming to himself. But Rakitin gave him a mocking look, and Alyosha suddenly stopped.

<!-- B07-C03-P083 -->
“They loaded you with your elder earlier, and now you’ve fired your elder at me, Alyoshenka, you little man of God,” Rakitin said with a hateful smile.

<!-- B07-C03-P084 -->
“Don’t laugh, Rakitin, don’t smirk, don’t speak about the dead man: he’s greater than anyone who ever lived on earth!” Alyosha cried, tears in his voice. “I didn’t get up to speak to you as a judge, but as the lowest of the accused myself. Who am I beside her? I came here to be ruined and said, ‘Let it happen, let it!’ And that was my cowardice. But she, after five years of torment, the moment someone first comes and speaks a sincere word to her—she’s forgiven everything, forgotten everything, and she’s crying! The man who wronged her has returned, he’s calling her, and she forgives him everything and hurries to him in joy, and she won’t take a knife, she won’t! No, I’m not like that. No, you haven’t; if you had, you would have understood everything long ago… And the other one, the one she hurt the other day, let her forgive her too! <!-- B07-C03-P085 -->
Alyosha stopped because his breath failed him. Rakitin, despite all his anger, stared in astonishment. He had never expected such a tirade from quiet Alyosha.

<!-- B07-C03-P086 -->
“Well, here’s a lawyer for you! Have you fallen in love with her or something? Agrafena Alexandrovna, our fasting saint really has fallen in love with you. You’ve won!” he shouted with an insolent laugh.

<!-- B07-C03-P087 -->
Grushenka raised her head from the pillow and looked at Alyosha with a tender smile that lit up her face, suddenly swollen from those recent tears.

<!-- B07-C03-P088 -->
“Leave him alone, Alyosha, my cherub. You see what he’s like. What a person to talk to! Mikhail Osipovich,” she turned to Rakitin, “I was going to ask you to forgive me for insulting you, but now I don’t want to again. Alyosha, come to me, sit here.” She beckoned him with a joyful smile. “There, sit down here. Tell me”—she took his hand and looked up into his face, smiling—“tell me: do I love that man or don’t I? The man who wronged me, do I love him or don’t I? Before you came I lay here in the dark, questioning my heart over and over: do I love him or don’t I? Decide for me, Alyosha, the time has come. Whatever you decide, that’s how it will be. Should I forgive him or not?”

<!-- B07-C03-P089 -->
“But you’ve already forgiven him,” Alyosha said, smiling.

<!-- B07-C03-P090 -->
“So I have,” Grushenka said thoughtfully. “What a base heart! Here’s to my base heart!” Suddenly she snatched a glass from the table, drained it, lifted it and hurled it to the floor. The glass shattered with a ringing crash. A trace of cruelty flashed in her smile.

<!-- B07-C03-P091 -->
“But perhaps I haven’t forgiven him yet,” she said with a certain menace, her eyes lowered to the floor, as if speaking to herself alone. “Perhaps my heart is only getting ready to forgive. I’ll fight with my heart yet. You see, Alyosha, I’ve grown terribly fond of those five years of tears… Perhaps it’s only my hurt I’ve grown to love, not him at all!”

<!-- B07-C03-P092 -->
“Well, I wouldn’t want to be in his place!” Rakitin hissed.

<!-- B07-C03-P093 -->
“And you won’t be, Rakitka, you’ll never be in his place. You’ll make my shoes, Rakitka, that’s what I’ll use you for, but you’ll never have a woman like me… And perhaps he won’t either…”

<!-- B07-C03-P094 -->
“He won’t? Then why get dressed up?” Rakitin taunted spitefully.

<!-- B07-C03-P095 -->
“Don’t reproach me with my finery, Rakitka, you don’t know my whole heart yet! If I want to, I’ll tear it off, right now, this very minute!” she cried in a ringing voice. “You don’t know what this finery is for, Rakitka! <!-- B07-C03-P096 -->
She screamed the last words hysterically, but broke down again, covered her face with her hands, flung herself into the pillow and once more shook with sobs. Rakitin stood up.

<!-- B07-C03-P097 -->
“Time to go,” he said. “It’s late. They won’t let us into the monastery.”

<!-- B07-C03-P098 -->
Grushenka sprang up.

<!-- B07-C03-P099 -->
“You don’t really mean to leave, Alyosha!” she exclaimed in anguished astonishment. “What are you doing to me now? You’ve stirred up my whole being, torn me apart, and now another night, now I’m to be left alone again!”

<!-- B07-C03-P100 -->
“He can hardly spend the night with you, can he? But if he wants to, let him! I’ll leave by myself!” Rakitin said with biting mockery.

<!-- B07-C03-P101 -->
“Be quiet, you wicked soul!” Grushenka cried furiously. “You’ve never spoken words to me like the ones he’s come to say.”

<!-- B07-C03-P102 -->
“What has he said to you?” Rakitin grumbled irritably.

<!-- B07-C03-P103 -->
“I don’t know, I can’t tell, I can’t tell at all what he’s said to me. It spoke to my heart, he turned my heart upside down… He was the first, the only one to pity me, that’s what! <!-- B07-C03-P104 -->

<!-- B07-C03-P105 -->

<!-- B07-C03-P107 -->

<!-- B07-C03-P109 -->

<!-- B07-C03-P117 -->

<!-- B07-C03-P124 -->
