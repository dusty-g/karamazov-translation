# Chapter V — Elders

*Part I · Book I: The Story of One Little Family*

<!-- B01-C05-P001 -->

Perhaps some reader will imagine that my young man was sickly, given to ecstasies, stunted in his
development, a pale dreamer, a frail, haggard little creature. Quite the contrary: Alyosha was then
a well-built, rosy-cheeked boy of nineteen, clear-eyed and brimming with health. He was even very
handsome at that time, slender, somewhat above middle height, with dark blond hair, a regular though
slightly elongated oval face, and shining dark gray eyes set wide apart; he was very thoughtful and
apparently very calm. Perhaps someone will say that rosy cheeks are no obstacle to either fanaticism
or mysticism; but it seems to me that Alyosha was more of a realist than anyone. Oh, of course, in
the monastery he believed completely in miracles, but in my opinion miracles will never unsettle a
realist. Miracles do not bring a realist to faith. A true realist, if he is an unbeliever, will
always find the strength and ability to disbelieve even a miracle, and if a miracle confronts him as
an undeniable fact, he will sooner disbelieve his own senses than admit the fact. And if he does
admit it, he will admit it as a natural fact, merely one unknown to him until then. In a realist,
faith is not born of miracles; miracles are born of faith. Once a realist believes, it is precisely
his realism that must make him accept miracles too. The apostle Thomas declared that he would not
believe until he saw, and when he saw, he said, “My Lord and my God!” Was it a miracle that made him
believe? Most likely not: he came to believe solely because he wanted to believe, and perhaps, in
the hidden depths of his being, already believed completely, even while he was still saying, “I will
not believe until I see.”

Perhaps someone will say that Alyosha was dull, undeveloped, had not completed his course of study,
and so on. It was true that he had not completed it, but it would be a great injustice to say he was
dull or stupid. I will simply repeat what I have already said: he took this path only because at that
time it alone had caught his imagination and had shown his soul, struggling from darkness toward
light, the ideal path of escape. Add to this that he was already, in part, a young
man of our own most recent times: honest by nature, demanding truth, seeking it and believing in it,
and once he believed, demanding to take part in it at once with all the strength of his soul,
demanding some great deed without delay, with the firm resolve to sacrifice everything for that deed
if need be, even his life. Unfortunately, these young men do not understand that in a great many
such cases sacrificing one's life may be the very easiest of all sacrifices, whereas to sacrifice,
say, five or six years of a life seething with youth to difficult, arduous study, to learning, if
only to increase tenfold one's strength to serve that same truth and accomplish that same deed one
has set one's heart on and resolved to perform—such a sacrifice is very often almost entirely beyond
many of them. Alyosha had simply chosen a path opposite to everyone else's, but with the same
thirst for an immediate great deed. No sooner had serious thought brought home to him the conviction
that immortality and God existed than he naturally said to himself at once, “I want to live for
immortality, and I will accept no halfway compromise.” Just so, had he decided that immortality and
God did not exist, he would at once have joined the atheists and socialists (for socialism is not
merely a question of labor, or of the so-called fourth estate, but above all a question of atheism,
of atheism embodied in the present day, of the Tower of Babel built expressly without God, not to
reach heaven from earth, but to bring heaven down to earth). To Alyosha, even the thought of living
as before seemed strange and impossible. It is said, “Give away everything and follow me, if you
wish to be perfect.” And Alyosha said to himself, “I cannot give two rubles instead of ‘everything,’
or merely go to the liturgy instead of ‘follow me.’” Among his memories of early childhood there may
have been something of our monastery outside town, where his mother might have taken him to the
liturgy. Perhaps the slanting rays of the setting sun before the icon toward which his mother, the
klikusha, held him out had also played their part. He had come to us then deep in thought, perhaps
merely to see whether here there was everything, or whether here too there were only two rubles,
and—in the monastery he met this elder…

This elder, as I have already explained, was the elder Zosima; but I ought to say a few words here
about what “elders” in our monasteries actually are, and I regret to say that I do not feel
knowledgeable or sure enough of my ground. Still, I will try to give a brief and general account.
First of all, specialists and people qualified to judge maintain that elders and the institution of
eldership appeared here, in our Russian monasteries, only very recently, not even a hundred years
ago, whereas throughout the Orthodox East, particularly on Sinai and Mount Athos, they have existed
for well over a thousand years. They maintain that eldership existed in Russia too in the earliest
times, or certainly must have existed, but that owing to Russia's calamities, Tatar rule, the times
of unrest, and the severing of former ties with the East after the conquest of Constantinople, this
institution was forgotten here and the succession of elders died out. It was revived here from the
end of the last century onward by one of the great ascetics (as he is called), Paisius Velichkovsky,
and his disciples; yet even now, after nearly a hundred years, it exists in only a very few
monasteries and has even sometimes been all but persecuted as an innovation unheard of in Russia.
Here in Russia it has flourished especially at the famous Optina Hermitage of Kozelsk. When and
by whom it was established in our monastery outside town, I cannot say, but the succession there had
already reached its third elder, and the elder Zosima was the last of them; he too, however, was
almost dying of weakness and illness, and they did not even know who could replace him. This was an
important question for our monastery, since until then it had been famous for nothing in particular:
it had no relics of holy saints, no miraculously revealed, wonder-working icons, not even any
glorious legends connected with our history; no historic deeds or services to the fatherland stood
to its credit. It had flourished and become famous throughout Russia precisely because of its
elders, whom crowds of pilgrims flocked to see and hear from all over Russia, from thousands of
versts away. What, then, is an elder? An elder is someone who takes your soul and your will into his
own soul and his own will. Once you choose an elder, you renounce your own will and surrender it to
him in complete obedience, with complete self-renunciation. A person who commits himself to this
trial, this terrible school of life, accepts it voluntarily, in the hope that after a long trial he
will overcome himself and gain such mastery of himself that at last, through lifelong obedience, he
may attain complete freedom, that is, freedom from himself, and escape the fate of those who have
lived their whole lives without finding themselves within themselves. This invention, that is,
eldership, is not theoretical: it was developed in the East through a practice now a thousand years
old. The obligations to an elder are not the same as the ordinary “obedience” that has always
existed in our Russian monasteries too. Here there is continual confession to the elder by all those
pursuing the ascetic life, and an indissoluble bond between the one who binds and the one who is
bound. They tell, for example, of a novice of this kind in the earliest days of Christianity who
failed to carry out an act of obedience imposed on him by his elder, left him and the monastery, and
went to another country, from Syria to Egypt. There, after long and great ascetic labors, he was at
last granted the grace of suffering torture and a martyr's death for the faith. But when the Church
was burying his body, already honoring him as a saint, suddenly, at the deacon's cry, “Catechumens,
depart!” the coffin, with the martyr's body in it, broke loose from its place and was hurled out of
the church; this happened three times. Only at last did they learn that this sainted sufferer had
broken his obedience and left his elder, and therefore could not be forgiven without the elder's
release, despite all his great ascetic labors. Only when the elder was summoned and released him
from his obedience could the burial take place. Of course, all this is only an ancient legend, but
here is a recent true story: one of our own contemporary monks was working out his salvation on
Mount Athos when his elder suddenly ordered him to leave Athos, which he had come to love to the
depths of his soul as a holy place, a quiet haven, and go first to Jerusalem to venerate the holy
places, then back to Russia, to the north, to Siberia: “Your place is there, not here.” Stunned and
overcome with grief, the monk went to Constantinople to see the ecumenical patriarch and begged to
be released from his obedience. And the ecumenical bishop answered that not only could he, the
ecumenical patriarch, not release him, but there was not, and could not be, any authority on earth
that could release him from an obedience once imposed by an elder, except the authority of the very
elder who had imposed it. Thus eldership is endowed, in certain cases, with boundless and
unfathomable power. That is why in many of our monasteries it was at first greeted with something
close to persecution. Meanwhile the people immediately came to hold the elders in great respect.
Both common people and the highest nobility, for instance, flocked to the elders of our monastery to
fall before them, confess their doubts, their sins, their sufferings, and ask for advice and
guidance. Seeing this, the elders' opponents cried out, among other accusations, that the sacrament
of confession was being willfully and thoughtlessly debased, although the continual confession of
one's soul to an elder, whether by his novice or by a layperson, is not performed as a sacrament at
all. In the end, however, eldership survived and is gradually establishing itself in Russian
monasteries. It may also be true that this tried and tested instrument, already a thousand years
old, for a person's moral rebirth from slavery into freedom and toward moral perfection, can become
a double-edged instrument: instead of humility and ultimate self-mastery, it may lead some people to
the very opposite, the most satanic pride—that is, to chains rather than freedom.

The elder Zosima was about sixty-five, came from the landowning gentry, and had once, in his
earliest youth, been a military man, serving in the Caucasus as a junior commissioned officer. No
doubt some special quality of his soul had made a powerful impression on Alyosha. Alyosha lived in
the elder's own cell; the elder had grown very fond of him and admitted him to live there. I should point out that
although Alyosha was living in the monastery then, he was not yet bound by anything, could go
wherever he liked even for whole days at a time, and wore his undercassock voluntarily, so as to look no
different from anyone else in the monastery. But of course he liked wearing it himself, too. Perhaps
the power and fame that constantly surrounded his elder had made a strong impression on Alyosha's
youthful imagination. Many people said of the elder Zosima that after so many years of receiving
everyone who came to confess their hearts to him and thirsted for his advice and a healing word, he
had taken so many disclosures, acts of contrition, and confessions into his soul that he had at
last acquired such keen insight that at his first glance at a stranger's face he could guess what
had brought the person there, what he needed, and even what kind of torment was troubling his
conscience; sometimes he astonished, disconcerted, and almost frightened his visitor by knowing his
secret before he had uttered a word. At the same time, Alyosha almost always noticed that many,
nearly all, of those who came to the elder for their first private conversation entered in fear and
anxiety but almost always left radiant and joyful, and the gloomiest face would become happy.
Alyosha was also extraordinarily struck by the fact that the elder was not at all stern; on the
contrary, he was almost always cheerful in his manner. The monks said that it was precisely the more
sinful whom he took to his heart, and whoever was most sinful he would love most of all. Even toward
the very end of the elder's life, some of the monks hated and envied him, but they were becoming few
and kept silent, although among them were several very celebrated and important figures in the
monastery, such as one of the oldest monks, a renowned keeper of silence and an extraordinary
faster. Still, the overwhelming majority were now undoubtedly on the elder Zosima's side, and very
many of them even loved him with all their hearts, warmly and sincerely; some were attached to him
almost fanatically. These said outright, though not quite openly, that he was a saint, that there
could no longer be any doubt of it, and, foreseeing his approaching death, expected even immediate
miracles and great glory for the monastery from the departed elder in the very near future. Alyosha
too believed unquestioningly in the elder's miraculous power, just as he believed unquestioningly in
the story of the coffin flying out of the church. He saw how many of those who came with sick
children or grown relatives and begged the elder to lay his hands on them and say a prayer over them
returned soon afterward, some the very next day, and fell before the elder in tears, thanking him
for healing their sick. Whether there had really been a healing or merely a natural improvement in
the course of the illness—for Alyosha there was no such question, for he already believed completely
in his teacher's spiritual power, and the elder's fame was, as it were, his own triumph. His heart
trembled especially, and he seemed to shine all over, when the elder came out to the crowd of
pilgrims waiting at the hermitage gates, common people who flocked there from all over Russia
expressly to see the elder and receive his blessing. They fell before him, wept, kissed his feet,
kissed the ground where he stood, cried out; the peasant women held their children out to him, and
ailing klikushi were brought forward. The elder spoke with them, said a short prayer over them,
blessed them, and sent them away. Lately his bouts of illness sometimes left him so weak that he
could scarcely manage to leave his cell, and the pilgrims sometimes waited several days at the
monastery for him to come out. Alyosha had no question about why they loved him so, why they fell
before him and wept with tender feeling at the mere sight of his face. Oh, he understood perfectly
that for the humble soul of a Russian commoner, worn out by toil and grief and above all by
continual injustice and continual sin, both his own and the world's, there was no greater need or
consolation than to find a shrine or a holy person, fall before them and venerate them: “Even if we
have sin, injustice, and temptation, somewhere on earth, in some place or other, there is still
someone holy and above all this; he, at least, has the truth, he, at least, knows the truth; that
means it is not dying out on earth, and so someday it will come to us too and reign over the whole
earth, as promised.” Alyosha knew that this was precisely what the people felt and even how they
reasoned; he understood it, and he himself, together with these weeping peasants and their ailing
women holding their children out to the elder, had not the slightest doubt that the elder was indeed
this very holy man, this guardian of God's truth in the eyes of the people. The conviction that
after his death the elder would bring extraordinary glory to the monastery held sway over Alyosha's
soul perhaps even more powerfully than over anyone else in the monastery. And indeed, throughout
this recent period, a deep, ardent inner rapture had been burning ever more fiercely in his heart.
It did not trouble him at all that this elder was, after all, only one man: “All the same, he is
holy; in his heart is the secret of renewal for everyone, the power that will at last establish
truth on earth, and everyone will be holy, everyone will love one another, and there will be neither
rich nor poor, neither exalted nor humiliated, but everyone will be like God's children, and the
true kingdom of Christ will come.” These were the dreams in Alyosha's heart.

The arrival of his two brothers, whom until then he had not known at all, seems to have made a very
deep impression on Alyosha. He grew closer, and more quickly, to his brother Dmitri Fyodorovich,
though he arrived later, than to his other brother, Ivan Fyodorovich, who shared his mother. He was
terribly eager to get to know his brother Ivan, but Ivan had now been living there for two months,
and though they saw each other quite often, they still could not seem to grow close: Alyosha himself
was quiet and seemed to be waiting for something, seemed to feel shy about something, while his
brother Ivan, although Alyosha had at first noticed his long, curious looks, soon seemed to have
stopped even thinking about him. Alyosha noticed this with some unease. He attributed his brother's
indifference to the difference in their ages and especially their education. But he had another
thought as well: perhaps Ivan's lack of curiosity and interest in him came from something entirely
unknown to Alyosha. For some reason he kept feeling that Ivan was occupied with something, something
inward and important, that he was striving toward some goal, perhaps a very difficult one, which
left him no time for Alyosha, and that this alone explained why he looked at him so absently.
Alyosha also wondered whether there might be some contempt in it, the learned atheist's contempt for
him, the foolish little novice. He knew perfectly well that his brother was an atheist. Such
contempt, if it existed, could not offend him, but still he waited for his brother to wish to come
closer to him, with an anxious uneasiness he himself could not explain. His brother Dmitri
Fyodorovich spoke of his brother Ivan with the deepest respect, spoke of him with a peculiar
intensity of feeling. It was from him too that Alyosha learned all the details of the important
matter that had lately bound his two older brothers together in a remarkably close bond. To Alyosha,
Dmitri's enthusiastic praise of his brother Ivan was all the more striking because Dmitri, compared
with Ivan, was almost wholly uneducated, and the two of them, placed side by side, seemed to present
so stark a contrast in personality and character that perhaps it would have been impossible to
invent two people less alike.

It was at this very time that the meeting—or rather, the family gathering—of all the members of this
discordant family took place in the elder's cell, a gathering that had an extraordinary influence on
Alyosha. The pretext for it was, in truth, a false one. Just then, the disagreements over
inheritance and property accounts between Dmitri Fyodorovich and his father, Fyodor Pavlovich, had
apparently reached an impossible point. Their relations had grown bitter and become unbearable.
Fyodor Pavlovich seems to have been the first to suggest, and apparently as a joke, that they should
all meet in the elder Zosima's cell and, without actually asking him to mediate, somehow reach a more
seemly agreement, with the elder's rank and presence inspiring respect and conciliation. Dmitri
Fyodorovich, who had never visited the elder or even seen him,
naturally thought they were hoping to use the elder to intimidate him, as it were; but since he
privately reproached himself for many particularly harsh outbursts in his recent quarrels with his
father, he accepted the challenge. I should mention, by the way, that he was not living in his
father's house, as Ivan Fyodorovich was, but separately, at the other end of town. Now it so
happened that Pyotr Alexandrovich Miusov, who was staying here at the time, seized on this idea of
Fyodor Pavlovich's with particular enthusiasm. A liberal of the forties and fifties, a freethinker
and atheist, he took an extraordinary interest in the affair, perhaps out of boredom, perhaps for
idle amusement. He suddenly wanted to have a look at the monastery and the “saint.” Since his
longstanding disputes with the monastery were still continuing, and the lawsuit over the boundary
between their lands, over some rights to cut timber in the forest and fish in the river and so
forth, was still dragging on, he hastened to take advantage of this, on the pretext that he himself
would like to speak with the Father Abbot about whether they might somehow settle their disputes
amicably. A visitor with such good intentions could of course expect a more attentive and obliging
welcome at the monastery than someone who was merely curious. All these considerations thus made it
possible for some influence to be brought to bear within the monastery on the sick elder, who lately
scarcely left his cell at all and because of his illness refused even ordinary visitors. In the end
the elder consented, and a day was set. “Who appointed me to divide things between them?”[^b01-c05-n001]
was all he said to Alyosha, with a smile.

When Alyosha learned of the meeting, he became very uneasy. If any of these disputing and bickering
people could take the gathering seriously, it was undoubtedly his brother Dmitri alone; all the rest
would come for frivolous reasons, perhaps offensive to the elder—that was what Alyosha understood.
His brother Ivan and Miusov would come out of curiosity, perhaps of the crudest kind, and his father
perhaps to stage some buffoonish theatrical scene. Oh, Alyosha may have kept silent, but by then he
knew his father well—knew him deeply. I repeat, this boy was by no means as simplehearted as everyone
thought. He waited for the appointed day with a heavy heart. No doubt he was deeply concerned,
inwardly, in his heart, that all these family disagreements should somehow end. Yet his chief
concern was for the elder: he trembled for him and for his reputation, fearing insults to him,
especially Miusov's subtle, polite mockery and the learned Ivan's condescending insinuations; this
was how he pictured it all. He even wanted to risk warning the elder, to say something about the
people who might come, but he thought better of it and kept silent. Only on the eve of the appointed
day did he send word through an acquaintance to his brother Dmitri that he loved him very much and
expected him to keep his promise. Dmitri was puzzled, because he could not remember having promised
him anything of the kind; he replied only with a letter saying that he would do his utmost to
restrain himself “in the face of baseness,” and although he deeply respected the elder and his
brother Ivan, he was convinced that this was either some trap for him or an unworthy comedy.
“Nevertheless, I would sooner swallow my tongue than fail to show respect to the holy man whom you so
greatly respect,” Dmitri concluded his little letter. It did not do much to reassure Alyosha.
