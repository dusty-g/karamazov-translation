# Book XII: A Judicial Error

## X. The Defense Counsel’s Speech. A Stick with Two Ends

<!-- B12-C10-P001 -->
Everything fell silent at the famous orator’s first words. The entire courtroom fixed its eyes on him. He began with extraordinary directness, simplicity, and conviction, but without the slightest arrogance. There was not the slightest attempt at eloquence, at a note of pathos, at little words ringing with emotion. He was a man speaking among an intimate circle of sympathetic people. His voice was beautiful, strong and appealing, and even in the voice itself one seemed already to hear something sincere and straightforward. But everyone immediately understood that the speaker could suddenly rise to true pathos and “strike their hearts with an unknown force.” Perhaps his speech was less grammatically correct than Ippolit Kirillovich’s, but he used no long sentences and was even more precise. One thing displeased the ladies: he kept bending his back in an odd way, particularly at the beginning of his speech, not quite bowing but seeming to strain and fly toward his listeners, bending, as it were, halfway along his long back, as though a hinge had been fitted in the middle of that long, thin back so that it could bend almost at a right angle. At first he spoke in a scattered fashion, seemingly without a system, seizing upon disconnected facts, yet in the end a whole emerged. His speech could have been divided into two halves: the first was criticism, a refutation of the prosecution, at times biting and sarcastic. But in the second half he seemed suddenly to change both his tone and his very method, and at once rose to pathos; the courtroom seemed to have been waiting for it and quivered with delight. He approached the matter directly and began by saying that although he practiced in Petersburg, this was not his first visit to a Russian provincial city to defend an accused person—but only someone of whose innocence he was either convinced or had an intimation beforehand. “The same thing happened to me in the present case,” he explained. “Even in the first newspaper reports alone I glimpsed something that struck me forcibly in the defendant’s favor. In a word, what first interested me was a certain legal circumstance which, though it frequently recurs in judicial practice, has never, I think, appeared so fully or with such distinctive features as in this case. I ought to formulate this circumstance only at the close of my speech, when I have finished, but I shall nevertheless express my thought at the very beginning, for I have a weakness for coming straight to the point, without keeping effects in reserve or rationing impressions. This may be poor tactics on my part, but at least it is sincere. My thought, my formulation, is this: an overwhelming accumulation of facts against the defendant, yet not one fact that stands up to criticism when examined separately, on its own! As I followed the case further through reports and newspapers, I became more and more confirmed in my view, and suddenly I received an invitation from the defendant’s relatives to defend him. I hurried here at once and here became fully convinced. It was to break down this terrible accumulation of facts and expose the unproven, fanciful nature of each incriminating fact separately that I undertook the defense in this case.”

That was how the defense counsel began, then suddenly he proclaimed: “Gentlemen of the jury, I am a newcomer here. I have received all my impressions without preconceptions. The defendant, violent and unbridled in character, has not previously offended me, as he has perhaps a hundred people in this town, which accounts for many people’s prejudice against him. Of course, I too admit that local society’s moral feelings have rightly been aroused: the defendant is violent and unrestrained. Yet he was received in local society, even warmly welcomed in the household of the highly talented prosecutor.” (Nota bene: at these words two or three chuckles sounded among the public; quickly suppressed though they were, everyone noticed them. We all knew that the prosecutor admitted Mitya to his house against his will, solely because the prosecutor’s wife for some reason found him interesting—a most virtuous and respectable lady, but fanciful and willful, who liked on certain occasions, chiefly in small matters, to oppose her husband. Mitya, however, visited their house rather rarely.) “Nevertheless, I venture to suggest,” the defense counsel continued, “that even so independent a mind and so just a character as my opponent’s might have formed a certain mistaken prejudice against my unfortunate client. Oh, it is so natural: the unfortunate man has done all too much to deserve even prejudiced treatment. And offended moral feelings, and still more offended aesthetic feelings, can sometimes be implacable. Of course, in the highly talented speech for the prosecution we heard throughout a rigorous analysis of the defendant’s character and conduct, a rigorously critical approach to the case, and above all such psychological depths were displayed to explain the substance of the case that penetration into those depths would have been quite impossible with any deliberately or maliciously prejudiced attitude toward the defendant’s person. But there are things even worse, even more destructive in such cases, than the most malicious and deliberate attitude toward a case. Suppose, for example, we become possessed by a kind of artistic play, so to speak, a need for artistic creation, for creating a novel, as it were, particularly when God has endowed our abilities with rich psychological gifts. While still in Petersburg, while merely preparing to come here, I was advised—indeed, I knew it myself without any advice—that I would find here as my opponent a profound and exceedingly subtle psychologist, who had long since earned a special reputation for this quality in our still-young legal world. But psychology, gentlemen, profound though it is, still resembles a stick with two ends.” (A chuckle from the public.) “Oh, you will of course forgive my commonplace comparison; I am no master of excessively eloquent speech. But here is an example—I take the first that comes to hand from the prosecutor’s speech. At night, in the garden, the defendant climbs over the fence as he runs away and strikes down with a brass pestle the servant who has seized his leg. Then he immediately jumps back into the garden and spends a full five minutes tending the fallen man, trying to determine whether he has killed him. And the prosecutor absolutely refuses to believe the truth of the defendant’s statement that he jumped down to old Grigory out of pity. ‘No,’ he says, ‘could there be such tenderness at such a moment? That would be unnatural. He jumped down precisely to make sure whether the sole witness to his crime was alive or dead, and thus testified that he had committed that crime, since he could not have jumped into the garden for any other reason, impulse, or feeling.’ That is psychology; but take the very same psychology and apply it to the case from the other end, and the result will be no less plausible. The murderer jumps down as a precaution, to make sure whether the witness is alive, yet according to the prosecutor himself he has just left in his murdered father’s study a stupendous piece of evidence against himself, in the form of a torn packet on which it was written that it contained three thousand rubles. ‘Had he taken that packet with him, no one in the whole world would have known that there was or had been a packet, with money in it, and that the money had therefore been stolen by the defendant.’ Those are the prosecutor’s own words. So, you see, on one point he failed to take precautions, the man lost his head, took fright, and ran away, leaving evidence on the floor, but when some two minutes later he struck and killed another man, the most heartless and calculating instinct of caution immediately appears at our service. But let it be so, let it have happened that way: that, they say, is the subtlety of psychology, that in such circumstances one moment I am bloodthirsty and sharp-eyed as a Caucasian eagle, and the next blind and timid as an insignificant mole. But if I am so bloodthirsty and so cruelly calculating that after killing I jump down only to see whether the witness against me is alive, why, one wonders, should I fuss over this new victim for a full five minutes, perhaps bringing new witnesses upon myself? Why soak my handkerchief wiping blood from the fallen man’s head, so that the handkerchief can later serve as evidence against me? No, if we are so calculating and hardhearted, would it not have been better, after jumping down, simply to batter the fallen servant’s head again and again with the same pestle, to kill him outright and, having eliminated the witness, remove every anxiety from our heart? And finally, I jump down to check whether the witness against me is alive, and there on the path I leave another witness, namely that same pestle which I took from two women, both of whom can always identify it afterward as theirs and testify that I took it from them. Nor did I forget it on the path, drop it in distraction or confusion: no, we actually flung away our weapon, because it was found some fifteen paces from the spot where Grigory had been struck down. The question is, why did we do that? Precisely because we were grieved at having killed a man, an old servant, and so in anguish, with a curse, flung away the pestle as the murder weapon. It could not have been otherwise; why else throw it with such force? And if we could feel pain and pity at having killed a man, it was surely because we had not killed our father: after killing our father we would not have jumped down to another fallen man out of pity; there would have been another feeling then, no room for pity, only for saving ourselves, and that is certainly so. On the contrary, I repeat, we would have smashed his skull outright instead of fussing over him for five minutes. There was room for pity and kind feeling precisely because our conscience had been clear until then. So there is another psychology for you. I have deliberately resorted to psychology myself, gentlemen of the jury, to demonstrate plainly that anything at all can be derived from it. Everything depends on whose hands it is in. Psychology tempts even the most serious people into novel-writing, quite involuntarily. I am speaking of excessive psychology, gentlemen of the jury, of a certain abuse of it.” Here approving chuckles sounded again among the public, all directed at the prosecutor. I shall not reproduce the defense counsel’s entire speech in detail, but take only certain passages, certain principal points.
