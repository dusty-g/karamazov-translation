# Book XII: A Judicial Error
## III. Medical Testimony and a Pound of Nuts

<!-- B12-C03-P001 -->
The medical experts did not help the defendant much either. Fetyukovich himself seems not to have counted on them very heavily, as became clear afterward. Essentially, the medical examination had come about solely at the insistence of Katerina Ivanovna, who had specially summoned the famous doctor from Moscow. The defense could lose nothing by it, of course, and at best might gain something. In part, though, the result was almost comical, owing to a certain disagreement among the doctors. The experts were the visiting celebrity, our own Dr. Herzenstube, and, finally, the young physician Varvinsky. The latter two also appeared as ordinary witnesses called by the prosecutor. Dr. Herzenstube was the first to be questioned as an expert. He was an old man of seventy, gray-haired and balding, of medium height and sturdy build. Everyone in our town valued and respected him highly. He was a conscientious physician, an excellent and devout man, some sort of Herrnhuter or “Moravian Brother”—I do not know for certain. He had lived among us for a very long time and conducted himself with extraordinary dignity. He was kind and humane, treated poor patients and peasants for nothing, went himself into their hovels and cottages and left money for medicine, but he was also as stubborn as a mule. Once an idea had lodged in his head, it was impossible to dislodge it. Incidentally, almost everyone in town already knew that in the two or three days since his arrival, the famous visiting physician had allowed himself several extremely offensive remarks about Dr. Herzenstube’s abilities. The fact was that, although the Moscow doctor charged no less than twenty-five rubles a visit, some people in our town had welcomed the opportunity his arrival offered, spared no expense, and rushed to consult him. All these patients had previously been treated, of course, by Dr. Herzenstube, and the celebrated physician had criticized his treatment everywhere with extraordinary severity. By the end, on arriving at a patient’s house, he would simply ask: “Well, who’s been making a mess of you here? Herzenstube? Heh-heh!” Dr. Herzenstube had, of course, heard all about it. Now the three doctors appeared one after another to be questioned. Dr. Herzenstube stated outright that “the abnormality of the defendant’s mental faculties is evident in itself.” Then, after offering his reasons, which I omit here, he added that this abnormality could be seen not only, and chiefly, in many of the defendant’s previous actions, but now as well, at this very moment. Asked to explain how it could be seen now, at this very moment, the old doctor, with all the directness of his simplehearted nature, pointed out that on entering the hall the defendant “had an unusual appearance and a strange one in the circumstances, marched forward like a soldier, and kept his eyes stubbornly fixed in front of him, whereas it would have been more correct to look to the left, where the ladies sit in the audience, for he was a great lover of the fair sex and must have thought a great deal about what the ladies would now say of him,” the old man concluded in his peculiar way of speaking. I should add that he spoke Russian freely and willingly, but somehow every sentence came out in a German fashion, which never troubled him, however, since all his life he had had a weakness for considering his Russian exemplary, “better even than the Russians’.” He also loved using Russian proverbs, assuring everyone each time that Russian proverbs were the best and most expressive in the world. I should mention, too, that in conversation, perhaps from absentmindedness, he often forgot the most ordinary words, words he knew perfectly well but which for some reason would suddenly slip his mind. The same thing happened when he spoke German, for that matter; and whenever it did, he would wave his hand in front of his face, as if trying to catch the lost little word, and no one could make him continue what he had begun until he found it. His remark that the defendant should have looked at the ladies on entering provoked playful whispers in the audience. All our ladies were very fond of the old man and knew that, a lifelong bachelor, devout and chaste, he regarded women as higher, ideal beings. His unexpected remark therefore struck everyone as exceedingly strange.

Questioned in his turn, the Moscow doctor bluntly and insistently confirmed that he considered the defendant’s mental state abnormal, “even to the highest degree.” He spoke at length and intelligently about “affect” and “mania,” concluding from all the information gathered that for several days before his arrest the defendant had undoubtedly been in a state of pathological affect, and that if he had committed the crime, he had done so consciously, but almost involuntarily, utterly unable to resist the morbid psychological impulse that had taken possession of him. Besides this affect, however, the doctor discerned a mania, which, he said, already pointed straight toward complete insanity in the future. (NB: I am putting this in my own words; the doctor expressed himself in highly scientific and specialized language.) “All his actions run counter to common sense and logic,” he continued. “I will not speak of what I did not see, that is, the crime itself and this whole catastrophe, but even the day before yesterday, while talking with me, he had an inexplicable fixed stare. Unexpected laughter where it was quite uncalled for. Incomprehensible, constant irritation; strange words: ‘Bernard,’ ‘Ethicks,’ and others for which there was no need.” But the doctor saw this mania especially in the fact that the defendant could not even speak about the three thousand rubles he believed he had been cheated of without some extraordinary irritation, whereas he spoke and recalled all his other failures and wrongs with comparative ease. Moreover, according to the information obtained, he had formerly gone into almost the same frenzy every time these three thousand were mentioned, yet people testified that he was disinterested and free of greed. “As for my learned colleague’s opinion,” the Moscow doctor added ironically as he concluded, “that on entering the hall the defendant should have looked at the ladies instead of straight ahead, I shall say only that, apart from the playfulness of such a conclusion, it is also fundamentally mistaken. Although I fully agree that the defendant, on entering the courtroom where his fate is to be decided, should not have stared so fixedly in front of him, and that this might indeed be considered a sign of his abnormal mental state at that moment, I maintain at the same time that he should have looked not to the left, toward the ladies, but, on the contrary, precisely to the right, seeking with his eyes his counsel, in whose assistance all his hopes lie, and on whose defense his entire fate now depends.” The doctor expressed this opinion firmly and emphatically. What made the disagreement between the two learned experts particularly comic, however, was the unexpected conclusion of the physician Varvinsky, questioned last. In his view the defendant was in a perfectly normal state, now as formerly; and although he must indeed have been nervous and extraordinarily agitated before his arrest, this could have arisen from many perfectly obvious causes: jealousy, anger, continual drunkenness, and so forth. But this nervous condition could not have involved any special “affect” of the kind just described. As for whether the defendant should have looked left or right on entering the hall, “in his humble opinion” the defendant should have looked precisely straight ahead, as in fact he had, since directly in front of him sat the presiding judge and members of the court, on whom his entire fate now depended. “Thus, by looking straight ahead, he demonstrated by that very act the perfectly normal state of his mind at that moment,” the young doctor concluded his “humble” testimony with some warmth.

“Bravo, doctor!” Mitya shouted from his seat. “Exactly!”

Mitya was, of course, stopped, but the young doctor’s opinion had a most decisive effect on both court and audience, for, as became clear afterward, everyone agreed with him. Dr. Herzenstube, however, when questioned as an ordinary witness, suddenly and quite unexpectedly did Mitya a service. As a longtime resident who had known the Karamazov family for many years, he gave several pieces of evidence of considerable interest to the “prosecution,” then suddenly added, as though something had occurred to him:

<!-- B12-C03-P005 -->
“And yet the poor young man might have had an incomparably better fate, for he had a good heart, both in childhood and after childhood, for I know this. But the Russian proverb says: ‘If someone has one head, that is good, but if another clever man comes to visit, it will be better still, for then there will be two heads and not only one…’”

<!-- B12-C03-P006 -->
“One head is good, but two are better,” the prosecutor supplied impatiently. He had long known the old man’s habit of speaking slowly and at length, untroubled by the impression he made or by keeping everyone waiting, and, on the contrary, highly esteeming his own ponderous, potato-like German wit, always so cheerfully pleased with itself. The old man did love a joke.

<!-- B12-C03-P007 -->
“Oh, y-yes, that is what I say,” he went on stubbornly. “One mind is good, but two are much better. But no one with another mind came to him, and he let his own go… What is it, where did he let it go? That word—where he let his mind go—I have forgotten,” he continued, turning his hand in front of his eyes. “Ah yes, *spazieren*.”

<!-- B12-C03-P008 -->
“For a walk?”

<!-- B12-C03-P009 -->
“Yes, for a walk, that is what I say. So his mind went out walking and came to such a deep place that it lost itself there. And yet he was a grateful and sensitive young man. Oh, I remember him very well, still a little thing this high, abandoned in his father’s back yard, running about on the ground with no boots and his little trousers held up by one button.”

<!-- B12-C03-P010 -->
A tender, deeply felt note suddenly sounded in the honest old man’s voice. Fetyukovich gave a start, as though sensing something, and immediately seized on it.

<!-- B12-C03-P011 -->
“Oh yes, I was still a young man myself then… I was… yes, I was forty-five then and had only just come here. And I felt sorry for the boy, and asked myself: why can I not buy him a pound of… Yes, a pound of what? I have forgotten what it is called… a pound of something children like very much, what is it—now, what is it…” The doctor began waving his hands again. “It grows on a tree, and they gather it and give it to everyone…”

<!-- B12-C03-P012 -->
“Apples?”

<!-- B12-C03-P013 -->
“Oh, n-no-o! A pound, a pound; apples by the dozen, not by the pound… No, there are many of them, all small, you put them in your mouth and cr-r-rack!…”

<!-- B12-C03-P014 -->
“Nuts?”

<!-- B12-C03-P015 -->
“Yes, nuts, that is what I say,” the doctor confirmed perfectly calmly, as though he had not been searching for the word at all. “And I brought him a pound of nuts, for no one had ever brought the boy a pound of nuts before, and I raised my finger and said to him: ‘Boy! *Gott der Vater*.’[^b12-c03-n001] He laughed and said: ‘*Gott der Vater*.’ ‘*Gott der Sohn*.’ He laughed again and lisped: ‘*Gott der Sohn*.’ ‘*Gott der heilige Geist*.’ Then he laughed again and said as well as he could: ‘*Gott der heilige Geist*.’ And I went away. Two days later I was passing, and he called out to me himself: ‘Uncle, *Gott der Vater, Gott der Sohn*,’ and had only forgotten ‘*Gott der heilige Geist*,’ but I reminded him, and again I felt very sorry for him. But they took him away, and I did not see him again. And then twenty-three years passed. One morning I was sitting in my study, my head already white, when suddenly a flourishing young man came in, whom I could not recognize at all, but he raised his finger and said, laughing: ‘*Gott der Vater, Gott der Sohn und Gott der heilige Geist!* I have just arrived and have come to thank you for the pound of nuts; for no one ever bought me a pound of nuts then, and you alone bought me a pound of nuts.’ And then I remembered my happy youth and the poor boy in the yard with no boots, and my heart turned over, and I said: ‘You are a grateful young man, for all your life you have remembered that pound of nuts I brought you in your childhood.’ And I embraced him and blessed him. And I wept. He laughed, but he wept too… for a Russian very often laughs where he ought to weep. But he wept too; I saw it. And now, alas!…”

<!-- B12-C03-P016 -->
“I’m weeping now too, German, I’m weeping now too, you man of God!” Mitya suddenly shouted from his seat.

<!-- B12-C03-P017 -->
Whatever else may be said, the little story made a somewhat favorable impression on the audience. But the chief effect in Mitya’s favor was produced by Katerina Ivanovna’s testimony, which I shall now describe. Indeed, in general, when the witnesses *à décharge* began—that is, those called by the defense—fortune seemed suddenly, and even in earnest, to smile upon Mitya, and, most remarkably of all, unexpectedly even for the defense itself. Before Katerina Ivanovna, however, Alyosha was questioned, and suddenly remembered a fact that seemed almost like positive evidence against one of the most important points in the prosecution’s case.

[^b12-c03-n001]: German for “God the Father”; the phrases that follow mean “God the Son” and “God the Holy Spirit.”
