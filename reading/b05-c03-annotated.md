# Book V: Pro and Contra
## III. The Brothers Get Acquainted

<!-- B05-C03-P001 -->
Ivan was not, however, in a private room. It was only a space by the window partitioned off with screens, though people outside could not see those sitting behind them. This was the first room, just inside the entrance, with a counter along one wall. Waiters darted through it every moment. The only other customer was an elderly retired military man drinking tea in a corner. But in the other rooms all the usual tavern bustle was going on: shouted calls, beer bottles being uncorked, billiard balls clicking, an organ droning. Alyosha knew that Ivan hardly ever visited this tavern and did not care for taverns in general. So, he thought, he must have come here solely to meet his brother Dmitri by arrangement. Yet Dmitri was not there.

<!-- B05-C03-P002 -->
“Shall I order you some fish soup or something? You can't live on tea alone,” Ivan called, apparently delighted to have drawn Alyosha in. He had finished his own dinner and was drinking tea.

<!-- B05-C03-P003 -->
“Yes, fish soup, and tea afterward too. I'm hungry,” Alyosha said cheerfully.

<!-- B05-C03-P004 -->
“And cherry preserves? They have them here. Remember how you loved cherry preserves at Polenov's when you were little?”

<!-- B05-C03-P005 -->
“You remember that? Yes, preserves too. I still love them.”

<!-- B05-C03-P006 -->
Ivan rang for the waiter and ordered fish soup, tea, and preserves.

<!-- B05-C03-P007 -->
“I remember everything, Alyosha. I remember you up to the age of eleven; I was going on fifteen then. Fifteen and eleven—that's such a difference that brothers at those ages are never companions. I don't even know whether I loved you. When I went to Moscow, for the first few years I never even thought about you. Then, when you came to Moscow yourself, we met only once somewhere, I think. And now I've been living here for over three months, and we still haven't said a word to each other. I'm leaving tomorrow, and just now, sitting here, I was thinking: how can I see him to say goodbye? And there you were, walking past.”

<!-- B05-C03-P008 -->
“Did you really want to see me?”

<!-- B05-C03-P009 -->
“Very much. I want to get to know you once and for all, and let you get to know me. And then say goodbye. I think the best time to get acquainted is just before parting. I saw how you looked at me all these three months. There was a sort of constant expectation in your eyes, and that's exactly what I can't stand, which is why I didn't approach you. But in the end I learned to respect you: there's a fellow who stands firm, I thought. Mind you, though I'm laughing now, I mean it. You do stand firm, don't you? I like people who stand firm, whatever they stand on, even if they're little boys like you. By the end your expectant look had stopped bothering me altogether. On the contrary, I finally grew fond of

<!-- B05-C03-P010 -->
your expectant look. You seem to love me for some reason, Alyosha?”

<!-- B05-C03-P011 -->
“I do, Ivan. Brother Dmitri says of you, ‘Ivan's a tomb.’ I say, ‘Ivan's a riddle.’ You're still a riddle to me, but I've understood something about you already, and only since this morning!”

<!-- B05-C03-P012 -->
“What is it?” Ivan laughed.

<!-- B05-C03-P013 -->
“You won't be angry?” Alyosha laughed too.

<!-- B05-C03-P014 -->
“Well?”

<!-- B05-C03-P015 -->
“That you're just as young as all the other young men of twenty-three. Just as young, as very young, a fresh, delightful boy—a fledgling, after all! Have I offended you very much?”

<!-- B05-C03-P016 -->
“On the contrary, you've astonished me with the coincidence!” Ivan exclaimed warmly and cheerfully. “Would you believe that after our meeting at her house earlier, that was all I could think about—what a twenty-three-year-old fledgling I am? And suddenly you've guessed it and begin with that very thing. I was sitting here just now, and do you know what I was saying to myself? If I lost faith in life, lost faith in the woman I loved, lost faith in the order of things, even became convinced that everything was, on the contrary, a disorderly, accursed, perhaps demonic chaos, if every horror of human disillusionment struck me, I'd still want to live. Once I'd put my lips to this cup, I wouldn't take them away until I'd drained it! Though by thirty I'll probably throw the cup aside, even if I haven't finished it, and go away… I don't know where. But until I'm thirty, I know for certain, my youth will overcome everything—every disillusionment, every disgust with life. I've often asked myself whether there is any despair in the world that could overcome this frantic, perhaps indecent thirst for life in me, and I've decided there seems to be none. Again, I mean until I'm thirty; after that I think I won't want it myself. Certain consumptive little sniveling moralists often call this thirst for life base, especially the poets. It's partly a Karamazov trait, that's true, this thirst for life in spite of everything; it's certainly in you too. But why is it base? There's still a tremendous amount of centripetal force on our planet, Alyosha. I want to live, and I do live, even in defiance of logic. I may not believe in the order of things, but I cherish the sticky little leaves opening in spring, the blue sky, some person whom, would you believe it, you sometimes love without knowing why, some human feat that you may long since have ceased to believe in, but still honor in your heart for old times' sake. Here's your fish soup. Eat and enjoy it. It's excellent soup; they cook well. I want to go to Europe, Alyosha, and I'll go there from here. I know I'll only be going to a graveyard, but to the dearest, the very dearest graveyard—that's what! The dead who lie there are dear to me. Every stone over them speaks of such ardent life in the past, such passionate faith in their achievement, their truth, their struggle, and their knowledge, that I know beforehand I'll fall to the ground and kiss those stones and weep over them—all the while convinced with my whole heart that it has long been nothing but a graveyard. And I won't weep from despair, but simply because I'll be happy in the tears I shed. I'll get drunk on my own tender feelings. The sticky spring leaves, the blue sky—I love them, that's what! It isn't intellect or logic; you love with your insides, your belly, you love your first youthful strength… Do you understand any of my nonsense, Alyoshka, or don't you?” Ivan suddenly laughed.

<!-- B05-C03-P017 -->
“I understand only too well, Ivan. You want to love with your insides and your belly—you put that beautifully, and I'm so glad you want to live so much,” Alyosha exclaimed. “I think everyone must love life before anything else in the world.”

<!-- B05-C03-P018 -->
“Love life more than its meaning?”

<!-- B05-C03-P019 -->
“Absolutely. Love it before logic, as you say, absolutely before logic, and only then will I understand its meaning. That's something I've glimpsed for a long time. Half your task is done and won, Ivan: you love life. Now you need to work at the other half, and you're saved.”

<!-- B05-C03-P020 -->
“You're saving me already, when perhaps I haven't even been lost! And what's this other half of yours?”

<!-- B05-C03-P021 -->
“You must raise your dead, who perhaps never died at all. Now give me some tea. I'm glad we're talking, Ivan.”

<!-- B05-C03-P022 -->
“I see you're quite inspired. I love such professions de foi[^b05-c03-n001] from such… novices. You're a firm man, Alexei. Is it true you want to leave the monastery?”

<!-- B05-C03-P023 -->
“Yes. My elder is sending me into the world.”

<!-- B05-C03-P024 -->
“Then we'll see each other in the world, we'll meet before I'm thirty, when I begin taking my lips from the cup. Father doesn't want to leave his cup until he's seventy; he even dreams of eighty, he told me so himself. He's very serious about that, buffoon though he is. He's taken his stand on his sensuality as though on a rock… though after thirty, it's true, perhaps there's nothing else to stand on… But to keep at it until seventy is base. Better stop at thirty: you can preserve ‘a shade of nobility’ by fooling yourself. Have you seen Dmitri today?”

<!-- B05-C03-P025 -->
“No, but I saw Smerdyakov.” And Alyosha gave his brother a quick but detailed account of his encounter with Smerdyakov. Ivan suddenly began listening with great concern and even asked him to repeat a few things.

<!-- B05-C03-P026 -->
“Only he asked me not to tell Dmitri what he'd said about him,” Alyosha added.

<!-- B05-C03-P027 -->
Ivan frowned and fell into thought.

<!-- B05-C03-P028 -->
“Is it Smerdyakov you're frowning about?” Alyosha asked.

<!-- B05-C03-P029 -->
“Yes, him. To hell with him. I did want to see Dmitri, but now there's no need…” Ivan said reluctantly.

<!-- B05-C03-P030 -->
“Are you really leaving so soon, brother?”

<!-- B05-C03-P031 -->
“Yes.”

<!-- B05-C03-P032 -->
“What about Dmitri and Father? How will it end between them?” Alyosha asked anxiously.

<!-- B05-C03-P033 -->
“There you go again! What have I to do with it? Am I my brother Dmitri's keeper?” Ivan snapped irritably, but suddenly gave a bitter smile. “Cain's answer to God about his murdered brother, eh? Perhaps that's what you're thinking now? But, damn it, I really can't stay here to watch over them, can I? I've finished my business, and I'm leaving. You don't imagine I'm jealous of Dmitri, that I've spent these three months trying to steal his beautiful Katerina Ivanovna? Oh, to hell with it, I had my own business. I've finished my business, and I'm leaving. I finished it earlier today; you witnessed it.”

<!-- B05-C03-P034 -->
“You mean earlier at Katerina Ivanovna's?”

<!-- B05-C03-P035 -->
“Yes, at her house, and I freed myself all at once. So what? What do I care about Dmitri? Dmitri has nothing to do with it. I had only my own business with Katerina Ivanovna. You know yourself, on the contrary, that Dmitri behaved as though he were conspiring with me. I never asked him for anything, but he solemnly handed her over to me himself and gave us his blessing. It's all ridiculous. No, Alyosha, no, if you knew how light I feel now! I was sitting here eating dinner, and believe it or not, I was going to order champagne to celebrate my first hour of freedom. Pah, almost six months—and suddenly, all at once, I threw it all off. Could I even have suspected yesterday how easy it was to end it, if I wanted to?”

<!-- B05-C03-P036 -->
“You mean your love, Ivan?”

<!-- B05-C03-P037 -->
“Love, if you like. Yes, I fell in love with a young lady, a boarding-school girl. I tormented myself over her, and she tormented me. I sat brooding over her… and suddenly it all fell away. Earlier I spoke with inspiration, but I went out and burst out laughing—do you believe that? No, I mean it literally.”

<!-- B05-C03-P038 -->
“You sound so cheerful about it even now,” Alyosha observed, studying his face, which had indeed suddenly brightened.

<!-- B05-C03-P039 -->
“How was I to know I didn't love her at all? Heh-heh! It turns out I don't. And yet how I liked her! How I liked her even earlier today, while I was making my speech. And you know, I still like her tremendously, yet it's so easy to leave her. Do you think I'm showing off?”

<!-- B05-C03-P040 -->
“No. Only perhaps it wasn't love.”

<!-- B05-C03-P041 -->
“Alyoshka,” Ivan laughed, “don't start theorizing about love! It doesn't suit you. Earlier, the way you burst out earlier—oh! I even forgot to kiss you for that… But how she tormented me! I really was sitting beside self-torment itself. Oh, she knew I loved her! She loved me, not Dmitri,” Ivan insisted cheerfully. “Dmitri is only her self-torment. Everything I told her earlier was the absolute truth. Only the thing is, the most important thing, it may take her fifteen or twenty years to realize that she doesn't love Dmitri at all, but only me, the man she torments. And perhaps she'll never realize it, despite today's lesson. All the better: get up and leave forever. By the way, how is she now? What happened after I left?”

<!-- B05-C03-P042 -->
Alyosha told him about her hysterics and that she seemed now to be unconscious and delirious.

<!-- B05-C03-P043 -->
“Could Khokhlakov be lying?”

<!-- B05-C03-P044 -->
“I don't think so.”

<!-- B05-C03-P045 -->
“I should inquire. Still, no one ever died of hysterics. Let her have hysterics; God gave women hysterics out of love. I won't go there at all. Why get involved again?”

<!-- B05-C03-P046 -->
“Yet earlier you told her she'd never loved you.”

<!-- B05-C03-P047 -->
“I said that on purpose. Alyoshka, I'll order some champagne. We'll drink to my freedom. No, if you only knew how happy I am!”

<!-- B05-C03-P048 -->
“No, brother, we'd better not drink,” Alyosha suddenly said. “Besides, I feel rather sad.”

<!-- B05-C03-P049 -->
“Yes, you've been sad for a long time. I've noticed for a long time.”

<!-- B05-C03-P050 -->
“So you're definitely leaving tomorrow morning?”

<!-- B05-C03-P051 -->
“Morning? I didn't say morning… Though perhaps it will be morning. Would you believe I had dinner here today solely to avoid dining with the old man? He's become so repulsive to me. I'd have left long ago just on his account. Why are you so worried that I'm leaving? We still have God knows how much time before I go. An eternity of time, immortality!”

<!-- B05-C03-P052 -->
“If you're leaving tomorrow, how is it an eternity?”

<!-- B05-C03-P053 -->
“What does that matter to us?” Ivan laughed. “We'll still have time to talk about our own things, what we came here for. Why are you looking so surprised? Answer me: what have we met here for? To talk about love for Katerina Ivanovna, about the old man and Dmitri? About going abroad? About Russia's fatal predicament? About the emperor Napoleon? Is that it? Is that what we're here for?”

<!-- B05-C03-P054 -->
“No, it isn't.”

<!-- B05-C03-P055 -->
“Then you understand what it is. Other people have their concerns; we fledglings have ours. First of all we must settle the eternal questions—that's our concern. All young Russia talks about nothing but the eternal questions now. Precisely now, when the old men have all suddenly rushed into practical questions. Why have you looked at me so expectantly all these three months? To question me: ‘What dost thou believe, or dost thou not believe at all?’ That's what your three months of looks amounted to, Alexei Fyodorovich, isn't it?”

<!-- B05-C03-P056 -->
“Perhaps it is,” Alyosha smiled. “You're not laughing at me now, brother?”

<!-- B05-C03-P057 -->
“Laughing? I wouldn't want to hurt my little brother, who's looked at me so expectantly for three months. Alyosha, face it: I'm just as much a little boy as you are, except I'm not a novice. How do Russian boys go about things, even now? Some of them, I mean. Take this stinking tavern: they meet here and settle in a corner. They've never known each other before in their lives, and when they leave the tavern they won't know each other again for forty years. Well then, what will they discuss while they've caught a moment in the tavern? Universal questions, nothing less: does God exist, is there immortality? And those who don't believe in God will talk about socialism and anarchism, about remaking all humanity on a new plan. But it comes to the same damn thing, the same questions approached from the other end. And a great many of our most original Russian boys do nothing nowadays but talk about the eternal questions. Isn't that so?”

<!-- B05-C03-P058 -->
“Yes, for real Russians the questions of whether God exists and whether there is immortality, or, as you put it, the questions from the other end, are certainly the first questions and come before all others. And that's how it should be,” Alyosha said, still studying his brother with the same quiet, searching smile.

<!-- B05-C03-P059 -->
“You know, Alyosha, being Russian is sometimes no sign of intelligence at all, but it's still impossible to imagine anything more foolish than what Russian boys are doing now. Yet there's one Russian boy, Alyoshka, whom I love terribly.”

<!-- B05-C03-P060 -->
“What a lovely way you led up to that!” Alyosha suddenly laughed.

<!-- B05-C03-P061 -->
“Well then, tell me where to begin. You give the orders. With God? Whether God exists?”

<!-- B05-C03-P062 -->
“Begin wherever you like, even at ‘the other end.’ Yesterday at Father's you declared that there is no God.” Alyosha looked searchingly at his brother.

<!-- B05-C03-P063 -->
“Yesterday over dinner at the old man's I was teasing you on purpose, and I saw your eyes flare up. But now I'm quite willing to talk it through with you, and I mean that very seriously. I want to become close to you, Alyosha, because I have no friends. I want to try. Well, imagine this: perhaps I too accept God,” Ivan laughed. “That's unexpected for you, isn't it?”

<!-- B05-C03-P064 -->
“Yes, of course, unless you're joking again.”

<!-- B05-C03-P065 -->
“‘Joking.’ That's what they said at the elder's yesterday, that I was joking. You see, my dear fellow, there was an old sinner in the eighteenth century[^b05-c03-n002] who said that if God didn't exist, we would have to invent him: s’il n’existait pas Dieu il faudrait l’inventer. And indeed, man invented God. The strange thing, the marvelous thing, isn't that God actually exists, but that such an idea—the idea of God's necessity—could enter the head of so savage and wicked an animal as man. It's so holy, so moving, so wise, and does man such credit. As for me, I decided long ago not to think about whether man created God or God created man. Of course I won't run through all the current axioms of the Russian boys on that subject, all of them derived from European hypotheses; for what is a hypothesis there immediately becomes an axiom for the Russian boy, and not just for the boys, perhaps, but for their professors too, since Russian professors nowadays are very often the same Russian boys. So I'll pass over all the hypotheses. What is our task now? Our task is for me to explain my essence to you as quickly as possible: what sort of man I am, what I believe in and hope for. Isn't that it? So I declare plainly and simply that I accept God. But one thing must be noted: if God exists, and if he really created the earth, then, as we know perfectly well, he created it according to Euclidean geometry, and created the human mind with a conception of only three dimensions in space. Yet there have been, and still are, geometers and philosophers, even among the most remarkable, who doubt that the whole universe—or, more broadly still, all existence—was created according to Euclidean geometry alone. They even dare to dream that two parallel lines, which according to Euclid cannot possibly meet on earth, might perhaps meet somewhere in infinity. I've decided, my dear fellow, that if I can't even understand that, how could I understand God? I humbly admit that I have no ability to resolve such questions. My mind is Euclidean, earthly, so how can we decide about what is not of this world? And I advise you never to think about it either, my friend Alyosha, above all about whether God exists or not. These are all questions entirely unsuited to a mind created with a conception of only three dimensions. So I accept God, and not only willingly: more than that, I accept his wisdom and his purpose, which are utterly unknown to us. I believe in order, in the meaning of life, I believe in the eternal harmony in which we are supposedly all to merge, I believe in the Word toward which the universe strives, which itself ‘was with God’ and is itself God, and so on and so on, to infinity. A great many words have been manufactured on that subject. I seem to be on the right road now, don't I? Well then, imagine that in the final result I do not accept this world of God's, and though I know that world exists, I refuse to admit it at all. It's not God I don't accept, understand that. It's the world he created, God's world, that I don't accept and cannot agree to accept. Let me make a qualification: I am convinced, like a child, that suffering will heal and fade away, that all the offensive comedy of human contradictions will vanish like a wretched mirage, like a nasty little invention of the feeble, atom-small, Euclidean human mind, that finally, at the world's culmination, in the moment of eternal harmony, something so precious will happen and appear that it will suffice for every heart, to soothe every indignation, to redeem every human atrocity and all the blood people have shed; it will suffice not merely to make forgiveness possible but to justify everything that has happened to people. Let it all happen, let it appear—but I don't accept it, and I refuse to accept it! Even if parallel lines meet and I see it myself, I'll see it and say they've met, but I still won't accept it. That's my essence, Alyosha, that's my thesis. I've told you this quite seriously. I deliberately began our conversation in the most foolish way possible, but I've brought it to my confession, because that's all you needed. You didn't need to hear about God; you only needed to know what your beloved brother lives by. And I've told you.”

<!-- B05-C03-P066 -->
Ivan suddenly ended his long tirade with a peculiar, unexpected feeling.

<!-- B05-C03-P067 -->
“Why did you begin ‘in the most foolish way possible’?” Alyosha asked, looking at him thoughtfully.

<!-- B05-C03-P068 -->
“Well, first, for a touch of Russianness, if nothing else: Russian conversations on these subjects are always conducted in the most foolish way possible. And second, again, the more foolish, the closer to the point. The more foolish, the clearer. Foolishness is brief and simple, while intelligence dodges and hides. Intelligence is a scoundrel, but foolishness is straight and honest. I've brought things to my despair, and the more foolishly I've presented it, the better for me.”

<!-- B05-C03-P069 -->
“Will you explain why you ‘don't accept the world’?” Alyosha asked.

<!-- B05-C03-P070 -->
“Of course I will. It's no secret; that's what I was leading up to. My little brother, I don't want to corrupt you or shake you from your foundation. Perhaps I'd like to heal myself through you,” Ivan suddenly smiled, just like a gentle little boy. Alyosha had never seen him smile like that before.

[^b05-c03-n001]: “Declarations of faith” (French).

[^b05-c03-n002]: Voltaire, whose verse epistle “To the Author of the Book of the Three Impostors” (1769) contains the line Ivan quotes.
