# Book VII: Alyosha

## II. A Moment Like This

<!-- B07-C02-P001 -->
Father Paisius was certainly right in deciding that his “dear boy” would come back, and perhaps had even discerned the true meaning of Alyosha’s state of mind—not completely, but with insight all the same. Yet I freely admit that I myself would find it very hard now to convey clearly the precise meaning of this strange and uncertain moment in the life of my story’s hero, so dear to me and still so young. To Father Paisius’s anguished question to Alyosha, “Are you too among those of little faith?” I could, of course, firmly answer on Alyosha’s behalf: “No, he is not among those of little faith.” More than that, it was quite the opposite: all his distress arose precisely because he had so much faith. Yet the distress was there, it had come upon him, and it was so painful that even afterward, long afterward, Alyosha counted this sorrowful day among the most burdensome and fateful days of his life.

If anyone asks me outright, “Could all this anguish and such turmoil really have arisen in him solely because his elder’s body, instead of immediately beginning to work miraculous cures, had on the contrary begun to decay so soon?” I will answer without evasion: “Yes, that was indeed the case.” I would only ask the reader not to be too quick to laugh at my young man’s pure heart. As for me, far from intending to ask forgiveness for him, or to excuse and justify his simple faith by his youth, for example, or his limited success in his earlier studies, and so on and so forth, I will do the very opposite and firmly declare that I feel sincere respect for the nature of his heart.

No doubt another young man, one cautious about letting things touch his heart, one who already knew how to love warmly but without heat, whose mind, though sound, was far too reasonable for his age—and therefore cheap—such a young man, I say, would have avoided what happened to mine. But there are times, truly, when it is more honorable to yield to an impulse, however unreasonable, that springs from great love, than not to yield to it at all. All the more so in youth, for a young man who is too consistently reasonable is not to be trusted, and is worth very little—that is my opinion! “But,” sensible people may exclaim at this point, “surely we cannot have every young man believing in such superstition, and your young man sets no rule for anyone else.” To this I will answer once again: yes, my young man believed, devoutly and unshakably, but I still ask no forgiveness for him.

You see, although I declared above—and perhaps too hastily—that I would not explain, apologize for, or justify my hero, I can see that something must nevertheless be made clear if the rest of the story is to be understood. Let me put it this way: it was not so much a matter of miracles. There was no thoughtless impatience in his expectation of miracles. Nor did Alyosha need miracles then to vindicate any convictions of his—certainly not that—or so that some earlier, preconceived idea could triumph over another as quickly as possible. Oh no, nothing of the kind: through all this, first and foremost, what stood before him was a person, and only a person—the person of his beloved elder, of that righteous man whom he revered to the point of adoration. The very point is that all the love for “everyone and everything” hidden in his young, pure heart, both then and throughout the preceding year, seemed at times to concentrate itself, perhaps even wrongly, chiefly on a single being—at least in his heart’s most powerful impulses—on his beloved elder, now dead.

It is true that this being had stood before him so long as an unquestioned ideal that all his youthful strength and all its striving could no longer help turning exclusively toward this ideal, at times even to the point of forgetting “everyone and everything.” (He himself recalled afterward that on that painful day he had completely forgotten his brother Dmitri, about whom he had been so concerned and anxious the day before; he had also forgotten to take the two hundred rubles to Ilyushechka’s father, as he had so ardently intended to do that same previous day.)

But again, what he needed was not miracles, only “higher justice,” which, as he believed, had been violated, wounding his heart so cruelly and suddenly. And what did it matter that in Alyosha’s expectations this “justice,” by the very course of events, had taken the form of miracles expected immediately from the remains of the adored guide he had lost? Everyone in the monastery thought and expected the same, even those whose intellect Alyosha revered—Father Paisius himself, for example—and so Alyosha, untroubled by any doubts, had given his dreams the same form as everyone else. Indeed, a whole year of monastic life had long since settled things this way in his heart, and his heart had grown accustomed to expecting this. But it was justice he thirsted for, justice, and not merely miracles!

And now the very man who, in his hopes, should have been exalted above everyone in the whole world, instead of receiving the glory that was his due, had suddenly been cast down and disgraced! What for? Who had passed judgment? Who could have judged like this? These were the questions that at once began tormenting his inexperienced and innocent heart. He could not bear without taking offense, without even bitterness in his heart, that the most righteous of the righteous should be handed over to such mocking, malicious derision by a crowd so frivolous and so far beneath him. Well then, let there be no miracles at all, let nothing wondrous be revealed, let what they expected fail to come about at once—but why had dishonor appeared instead, why had disgrace been permitted, why this hasty decay that had “outstripped nature,” as the malicious monks were saying? Why this “sign” that they and Father Ferapont were now so triumphantly deducing from it, and why did they believe they had even been given the right to draw such a conclusion? Where was Providence, and its guiding finger? Why had it hidden its finger “at the very moment it was most needed,” Alyosha thought, as though it itself had chosen to submit to the blind, mute, pitiless laws of nature?

This was why Alyosha’s heart was bleeding, and certainly, as I have already said, what mattered above all was the person he loved more than anyone in the world, that very person now “disgraced” and “dishonored”! Grant that my young man’s protest was thoughtless and unreasonable; still, I repeat for the third time—and admit in advance that perhaps I too am being thoughtless—I am glad that my young man proved less than reasonable at such a moment, for reason will always have its day in a man who is no fool, whereas if love is absent from a young man’s heart even at such an exceptional moment, when will it ever come?

Yet I do not wish to pass over a certain strange phenomenon that manifested itself in his mind, if only for an instant, at this fateful and bewildering moment for Alyosha. This new something that had appeared and flashed through him was a certain painful impression left by yesterday’s conversation with his brother Ivan, which Alyosha now kept recalling. Precisely now. Oh, it was not that anything had shaken his soul’s fundamental, elemental beliefs, so to speak. He loved his God and believed in him unshakably, though he had suddenly murmured against him. Yet some vague but painful and angry impression, stirred by the memory of yesterday’s conversation with his brother Ivan, had suddenly begun moving in his soul again, pressing more and more insistently to rise to the surface. When dusk was already deepening, Rakitin, walking through the pine grove from the hermitage to the monastery, suddenly noticed Alyosha lying beneath a tree, face to the ground, motionless and apparently asleep. He approached and called to him.

<!-- B07-C02-P004 -->
“You’re here, Alexei? Surely you haven’t...” he began in surprise, but broke off without finishing. He had meant to say, “Surely it hasn’t come to this?” Alyosha did not look up at him, but from a slight movement Rakitin immediately guessed that he could hear and understand him.

<!-- B07-C02-P005 -->
“What’s the matter with you?” he went on in surprise, though the surprise on his face was already giving way to a smile that grew more and more mocking.

<!-- B07-C02-P006 -->
“Listen, I’ve been looking for you for over two hours. You suddenly vanished from back there. What are you doing here? What’s all this pious foolishness? At least look at me...”

<!-- B07-C02-P007 -->
Alyosha lifted his head, sat up, and leaned back against the tree. He was not crying, but his face showed suffering and his eyes irritation. He was looking off to one side, however, rather than at Rakitin.

<!-- B07-C02-P008 -->
“You know, your face has completely changed. None of that celebrated meekness of yours left. Angry with someone, are you? Has someone hurt your feelings?”

<!-- B07-C02-P009 -->
“Leave me alone!” Alyosha said suddenly, still not looking at him, and waved a weary hand.

<!-- B07-C02-P010 -->
“Oh, so that’s how it is! We’ve taken to shouting just like the rest of us mortals. And you one of the angels! Well, Alyoshka, you’ve surprised me, do you know that? I mean it. It’s been a long time since anything here surprised me. I did at least take you for an educated man...”

<!-- B07-C02-P011 -->
Alyosha finally looked at him, but absently, as though he still hardly understood him.

<!-- B07-C02-P012 -->
“Can all this really be just because your old man has begun to stink? Did you seriously believe he’d start pulling off miracles?” Rakitin exclaimed, passing once again into the most sincere astonishment.

<!-- B07-C02-P013 -->
“I did believe, I do believe, and I want to believe, and I will believe—what more do you want?” Alyosha shouted irritably.

<!-- B07-C02-P014 -->
“Nothing at all, my dear fellow. Damn it, even a thirteen-year-old schoolboy doesn’t believe that these days. Still, the devil... So now you’re angry with this God of yours, you’ve rebelled: passed over for promotion, were you, no decoration for the holiday! Oh, you people!”

<!-- B07-C02-P015 -->
Alyosha looked at Rakitin for a long time, his eyes narrowed a little, and something suddenly flashed in them... but it was not anger at Rakitin.

<!-- B07-C02-P016 -->
“I’m not rebelling against my God, I simply ‘don’t accept his world,’” Alyosha said with a sudden crooked smile.

<!-- B07-C02-P017 -->
“What do you mean, you don’t accept the world?” Rakitin considered Alyosha’s answer for a moment. “What nonsense is this?”

<!-- B07-C02-P018 -->
Alyosha did not answer.

<!-- B07-C02-P019 -->
“Well, enough nonsense. Now to business: have you eaten today?”

<!-- B07-C02-P020 -->
“I don’t remember... I think so.”

<!-- B07-C02-P021 -->
“You need something to keep your strength up, judging by your face. The sight of you makes me feel sorry for you. You didn’t sleep last night either, I hear—you were all sitting in session over there. And then all this fuss and mess... I suppose all you’ve chewed on is a scrap of blessed bread. I’ve got some sausage in my pocket; I brought it from town earlier, just in case, on my way here. Only you won’t eat sausage...”

<!-- B07-C02-P022 -->
“Give me some sausage.”

<!-- B07-C02-P023 -->
“Aha! So that’s how it is! Full-scale rebellion, then, up with the barricades! Well, my friend, we mustn’t neglect a thing like this. Let’s go to my place... I could knock back a little vodka myself now. I’m tired to death. I bet you won’t dare try the vodka... or will you have some?”

<!-- B07-C02-P024 -->
“Give me some vodka too.”

<!-- B07-C02-P025 -->
“Well, look at that! Extraordinary, my friend!” Rakitin stared at him wildly. “Anyway, one way or the other, vodka or sausage, this is a bold move, a good one, and we can’t let it slip. Come along!”

<!-- B07-C02-P026 -->
Alyosha got up from the ground without a word and followed Rakitin.

<!-- B07-C02-P027 -->
“If your brother Vanechka could see this, wouldn’t he be amazed! By the way, that brother of yours, Ivan Fyodorovich, set off for Moscow this morning. Did you know?”

<!-- B07-C02-P028 -->
“I know,” Alyosha said indifferently, and suddenly an image of his brother Dmitri flashed through his mind—but only flashed; and though it reminded him of something, some urgent business that could no longer be postponed even for a moment, some duty, some terrible obligation, even this recollection made no impression on him, did not reach his heart, flew out of his memory at that very instant and was forgotten. But Alyosha remembered this for a long time afterward.

<!-- B07-C02-P029 -->
“Your brother Vanechka once pronounced me a ‘talentless liberal nonentity.’ And you couldn’t help yourself once either, and gave me to understand that I was ‘dishonest’... So be it! Now let’s have a look at your talent and honesty.” (Rakitin finished this to himself, in a whisper.) “Pah, listen!” he went on aloud again. “Let’s go around the monastery and take the path straight into town... Hm. I ought to drop in on Khokhlakov while I’m there. Imagine: I wrote to tell her everything that had happened, and would you believe it, she sends a note straight back, in pencil—this lady is terribly fond of writing notes—to say that ‘she never expected such behavior from so venerable an elder as Father Zosima!’ That’s exactly what she wrote: ‘behavior’! She’s angry too. Oh, all of you! Wait!” he suddenly cried again, stopping short and catching Alyosha by the shoulder to stop him too.

<!-- B07-C02-P030 -->
“You know, Alyoshka...” He peered searchingly into his eyes, wholly taken up with a sudden new thought that had flashed upon him, and though outwardly he was laughing, he was plainly afraid to voice this sudden new thought: he still found it so hard to believe the strange and utterly unexpected mood in which he now saw Alyosha. “Alyoshka, do you know where we’d do best to go now?” he finally said, timidly, coaxingly.

<!-- B07-C02-P031 -->
“It makes no difference... wherever you like.”

<!-- B07-C02-P032 -->
“Let’s go to Grushenka’s, shall we? Will you?” Rakitin finally said, actually trembling all over with timid anticipation.

<!-- B07-C02-P033 -->
“Let’s go to Grushenka’s,” Alyosha answered calmly and at once, and this was so unexpected to Rakitin—his agreeing so quickly and calmly, that is—that he almost jumped back.

<!-- B07-C02-P034 -->
“W-well!.. There!” he began to cry in astonishment, but suddenly seized Alyosha firmly by the arm and hurried him down the path, still desperately afraid that his resolve would fade. They walked in silence; Rakitin was afraid even to speak.

<!-- B07-C02-P035 -->
“How pleased she’ll be, how pleased...” he began to mutter, but fell silent again. And it was certainly not for Grushenka’s pleasure that he was dragging Alyosha to her; he was a serious man and did nothing without some advantage to himself in view. His purpose now was twofold: first, revenge—that is, to witness “the disgrace of the righteous man” and Alyosha’s probable “fall” from “saint to sinner,” a prospect he was already savoring; and second, he also had a certain material aim in mind, highly profitable to himself, of which more will be said below.

<!-- B07-C02-P036 -->
“So a moment like this has come along,” he thought to himself, merrily and maliciously. “Well then, we’ll catch it by the scruff of the neck, this little moment, since it suits us so very well.”
