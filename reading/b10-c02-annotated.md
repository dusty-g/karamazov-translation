# Book X: Boys
## II. The Little Ones

<!-- B10-C02-P001 -->
And so, on that frosty November morning, with its bitter north wind, the boy Kolya Krasotkin was sitting at home. It was Sunday, and there were no classes. But eleven had already struck, and he absolutely had to go out “on a matter of great importance”; meanwhile he was the only one left in the entire house, effectively its guardian, for it so happened that all its grown-up inhabitants had gone out because of a certain urgent and unusual circumstance. In the widow Krasotkin’s house, across the entrance passage from the rooms she occupied herself, there was one other little apartment—the only other one in the house—consisting of two small rooms, which she rented to a doctor’s wife and her two young children. The doctor’s wife was the same age as Anna Fyodorovna and a close friend of hers. As for the doctor himself, a year earlier he had gone away somewhere, first to Orenburg and then to Tashkent, and for six months now not a word had been heard from him; so if her friendship with Madame Krasotkin had not somewhat eased the abandoned wife’s grief, she would positively have wept herself away. And, to crown all these oppressions of fate, it had to happen that on this very night, between Saturday and Sunday, Katerina, the doctor’s wife’s only servant, suddenly announced, to her mistress’s complete surprise, that she intended to give birth to a baby by morning. How no one had noticed beforehand was almost a miracle to them all. The astonished doctor’s wife decided, while there was still time, to take Katerina to a midwife’s establishment in our town equipped for such occasions. Since she valued this servant very highly, she immediately carried out her plan, took her there, and stayed with her as well. Then in the morning, for some reason, the full friendly assistance and support of Madame Krasotkin herself became necessary: she could ask someone for something in this case and bring some influence to bear. Thus both ladies were absent, while Madame Krasotkin’s own servant, Agafya, had gone to the market, leaving Kolya temporarily guardian and watchman of the “little bubbles”—the doctor’s boy and girl, left all on their own. Kolya was not afraid of guarding the house, especially since he had Perezvon with him. The dog had been ordered to lie facedown under the bench in the front hall “without moving,” and for that very reason, whenever Kolya appeared in the hall as he paced from room to room, he would jerk his head up and give two firm, ingratiating thumps of his tail against the floor; but alas, the whistle summoning him never came. Kolya would cast a stern look at the unfortunate dog, who would freeze once more into obedient immobility. If anything troubled Kolya, it was only the “bubbles.” He naturally regarded Katerina’s unexpected adventure with the deepest contempt, but he was very fond of the temporarily orphaned “bubbles”, and had already brought them some children’s book. Nastya, the older girl, was eight and could read, and the younger bubble, seven-year-old Kostya, loved listening when Nastya read to him. Of course, Krasotkin could have entertained them more interestingly: lined them both up and started playing soldiers with them, or hide-and-seek all over the house. He had often done so before and was not above it, so that word had even once gone round his class that at home Krasotkin played horses with the little children lodging there, prancing along as an outside horse and bending his head. But Krasotkin proudly parried the charge, pointing out that it would indeed be shameful “in this day and age” to play horses with boys his own age, thirteen-year-olds, but that he did it for the “bubbles” because he loved them, and no one dared call him to account for his feelings. And how both the “bubbles” adored him! This time, however, there was no time for games. He had a very important piece of business of his own ahead of him, something apparently almost mysterious; meanwhile time was passing, and Agafya, with whom he could have left the children, still showed no sign of returning from the market. Several times already he had crossed the passage, opened the doctor’s wife’s door, and looked anxiously at the “bubbles,” who were sitting over their book as he had ordered. Each time he opened the door they silently grinned at him from ear to ear, expecting him to come in and do something wonderful and amusing. But Kolya was troubled in spirit and did not come in. At last eleven struck, and he made a firm and final decision that if that “accursed” Agafya had not returned in ten minutes he would leave without waiting for her, after obtaining the “bubbles’” promise, of course, that they would not get frightened without him, get into mischief, or cry from fear. With these thoughts he put on his padded winter coat, with its fur collar of some sort of seal, slung his bag over his shoulder, and, despite his mother’s many earlier pleas that he should always wear his galoshes when going out “in such cold,” merely gave them a contemptuous look as he passed through the front hall and went out in his boots alone. Seeing him dressed, Perezvon began thumping his tail vigorously against the floor, his whole body twitching with excitement, and even gave a plaintive howl; but Kolya, judging that such passionate impetuosity in his dog was bad for discipline, kept him under the bench for another minute, and only when he had opened the door into the passage did he suddenly whistle. The dog sprang up like a mad thing and began leaping about before him in delight. Crossing the passage, Kolya opened the “bubbles’” door. They were still sitting at their little table, but had stopped reading and were arguing heatedly about something. These children often argued over various challenging questions of everyday life, and Nastya, as the older one, always got the upper hand; if Kostya disagreed with her, he nearly always went to appeal to Kolya Krasotkin, and whatever he decided stood as an absolute verdict binding on all parties. This time the “bubbles’” dispute interested Krasotkin somewhat, and he stopped in the doorway to listen. The children saw that he was listening and continued their wrangle with all the more zest.

“I’ll never, never believe,” Nastya chattered heatedly, “that midwives find little babies in the kitchen garden, among the cabbage beds. It’s winter now, and there aren’t any beds, so the midwife couldn’t have brought Katerina a daughter.”

<!-- B10-C02-P003 -->
“Whew!” Kolya whistled to himself.

<!-- B10-C02-P004 -->
“Or perhaps it’s like this: they bring them from somewhere, but only to people who get married.”

<!-- B10-C02-P005 -->
Kostya gazed intently at Nastya, listening and thinking it over very seriously.

<!-- B10-C02-P006 -->
“Nastya, you’re such a fool,” he said at last, firmly and without getting excited. “How can Katerina have a baby when she isn’t married?”

<!-- B10-C02-P007 -->
Nastya grew terribly heated.

<!-- B10-C02-P008 -->
“You don’t understand anything,” she snapped. “Perhaps she had a husband, only he’s in prison, and so she’s had a baby.”

<!-- B10-C02-P009 -->
“Is her husband in prison, then?” the practical-minded Kostya inquired gravely.

<!-- B10-C02-P010 -->
“Or else this,” Nastya interrupted breathlessly, completely abandoning and forgetting her first hypothesis. “She hasn’t got a husband, you’re right about that, but she wants to get married, so she began thinking about getting married, and she thought and thought and kept on thinking until what she got wasn’t a husband but a baby.”

<!-- B10-C02-P011 -->
“Well, that could be it,” agreed Kostya, entirely defeated. “But you didn’t say that before, so how could I know?”

<!-- B10-C02-P012 -->
“Well, little ones,” said Kolya, stepping into their room, “I see you’re a dangerous pair!”

<!-- B10-C02-P013 -->
“Is Perezvon with you too?” Kostya grinned and began snapping his fingers and calling Perezvon.

<!-- B10-C02-P014 -->
“Bubbles, I’m in a difficulty,” Krasotkin began gravely, “and you must help me. Agafya has broken her leg, of course, since she still hasn’t turned up—that’s settled, signed, and sealed—and I absolutely have to go out. Will you let me go or won’t you?”

<!-- B10-C02-P015 -->
The children exchanged anxious glances; their faces fell and began to show uneasiness. They did not yet quite understand, however, what was being asked of them.

<!-- B10-C02-P016 -->
“You won’t get into mischief without me? Won’t climb onto the cupboard and break your legs? Won’t cry from being frightened all alone?”

<!-- B10-C02-P017 -->
Terrible misery appeared on the children’s faces.

<!-- B10-C02-P018 -->
“In return I could show you something—a little brass cannon you can fire with real gunpowder.”

<!-- B10-C02-P019 -->
The children’s faces instantly brightened.

<!-- B10-C02-P020 -->
“Show us the cannon,” said Kostya, all aglow.

<!-- B10-C02-P021 -->
Krasotkin reached into his bag, took out a little bronze cannon, and set it on the table.

<!-- B10-C02-P022 -->
“Show you, indeed! Look, it’s on wheels.” He rolled the toy along the table. “And you can fire it. Load it with shot and fire.”

<!-- B10-C02-P023 -->
“Will it kill?”

<!-- B10-C02-P024 -->
“It’ll kill anyone, you only have to aim.” And Krasotkin explained where to put the powder and where to roll in the pellet, showed them the little touchhole, and told them about the recoil. The children listened with tremendous curiosity. The recoil especially fired their imaginations.

<!-- B10-C02-P025 -->
“Have you got any powder?” Nastya asked.

<!-- B10-C02-P026 -->
“Yes.”

<!-- B10-C02-P027 -->
“Show us the powder too,” she drew out the words with a pleading smile.

<!-- B10-C02-P028 -->
Krasotkin rummaged in his bag again and took out a little bottle that did indeed contain some real gunpowder, while a folded paper held a few pellets of shot. He even uncorked the bottle and poured a little powder into his palm.

<!-- B10-C02-P029 -->
“There, only there mustn’t be any fire near it, or it’ll blow up and kill us all,” Krasotkin warned, for effect.

<!-- B10-C02-P030 -->
The children studied the powder with reverent fear, which only increased their pleasure. But Kostya liked the shot better.

<!-- B10-C02-P031 -->
“Does shot burn?” he asked.

<!-- B10-C02-P032 -->
“Shot doesn’t burn.”

<!-- B10-C02-P033 -->
“Give me a little shot,” he said in a pleading little voice.

<!-- B10-C02-P034 -->
“I’ll give you a little. Here, take it, only don’t show your mama before I—before I come back, or she’ll think it’s gunpowder and die of fright, and give you a whipping.”

<!-- B10-C02-P035 -->
“Mama never whips us with a birch,” Nastya immediately pointed out.

<!-- B10-C02-P036 -->
“I know, I only said it for the elegance of the phrase. And you must never deceive your mama, but this time—until I come back. So, bubbles, may I go or not? You won’t cry from fright without me?”

<!-- B10-C02-P037 -->
“We wi-ill,” Kostya drawled, already preparing to cry.

<!-- B10-C02-P038 -->
“We will, we certainly will!” Nastya echoed in a frightened rush.

<!-- B10-C02-P039 -->
“Oh, children, children, what a perilous age you’re at. Nothing to be done, little chicks, I’ll have to sit with you for who knows how long. And the time, the time—ugh!”

<!-- B10-C02-P040 -->
“Tell Perezvon to play dead,” Kostya asked.

<!-- B10-C02-P041 -->
“Well, there’s nothing for it, we’ll have to fall back on Perezvon too. Ici, Perezvon!” And Kolya began giving the dog orders, and the dog performed everything he knew. He was a shaggy dog, the size of an ordinary mongrel, with a sort of grayish-purple coat. He was blind in his right eye, and for some reason his left ear was slit. He yelped and jumped, begged, walked on his hind legs, threw himself onto his back with all four paws in the air, and lay motionless as though dead. During this last trick the door opened, and Agafya, Madame Krasotkin’s stout servant, a pockmarked woman of about forty, appeared on the threshold, back from the market with a bag of provisions in her hand. She stopped, the bag dangling from her left hand, and watched the dog. Much as Kolya had been waiting for Agafya, he did not interrupt the performance; after keeping Perezvon dead for the prescribed time, he finally whistled, and the dog leaped up and began bounding about for joy at having done his duty.

<!-- B10-C02-P042 -->
“Look at that dog!” Agafya remarked instructively.

<!-- B10-C02-P043 -->
“And why are you late, female?” Krasotkin demanded sternly.

<!-- B10-C02-P044 -->
“Female, is it? You little pimple!”

<!-- B10-C02-P045 -->
“Pimple?”

<!-- B10-C02-P046 -->
“A pimple, yes. What’s it to you if I’m late? If I’m late, there’s a reason for it,” Agafya muttered, beginning to busy herself by the stove; but her voice was neither displeased nor angry—quite the contrary, she sounded very pleased, as though delighted at the chance to exchange jibes with the merry young master.

<!-- B10-C02-P047 -->
“Listen, frivolous old woman,” Krasotkin began, rising from the sofa, “can you swear to me by everything holy in this world, and by something else besides, that you’ll keep an unceasing watch over the bubbles in my absence? I’m going out.”

<!-- B10-C02-P048 -->
“Why should I swear to you?” Agafya laughed. “I’ll look after them anyway.”

<!-- B10-C02-P049 -->
“No, you must swear by the eternal salvation of your soul. Otherwise I won’t go.”

<!-- B10-C02-P050 -->
“Then don’t go. What do I care? It’s freezing outside, stay home.”

<!-- B10-C02-P051 -->
“Bubbles,” Kolya addressed the children, “this woman will stay with you until I return, or until your mama does, because she ought to have been back long ago too. What’s more, she’ll give you some breakfast. Will you give them something, Agafya?”

<!-- B10-C02-P052 -->
“That can be done.”

<!-- B10-C02-P053 -->
“Goodbye, little chicks. I’m leaving with an easy heart. And you, granny,” he added gravely in an undertone as he passed Agafya, “I trust you won’t tell them the usual old wives’ nonsense about Katerina. Spare their tender years. Ici, Perezvon!”

<!-- B10-C02-P054 -->
“Oh, go with God!” Agafya snapped, genuinely angry now. “Such a funny fellow! You ought to get a whipping yourself, that’s what, for talking that way.”
