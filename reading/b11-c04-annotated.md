# Book XI: Brother Ivan Fyodorovich
## IV. A Hymn and a Secret

<!-- B11-C04-P001 -->
It was already quite late—and how long is a November day, after all?—when Alyosha rang at the prison gate. Dusk was even beginning to fall. But Alyosha knew he would be admitted to Mitya without difficulty. Things in our town were much as they are everywhere. At first, of course, once the preliminary investigation was over, visits to Mitya by relatives and certain other people had still been subject to various necessary formalities; later, it was not so much that these formalities relaxed as that certain exceptions somehow established themselves, at least for some of Mitya’s visitors. It went so far that sometimes their meetings with the prisoner in the room set aside for the purpose took place almost in private. There were very few such visitors, however: only Grushenka, Alyosha, and Rakitin. The district police chief, Mikhail Makarovich himself, looked very favorably on Grushenka. The old man’s harsh shout at her in Mokroye weighed on his heart. Later, when he learned the whole story, he changed his opinion of her completely. And strangely, although he was firmly convinced of Mitya’s guilt, he had somehow come to regard him more and more gently since his imprisonment: “Perhaps the man had a good soul, but drink and disorder have finished him off, routed him like a Swede!” His earlier horror had given way to a kind of pity. As for Alyosha, the police chief liked him very much and had known him for a long time; and Rakitin, who subsequently took to visiting the prisoner very often, was one of the closest acquaintances of “the police chief’s young ladies,” as he called them, and hung around their house every day. He also gave lessons in the household of the prison governor, a good-natured old man, though a staunch man of duty. Alyosha, once again, was a special and longstanding acquaintance of the governor too, who liked talking to him about “wisdom” in general. The governor, for example, did not merely respect Ivan Fyodorovich; he was actually afraid of him, chiefly of his judgments, although he was a great philosopher himself, having, of course, “thought it all out on his own.” But for Alyosha he felt a sort of irresistible affection. Over the past year the old man had settled down to the apocryphal gospels and was constantly sharing his impressions with his young friend. Earlier he had even visited him at the monastery and spent hours talking with him and the priest-monks. In short, even if Alyosha arrived at the prison too late, he only had to go to the governor and everything could always be arranged. Besides, everyone in the prison, down to the last keeper, was used to Alyosha. The guard, of course, raised no obstacle provided the authorities gave permission. When summoned, Mitya always came downstairs from his little cell to the place set aside for visits. As Alyosha entered the room, he ran straight into Rakitin, who was just leaving Mitya. Both were speaking loudly. Mitya, seeing him out, was laughing heartily at something, while Rakitin seemed to be grumbling. Rakitin, especially of late, disliked meeting Alyosha, scarcely spoke to him, and even acknowledged his greeting with an effort. Now, seeing Alyosha enter, he frowned particularly hard and looked away, apparently absorbed in buttoning his large warm coat with its fur collar. Then he immediately began looking for his umbrella.

<!-- B11-C04-P002 -->
“Mustn’t leave any of my things behind,” he muttered, solely to have something to say.

<!-- B11-C04-P003 -->
“Mind you don’t leave any of other people’s things behind!” Mitya quipped, and immediately burst out laughing at his own joke. Rakitin flared up at once.

<!-- B11-C04-P004 -->
“Give that advice to your Karamazovs, you brood of serf owners, not to Rakitin!” he suddenly shouted, shaking with anger.

<!-- B11-C04-P005 -->
“What’s the matter with you? It was a joke!” Mitya cried. “Oh, damn it! They’re all like that,” he said to Alyosha, nodding toward the rapidly departing Rakitin. “He sat here laughing and enjoying himself, then suddenly he boils over! He didn’t even nod to you. Have you two quarreled for good? Why are you so late? I haven’t just been waiting for you, I’ve been thirsting for you all morning. Well, never mind! We’ll make up for it.”

<!-- B11-C04-P006 -->
“Why has he taken to coming so often? Have you become friends?” Alyosha asked, nodding too toward the door through which Rakitin had gone.

<!-- B11-C04-P007 -->
“Friends with Mikhail? No, not exactly. Why should I? The swine! He thinks I’m… a scoundrel. And they can’t take a joke—that’s the chief thing about them. They’ll never understand a joke. Their souls are dry, too, flat and dry, like when I was being driven up to the prison that day and looked at the prison walls. But he’s a clever man, clever. Well, Alexei, my head’s lost now!”

<!-- B11-C04-P008 -->
He sat on a bench and made Alyosha sit beside him.

<!-- B11-C04-P009 -->
“Yes, the trial’s tomorrow. Do you really have no hope at all, brother?” Alyosha asked timidly.

<!-- B11-C04-P010 -->
“What do you mean?” Mitya looked at him vaguely. “Oh, you mean the trial! Well, damn it! All this time we’ve been talking about trifles, always this trial, and I’ve said nothing to you about what matters most. Yes, the trial’s tomorrow, but I wasn’t talking about the trial when I said my head was lost. My head isn’t lost; what was inside it is lost. Why are you looking at me with such a critical expression?”

<!-- B11-C04-P011 -->
“What are you talking about, Mitya?”

<!-- B11-C04-P012 -->
“Ideas, ideas, that’s what. Ethicks. What are ethicks?”

<!-- B11-C04-P013 -->
“Ethicks?” Alyosha asked in surprise.

<!-- B11-C04-P014 -->
“Yes, is it some sort of science?”

<!-- B11-C04-P015 -->
“Yes, there is such a science… only… I confess I can’t explain to you what sort of science.”

<!-- B11-C04-P016 -->
“Rakitin knows. Rakitin knows a great deal, damn him! He isn’t going to become a monk. He’s planning to go to Petersburg. Into the criticism section, he says, but with a noble social purpose. Well, he may do some good and make a career for himself. Oh, they’re masters at making careers! To hell with ethicks! I’m lost, Alexei, I’m lost, you man of God! I love you more than anyone. My heart shakes when I look at you, that’s what. Who was that Karl Bernard?”

<!-- B11-C04-P017 -->
“Karl Bernard?” Alyosha asked, surprised again.

<!-- B11-C04-P018 -->
“No, not Karl, wait, I got it wrong: Claude Bernard.[^b11-c04-n001] What’s that? Chemistry?”

<!-- B11-C04-P019 -->
“He must be a scientist,” Alyosha answered, “only I confess I can’t tell you much about him either. I’ve only heard he’s a scientist; I don’t know what kind.”

<!-- B11-C04-P020 -->
“Well, damn him, I don’t know either,” Mitya swore. “Some scoundrel, most likely; they’re all scoundrels. But Rakitin will squeeze his way through, Rakitin can squeeze through any crack—another Bernard. Oh, these Bernards! How they’ve multiplied!”

<!-- B11-C04-P021 -->
“What is the matter with you?” Alyosha asked insistently.

<!-- B11-C04-P022 -->
“He wants to write an article about me, about my case, and launch his literary career with it. That’s why he comes; he told me himself. He wants something with a social purpose: ‘He couldn’t help killing him, he was eaten up by his environment,’ and so on—that’s what he explained to me. It’ll have a tinge of socialism, he says. Well, damn him, a tinge it is; I don’t care. He doesn’t like our brother Ivan, he hates him, and he’s not fond of you either. But I don’t turn him away, because he’s a clever man. He does give himself airs, though. Just now I told him: ‘The Karamazovs aren’t scoundrels, they’re philosophers, because all real Russian people are philosophers; but you, for all your education, aren’t a philosopher, you’re a boor.’ He laughed, spitefully. So I said: ‘*De thoughtibus non est disputandum*’—there’s no disputing thoughts. Good joke, eh? At least I’ve joined the classical camp too!” Mitya suddenly laughed.

<!-- B11-C04-P023 -->
“Why are you lost? You said so just now,” Alyosha interrupted.

<!-- B11-C04-P024 -->
“Why am I lost? Hmm! Essentially… if you take the whole thing—I feel sorry for God, that’s why!”

<!-- B11-C04-P025 -->
“What do you mean, sorry for God?”

<!-- B11-C04-P026 -->
“Imagine: there in the nerves, in the head, that is, in the brain, those nerves—oh, damn them!—there are these little tails, these nerves have little tails, and as soon as they tremble there… that is, you see, I look at something with my eyes, like this, and they tremble, those little tails… and when they tremble, an image appears, and it doesn’t appear straightaway, but some instant, a second goes by, and then a sort of moment appears—that is, not a moment, damn the moment—but an image, that is, an object or an event, well, damn it all—that’s why I perceive, and then think… because of the little tails, not at all because I have a soul and am some sort of image and likeness; that’s all nonsense. Mikhail only explained this to me yesterday, brother, and it was as if I’d been burned. This science is magnificent, Alyosha! A new man will emerge, I understand that… And still I feel sorry for God!”

<!-- B11-C04-P027 -->
“Well, that’s something good at least,” Alyosha said.

<!-- B11-C04-P028 -->
“Feeling sorry for God? Chemistry, brother, chemistry! Nothing to be done, Your Reverence, move over a little, chemistry’s coming through! Rakitin doesn’t love God, oh, he doesn’t! That’s the sorest spot with all of them. But they hide it. They lie. They pretend. ‘So are you going to push that line in the criticism section?’ I ask. ‘Well, they won’t let me do it openly,’ he says, laughing. ‘But what happens to man after that?’ I ask. ‘Without God and without a future life? Does that mean everything’s permitted now, you can do anything?’ ‘Didn’t you know?’ he says. Laughing. ‘A clever man can do anything,’ he says. ‘A clever man knows how to fish for crayfish, whereas you went and killed someone and got caught, and now you’re rotting in prison!’ That’s what he says to me. A born swine! I used to throw fellows like that out, but now I listen. He does say a lot of sensible things. Writes cleverly too. A week or so ago he started reading me an article of his, and I deliberately copied out three lines. Wait, here they are.”

<!-- B11-C04-P029 -->
Mitya hurriedly took a piece of paper from his waistcoat pocket and read:

<!-- B11-C04-P030 -->
“‘To resolve this question, one must first of all set one’s personality at variance with one’s reality.’ Do you understand that or not?”

<!-- B11-C04-P031 -->
“No, I don’t,” Alyosha said.

<!-- B11-C04-P032 -->
He was looking at Mitya with curiosity and listening to him.

<!-- B11-C04-P033 -->
“Neither do I. Obscure and unclear, but clever. ‘Everyone writes like that now,’ he says, ‘because that’s the environment’… They’re afraid of the environment. He writes verse too, the scoundrel, he’s sung the praises of Khokhlakov’s little foot, ha, ha, ha!”

<!-- B11-C04-P034 -->
“I heard,” Alyosha said.

<!-- B11-C04-P035 -->
“You heard? Did you hear the verses?”

<!-- B11-C04-P036 -->
“No.”

<!-- B11-C04-P037 -->
“I’ve got them here; I’ll read them. You don’t know, I haven’t told you—there’s a whole story. The rogue! Three weeks ago he took it into his head to tease me. ‘You’ve landed in it like a fool over three thousand,’ he says, ‘whereas I’m going to grab a hundred and fifty thousand, marry a certain widow, and buy a stone house in Petersburg.’ And he told me he was courting Khokhlakov, who hadn’t been clever even when she was young and had lost her wits altogether at forty. ‘But she’s terribly sentimental,’ he says, ‘and that’s how I’ll finish her off. I’ll marry her, take her to Petersburg, and start publishing a newspaper.’ And there was such foul, lustful drool on his lips—not for Khokhlakov, but for that hundred and fifty thousand. He convinced me, he really did; he kept coming every day: ‘She’s giving in,’ he’d say. He was radiant with joy. Then suddenly they turned him out: Pyotr Ilyich Perkhotin got the upper hand, good for him! I could have covered that foolish woman with kisses for sending him packing! It was while he was coming here that he composed these little verses. ‘For the first time,’ he says, ‘I’m dirtying my hands by writing poetry—for seduction, that is, for a useful cause. Once I’ve got the money out of that fool, I can put it to public use.’ They have a civic justification for every filthy thing! ‘And still,’ he says, ‘I’ve written better than your Pushkin, because I’ve managed to slip civic sorrow into a comic little poem.’ I understand that part about Pushkin. What if the man really did have talent, but spent it describing little feet! But how proud he was of his verses! Their vanity, their vanity! ‘On the Recovery of My Beloved’s Sore Little Foot’—that’s the title he dreamed up. A playful fellow!

> Oh, what a little foot is this,  
> A foot a little swollen!  
> Doctors come and tend to it,  
> Bind it up and maul it.  
> It’s not for little feet I pine—  
> Let Pushkin sing their praises:  
> I pine for that dear little head  
> That cannot grasp ideas.  
> It had begun to grasp a few,  
> But then the foot got in the way!  
> So let the foot grow well again,  
> That the head may understand.

“A swine, an utter swine, but the villain’s made it playful! And he really has slipped in the ‘civic’ bit. How furious he was when they turned him out. He ground his teeth!”

<!-- B11-C04-P038 -->
“He’s already taken revenge,” Alyosha said. “He’s written a newspaper report about Khokhlakov.”

<!-- B11-C04-P039 -->
And Alyosha quickly told him about the report in *Rumors*.

<!-- B11-C04-P040 -->
“It was him, him!” Mitya confirmed with a frown. “It was him! Those reports… I know about them… I mean, how much filth they’ve already written, about Grusha, for instance!… And about the other one too, Katya… Hmm!”

<!-- B11-C04-P041 -->
He paced the room uneasily.

<!-- B11-C04-P042 -->
“Brother, I can’t stay long,” Alyosha said after a silence. “Tomorrow is a terrible, momentous day for you: God’s judgment will be passed on you… and I’m astonished to find you walking about talking of God knows what instead of your case.”

<!-- B11-C04-P043 -->
“No, don’t be astonished,” Mitya interrupted heatedly. “What am I supposed to talk about—that stinking dog? The murderer? We’ve talked about that enough. I don’t want to hear any more about that stinker, the son of Stinking Lizaveta! God will strike him dead, you’ll see. Be quiet!”

<!-- B11-C04-P044 -->
He came up to Alyosha in agitation and suddenly kissed him. His eyes lit up.

<!-- B11-C04-P045 -->
“Rakitin won’t understand this,” he began, seeming wholly carried away by a kind of rapture, “but you, you’ll understand everything. That’s why I was thirsting for you. You see, for a long time I’ve wanted to tell you so much here, within these peeling walls, but I kept silent about the chief thing: somehow the time still hadn’t come. Now I’ve waited till the last possible moment to pour out my soul to you. Brother, in these past two months I’ve felt a new man in myself; a new man has risen within me! He was shut up inside me, but he’d never have appeared without this thunderbolt. Terrible! And what does it matter to me if I spend twenty years in the mines hammering out ore? I’m not afraid of that at all. It’s something else that frightens me now: that this risen man may leave me! Even there, in the mines, underground, you can find a human heart beside you in another convict and murderer like yourself, and draw close to him, because even there you can live and love and suffer! You can revive and resurrect the frozen heart in that convict, you can tend it for years and finally bring out of that den into the light an exalted soul, a consciousness that has suffered, revive an angel, resurrect a hero! And there are so many of them, hundreds, and we are all guilty for them! Why did I dream of the ‘babe’ at such a moment? ‘Why is the babe poor?’ It was a prophecy for me in that moment! I’ll go for the ‘babe.’ Because everyone is guilty for everyone else. For all the ‘babes,’ because there are little children and big children. Everyone is a ‘babe.’ I’ll go for everyone, because someone has to go for everyone. I didn’t kill my father, but I have to go. I accept it! All this came to me here… within these peeling walls. And there are so many of them, hundreds down there, underground, with hammers in their hands. Oh yes, we’ll be in chains and have no freedom, but then, in our great sorrow, we’ll rise again to joy, without which a man cannot live, nor God exist, for God gives joy, that is his privilege, his great… Lord, let man dissolve in prayer! How shall I be underground without God? Rakitin lies: if they drive God from the earth, we’ll meet him underground! A convict cannot be without God, it’s even more impossible for him than for a man who isn’t a convict! And then we men underground will sing from the bowels of the earth a tragic hymn to God, who possesses joy! Long live God and his joy! I love him!”

<!-- B11-C04-P046 -->
As he spoke this wild speech, Mitya was almost breathless. He had turned pale, his lips quivered, and tears rolled from his eyes.

<!-- B11-C04-P047 -->
“No, life is full, there is life even underground!” he began again. “You won’t believe, Alexei, how I want to live now, what a thirst to exist and be conscious has been born in me within these very peeling walls! Rakitin doesn’t understand; all he wants is to build a house and take in tenants, but I’ve been waiting for you. And what is suffering? I’m not afraid of it, even if it’s beyond counting. I’m not afraid now; I used to be. You know, perhaps I won’t even answer at the trial… And I feel as though there’s so much strength in me now that I’ll overcome everything, every suffering, just to say and keep saying to myself every moment: I am! In a thousand torments—I am; I writhe under torture—but I am! I’m shut in a tower, but I too exist, I see the sun, and if I don’t see the sun, I know it is there. And knowing the sun is there—that is the whole of life. Alyosha, my cherub, all these philosophies are killing me, damn them! Brother Ivan…”

<!-- B11-C04-P048 -->
“What about brother Ivan?” Alyosha tried to interrupt, but Mitya did not hear.

<!-- B11-C04-P049 -->
“You see, I never had any of these doubts before, but they were all hidden inside me. Perhaps it was precisely because ideas I didn’t understand were raging within me that I drank and fought and ran wild. I fought to soothe them inside me, to subdue them, crush them down. Brother Ivan isn’t Rakitin; he keeps an idea hidden. Brother Ivan is a sphinx, and he keeps silent, always silent. But God torments me. That alone torments me. What if he doesn’t exist? What if Rakitin is right, and this is an idea humanity has made up? Then, if he doesn’t exist, man is chief of the earth, of the universe. Magnificent! Only how will he be virtuous without God? That’s the question! It’s all I think about. For whom will he love then, this man? To whom will he be grateful, to whom will he sing his hymn? Rakitin laughs. Rakitin says you can love humanity without God too. Well, only that sniveling little fungus could claim such a thing; I can’t understand it. Life’s easy for Rakitin: ‘You’d do better,’ he told me today, ‘to work for the extension of human civil rights, or at least to keep the price of beef from going up. That’s a simpler, more direct way to show your love for humanity than philosophizing.’ So I let him have it: ‘Without God,’ I said, ‘you’d raise the price of beef yourself if you got the chance, and make a ruble out of a kopeck.’ He got angry. For what is virtue? Answer me, Alexei. I have one sort of virtue and a Chinese man another, so it’s a relative thing. Or isn’t it? Isn’t it relative? A tricky question! You won’t laugh if I tell you I haven’t slept for two nights because of it. What amazes me now is how people out there live without ever thinking about it. Vanity! Ivan has no God. He has an idea. Beyond my measure. But he keeps silent. I think he’s a Freemason. I asked him—silence. I wanted to drink a little water from his spring—silence. Only once did he say one little thing.”

<!-- B11-C04-P050 -->
“What did he say?” Alyosha took him up quickly.

<!-- B11-C04-P051 -->
“I said to him: ‘So everything is permitted, if that’s how it is?’ He frowned: ‘Fyodor Pavlovich, our papa, was a piglet,’ he said, ‘but he thought correctly.’ That’s what he came out with. That’s all he said. It’s worse than Rakitin.”

<!-- B11-C04-P052 -->
“Yes,” Alyosha agreed bitterly. “When was he here?”

<!-- B11-C04-P053 -->
“That can wait; something else now. I’ve said almost nothing to you about Ivan up to now. I was putting it off till the end. When this business of mine here is over and they’ve pronounced the sentence, I’ll tell you something, I’ll tell you everything. There’s something terrible here… And you’ll be my judge in it. But don’t start on it now; silence for now. You talk about tomorrow, about the trial, but would you believe it, I know nothing.”

<!-- B11-C04-P054 -->
“Have you spoken to this lawyer?”

<!-- B11-C04-P055 -->
“The lawyer! I’ve told him everything. A smooth rogue from the capital. A Bernard! Only he doesn’t believe me one broken penny’s worth. He believes I killed him, imagine—I can see it. ‘Why have you come to defend me, in that case?’ I ask. To hell with them. They’ve sent for a doctor too, want to make me out to be mad. I won’t allow it! Katerina Ivanovna wants to do ‘her duty’ to the end. Straining every nerve!” Mitya smiled bitterly. “A cat! A cruel heart! And she knows what I said about her at Mokroye, that she’s a woman of ‘great wrath.’ Someone told her. Yes, the testimonies have multiplied like the sand of the sea! Grigory stands his ground. Grigory’s honest, but a fool. A lot of people are honest because they’re fools. That’s Rakitin’s thought. Grigory’s my enemy. Some people are more useful among your enemies than your friends. I mean Katerina Ivanovna. I’m afraid, oh, I’m afraid she’ll tell them at the trial about her bowing to the ground after that four thousand five hundred! She’ll repay it to the end, to the last farthing. I don’t want her sacrifice! They’ll shame me in court! Somehow I’ll endure it. Go to her, Alyosha, ask her not to mention it at the trial. Or can’t you? Oh, damn it, it makes no difference, I’ll endure it! And I don’t pity her. She wants it herself. The thief deserves his punishment. I’ll make my own speech, Alexei.” Again he smiled bitterly. “Only… only Grusha, Grusha, Lord! Why should she take such suffering on herself now!” he suddenly cried, with tears. “Grusha is killing me, the thought of her is killing me, killing me! She was here earlier…”

<!-- B11-C04-P056 -->
“She told me. She was very upset with you today.”

<!-- B11-C04-P057 -->
“I know. Damn my temper. I got jealous! By the time she left I was sorry; I kissed her. Didn’t ask forgiveness.”

<!-- B11-C04-P058 -->
“Why didn’t you?” Alyosha exclaimed.

<!-- B11-C04-P059 -->
Mitya suddenly laughed almost cheerfully.

<!-- B11-C04-P060 -->
“God preserve you, dear boy, from ever asking the woman you love to forgive you when you’ve wronged her! Especially the woman you love, especially, however guilty you are! Because a woman, brother, is the devil knows what—and women, at least, I do understand! Just try admitting you’re at fault: ‘I was wrong, forgive me, pardon me.’ That’s when the reproaches start hailing down! She’ll never forgive you outright and simply, but will humble you till you’re a rag, rake up things that never even happened, take everything into account, forget nothing, add something of her own, and only then forgive you. And that’s the best of them, the very best! She’ll scrape up the last scrapings and heap them all on your head—there’s such a flaying cruelty in them, I tell you, every last one, these angels we can’t live without! You see, my dear, I’ll say it frankly and simply: every decent man ought to be under some woman’s thumb. That’s my conviction; not a conviction, a feeling. A man ought to be magnanimous, and it won’t disgrace him. It won’t disgrace even a hero, it won’t disgrace Caesar! But don’t ask forgiveness all the same, never, not for anything. Remember the rule: your brother Mitya taught it to you, a man ruined by women. No, I’d rather do something to earn Grusha’s favor without asking forgiveness. I revere her, Alexei, revere her! Only she doesn’t see it; no, there’s never enough love for her. And she torments me, torments me with love. What was it before! Before, only her infernal curves tormented me, but now I’ve taken her whole soul into my soul, and through her I’ve become a human being myself! Will they let us marry? Otherwise I’ll die of jealousy. Every day there’s something I dream up… What did she say to you about me?”

<!-- B11-C04-P061 -->
Alyosha repeated everything Grushenka had said earlier. Mitya listened to it in detail, asked many questions, and was pleased.

<!-- B11-C04-P062 -->
“So she isn’t angry that I’m jealous,” he exclaimed. “A true woman! ‘I have a cruel heart myself.’ Oh, I love them like that, the cruel ones, though I can’t stand it when they’re jealous of me, can’t stand it! We’ll fight. But love her—I’ll love her without end. Will they let us marry? Do they marry convicts? That’s a question. And I can’t live without her…”

<!-- B11-C04-P063 -->
Frowning, Mitya paced the room. It was growing almost dark inside. Suddenly he became terribly troubled.

<!-- B11-C04-P064 -->
“So she says a secret, a secret? Says the three of us are plotting against her, with ‘Katka’ mixed up in it? No, my dear Grushenka, it’s not that. You’ve gone wrong there, made a silly woman’s mistake of your own! Alyosha, my dear, oh, come what may! I’ll tell you our secret!”

<!-- B11-C04-P065 -->
He looked around in every direction, quickly came right up to Alyosha, who stood before him, and began whispering with a mysterious air, although in fact no one could hear them: the old keeper was dozing on a bench in the corner, and not a word reached the soldiers on guard.

<!-- B11-C04-P066 -->
“I’ll tell you our whole secret!” Mitya whispered hurriedly. “I meant to tell you later, because how could I decide anything without you? You’re everything to me. Though I say Ivan is above us, you’re my cherub. Only your decision will decide it. Perhaps you’re the higher man, not Ivan. You see, it’s a matter of conscience, of the highest conscience—a secret so important I can’t manage it myself, so I put everything off till I could come to you. But even now it’s too early to decide, because we must wait for the sentence: once it’s pronounced, then you’ll decide my fate. Don’t decide now. I’ll tell you now, you’ll hear it, but don’t decide. Stand still and say nothing. I won’t tell you everything. Just the idea, without the details, and you keep silent. Not a question, not a movement, agreed? Though, Lord, what am I to do with your eyes? I’m afraid your eyes will give the decision even if you say nothing. Oh, I’m afraid! Alyosha, listen: brother Ivan is proposing that I escape. I won’t give the details: everything’s been anticipated, it can all be arranged. Be quiet, don’t decide. To America, with Grusha. I can’t live without Grusha! What if they won’t let her join me there? Do they marry convicts? Brother Ivan says no. And without Grusha, what am I underground with a hammer? I’ll only smash my own skull with that hammer! But on the other hand, what about conscience? I’ll have run away from suffering! There was a sign—I rejected the sign; there was a path to purification—I turned left about. Ivan says that in America, ‘with good inclinations,’ one can do more good than underground. But where will our underground hymn be sung? What is America? America’s vanity again! And I think there’s a great deal of swindling in America too. I’ll have run away from crucifixion! I’m telling you this, Alexei, because only you can understand, no one else. To the others it’s nonsense, delirium, everything I told you about the hymn. They’ll say I’ve gone mad, or I’m a fool. But I haven’t gone mad, and I’m not a fool. Ivan understands about the hymn too, oh, he understands, only he doesn’t answer that part, he keeps silent. He doesn’t believe in the hymn. Don’t speak, don’t speak: I can see the way you’re looking at me; you’ve decided already! Don’t decide, have pity on me, I can’t live without Grusha. Wait till the trial!”

<!-- B11-C04-P067 -->
Mitya finished as if in a frenzy. He held Alyosha by the shoulders with both hands and fastened his feverish, thirsting gaze on his eyes.

<!-- B11-C04-P068 -->
“Do they marry convicts?” he repeated for the third time, in a pleading voice.

<!-- B11-C04-P069 -->
Alyosha had listened with extraordinary astonishment and was deeply shaken.

<!-- B11-C04-P070 -->
“Tell me one thing,” he said. “Is Ivan pressing you hard, and who first thought of it?”

<!-- B11-C04-P071 -->
“He did, he thought of it, he’s pressing me! He hadn’t been coming to see me, and then suddenly a week ago he came and started straight in on this. He’s terribly insistent. He isn’t asking, he’s ordering. He has no doubt I’ll obey, though I laid my whole heart bare to him, as I have to you, and spoke about the hymn. He told me how he’ll arrange it, he’s gathered all the information, but that can wait. He wants it to the point of hysterics. The chief thing is money: ten thousand for your escape, he says, and twenty thousand for America, and with ten thousand we’ll arrange a splendid escape.”

<!-- B11-C04-P072 -->
“And he strictly forbade you to tell me?” Alyosha asked again.

<!-- B11-C04-P073 -->
“Strictly. No one, and above all you: on no account you! He must be afraid you’ll stand before me like my conscience. Don’t tell him I told you. Oh, don’t tell him!”

<!-- B11-C04-P074 -->
“You’re right,” Alyosha decided. “It’s impossible to decide before the court’s verdict. After the trial you’ll decide for yourself; then you’ll find the new man in yourself, and he’ll decide.”

<!-- B11-C04-P075 -->
“The new man, or a Bernard, who’ll decide it in the Ber-nard way! Because I think I’m a contemptible Bernard myself!” Mitya bared his teeth in a bitter grin.

<!-- B11-C04-P076 -->
“But is it really true, brother, that you have no hope at all of being acquitted?”

<!-- B11-C04-P077 -->
Mitya gave a convulsive shrug and shook his head.

<!-- B11-C04-P078 -->
“Alyosha, my dear, it’s time for you to go!” he suddenly said hurriedly. “The governor’s calling out in the yard; he’ll be here in a moment. We’ve stayed too late; it’s against the rules. Quickly, embrace me, kiss me, make the sign of the cross over me, my dear, bless me for tomorrow’s cross…”

<!-- B11-C04-P079 -->
They embraced and kissed.

<!-- B11-C04-P080 -->
“And Ivan,” Mitya suddenly said, “he’s proposed the escape, but he believes I killed him!”

<!-- B11-C04-P081 -->
A sad smile forced its way onto his lips.

<!-- B11-C04-P082 -->
“Did you ask him whether he believes it or not?” Alyosha asked.

<!-- B11-C04-P083 -->
“No, I didn’t. I meant to ask, but I couldn’t, I didn’t have the strength. It makes no difference; I can see it in his eyes. Well, goodbye!”

<!-- B11-C04-P084 -->
They quickly kissed once more, and Alyosha was just leaving when Mitya suddenly called him back:

<!-- B11-C04-P085 -->
“Stand in front of me, like this.”

<!-- B11-C04-P086 -->
Again he gripped Alyosha firmly by the shoulders with both hands. His face suddenly went completely pale, so strikingly that it was frightening even in the near darkness. His lips twisted; his eyes fastened on Alyosha.

<!-- B11-C04-P087 -->
“Alyosha, tell me the whole truth, as before the Lord God: do you believe I killed him, or don’t you? You, yourself, do you believe it or not? The whole truth, don’t lie!” he shouted in a frenzy.

<!-- B11-C04-P088 -->
Alyosha seemed to sway from head to foot, and he felt something sharp pass through his heart.

<!-- B11-C04-P089 -->
“Stop, what are you…” he began helplessly, as if lost.

<!-- B11-C04-P090 -->
“The whole truth, all of it, don’t lie!” Mitya repeated.

<!-- B11-C04-P091 -->
“Not for a single moment have I believed you were the murderer,” suddenly burst from Alyosha’s breast in a trembling voice, and he raised his right hand as if calling God to witness his words. Bliss instantly lit up Mitya’s whole face.

<!-- B11-C04-P092 -->
“Thank you!” he said slowly, as though releasing a breath after a faint. “Now you’ve brought me back to life… Would you believe it: till now I’ve been afraid to ask you—you, of all people, you! Well, go, go! You’ve strengthened me for tomorrow. God bless you! Go now, love Ivan!” The last words broke from Mitya.

<!-- B11-C04-P093 -->
Alyosha left in tears. The depth of Mitya’s apprehension, the depth of his mistrust even of him, Alyosha—all this suddenly revealed an abyss of inescapable grief and despair in his unfortunate brother’s soul that he had never suspected before. Deep, boundless compassion suddenly seized him and tormented him in an instant. His pierced heart ached terribly. “Love Ivan!” Mitya’s last words suddenly came back to him. And he was on his way to Ivan. Since that morning he had desperately needed to see him. Ivan tormented him no less than Mitya, and now, after this meeting with his brother, more than ever.
