# Book VIII: Mitya
## III. The Gold Mines

<!-- B08-C03-P001 -->
This was precisely the visit from Mitya that Grushenka had described to Rakitin with such fear. She was then awaiting her “express messenger” and was very glad that Mitya had not come either yesterday or today. She hoped that, God willing, he might not come before she left; and then he suddenly descended on her. What followed we know: to get him off her hands, she instantly persuaded him to escort her to Kuzma Samsonov’s, where she supposedly had to go most urgently “to count money.” Mitya escorted her there at once, and as she said goodbye at Kuzma’s gate, she made him promise to come for her between eleven and twelve and take her home again. Mitya was pleased with the arrangement too. “She’ll sit at Kuzma’s, so she won’t go to Fyodor Pavlovich… unless she’s lying,” he immediately added. But to his eye she did not seem to be lying. He was precisely the sort of jealous man who, when separated from the woman he loved, would immediately invent God knows what horrors about what was happening to her and how she was “betraying” him there. Yet when he ran back to her, shaken, crushed, now utterly convinced that she had indeed managed to betray him, at his very first glimpse of her face—this woman’s laughing, cheerful, affectionate face—he immediately revived in spirit, immediately lost every suspicion, and with joyful shame scolded himself for his jealousy. After escorting Grushenka he rushed home. Oh, there was still so much he had to get done today! But at least his heart was lighter. “Only I ought to find out from Smerdyakov as soon as possible whether anything happened there last night, whether perhaps she did come to Fyodor Pavlovich—ugh!” flashed through his head. So he had not even reached his lodgings before jealousy was stirring again in his restless heart.

<!-- B08-C03-P002 -->
Jealousy! “Othello is not jealous, he is trusting,” Pushkin observed, and that observation alone testifies to the extraordinary depth of our great poet’s mind. Othello’s soul is simply shattered, and his entire view of the world has clouded over, because his ideal has perished. But Othello will not hide, spy, peep: he is trusting. On the contrary, immense efforts were needed to lead him on, prod him, inflame him, merely to make him suspect infidelity. A truly jealous man is different. It is impossible even to imagine all the disgrace and moral degradation a jealous man can live with, without the slightest pang of conscience. And these need not all be vulgar, filthy souls. On the contrary, a man may have a noble heart and a pure, self-sacrificing love, and at the same time hide under tables, bribe the most contemptible people, and live amid the foulest filth of spying and eavesdropping. Othello could never have reconciled himself to infidelity—not that he could not have forgiven it, but could not have reconciled himself to it—though his soul is innocent and free of malice as a child’s. It is otherwise with the truly jealous man: one can scarcely imagine what some jealous men can live with, reconcile themselves to, and forgive! Jealous men are the quickest of all to forgive, and every woman knows it. A jealous man is quite capable of forgiving, extraordinarily quickly—after a terrible scene at first, of course—even an almost proven infidelity, for example, embraces and kisses he has seen with his own eyes, if he can somehow at the same time convince himself that it was “the last time” and that from that hour his rival will disappear, go to the ends of the earth, or that he himself will take her somewhere that this dreadful rival can never come again. Of course the reconciliation will last only an hour, because even if his rival really does disappear, the very next day he will invent another, a new one, and become jealous of him. And one would think: what is there in a love that needs such spying on; what is a love worth that has to be guarded so strenuously? But that is precisely what a truly jealous man will never understand; and yet, truly, there are even men with noble hearts among them. Another remarkable thing is that these very men with noble hearts, standing in some little closet, listening and spying, may understand perfectly clearly, “with their noble hearts,” the whole disgrace into which they have voluntarily plunged, yet at that moment at least, while they stand in that closet, never feel a pang of conscience. At the sight of Grushenka Mitya’s jealousy vanished, and for a moment he became trusting and noble, even despising himself for his ugly feelings. But this meant only that his love for this woman contained something far higher than he himself supposed, something beyond mere passion, beyond that “curve of the body” he had spoken of to Alyosha. Yet as soon as Grushenka disappeared, Mitya immediately began suspecting her again of every baseness and treachery of infidelity. And he felt no pangs of conscience at all.

<!-- B08-C03-P003 -->
So jealousy seethed up in him again. In any case he had to hurry. First of all he needed to find even a little money to tide him over. Yesterday’s nine rubles had almost all gone on travel, and without money, as everyone knows, you cannot take a step anywhere. But together with his new plan, he had worked out where to get a little to tide him over, back in the cart earlier. He owned a pair of good dueling pistols with ammunition, and if he had not pawned them before, it was because he loved them more than anything else he possessed. At the Capital City tavern he had long ago struck up a slight acquaintance with a young civil servant, and had somehow learned there that this well-off bachelor had a passion for weapons: he bought pistols, revolvers, daggers, hung them on his walls, showed them to acquaintances, boasted about them, and was an expert at explaining the mechanism of a revolver, how to load it, how to fire it, and so on. Without a second thought Mitya went straight to him and offered to pawn the pistols for ten rubles. The civil servant joyfully tried to persuade him to sell them outright, but Mitya refused, and the man gave him ten rubles, declaring that he would take no interest on any account. They parted as friends. Mitya was in a hurry: he rushed to the back of Fyodor Pavlovich’s place, to his gazebo, to summon Smerdyakov as quickly as possible. But thus another fact was established: only three or four hours before a certain incident of which I shall speak below, Mitya had not a kopeck and pawned his most treasured possession for ten rubles, whereas suddenly, three hours later, thousands appeared in his hands… But I am getting ahead of myself.

<!-- B08-C03-P004 -->
At Marya Kondratyevna’s—Fyodor Pavlovich’s neighbor—news of Smerdyakov’s illness awaited him, astonishing and troubling him immensely. He heard the story of the fall into the cellar, then of the fit, the doctor’s visit, and Fyodor Pavlovich’s solicitude; he also learned with curiosity that his brother Ivan Fyodorovich had already left for Moscow that morning. “He must have passed through Volovya ahead of me,” Dmitri Fyodorovich thought, but Smerdyakov worried him terribly. “What now? Who’ll keep watch, who’ll bring me word?” He eagerly began questioning the women about whether they had noticed anything the previous evening. They understood perfectly what he was trying to find out and set his mind entirely at rest: nobody had come, Ivan Fyodorovich had spent the night, “everything had been in perfect order.” Mitya thought. There was no question that he must keep watch today as well, but where: here or at Samsonov’s gate? He decided on both places, as circumstances dictated, but meanwhile, meanwhile… The fact was that this “plan” now stood before him, the new and certain plan he had devised earlier in the cart, and he could no longer put off carrying it out. Mitya decided to sacrifice an hour to it: “In an hour I’ll settle everything, find out everything, and then, then first to Samsonov’s house to ask whether Grushenka’s there, and straight back here, and here till eleven, and then back to Samsonov’s for her, to take her home.” That was what he decided.

<!-- B08-C03-P005 -->
He flew home, washed, combed his hair, brushed his clothes, dressed, and went to Madame Khokhlakov’s. Alas, that was where his “plan” lay. He had decided to borrow three thousand from this lady. The main thing was that suddenly, somehow quite suddenly, he had acquired an extraordinary conviction that she would not refuse him. It may seem surprising that, if he was so sure, he had not gone there before, to his own social circle, so to speak, instead of heading for Samsonov, a man of an alien cast, to whom he did not even know how to talk. But the fact was that in the last month he had almost entirely lost touch with Madame Khokhlakov, whom he had not known well before that either; and furthermore he knew very well that she herself could not stand him. The lady had hated him from the very beginning simply because he was Katerina Ivanovna’s fiancé, whereas for some reason she had suddenly taken it into her head that Katerina Ivanovna should drop him and marry “dear Ivan Fyodorovich, so chivalrous and cultivated, with such lovely manners.” Mitya’s manners, on the other hand, she hated. Mitya even laughed at her, and had once remarked that this lady was “as lively and forward as she was uneducated.” And earlier that morning in the cart, the brightest of thoughts had suddenly illuminated him: “If she’s so set against my marrying Katerina Ivanovna, so utterly set against it”—he knew it was almost to the point of hysteria—“why should she refuse me these three thousand now, precisely so that I can use the money to leave Katya and go away from here forever? These spoiled society ladies, once they’ve set their hearts on some whim, will spare nothing to get their way. And she’s so rich besides,” Mitya reasoned. As for the “plan” itself, it was exactly the same as before: an offer of his rights to Chermashnya, though no longer for commercial purposes, as with Samsonov yesterday, no longer enticing this lady with the prospect, held out to Samsonov, of making twice the money, six or seven thousand instead of three, but simply as an honorable guarantee for the loan. As he developed this new idea, Mitya grew ecstatic, as he always did with every enterprise, every sudden resolution. He gave himself up passionately to every new thought. Nevertheless, when he stepped onto the porch of Madame Khokhlakov’s house, he suddenly felt a chill of terror down his back: only at that second did he fully realize, with mathematical clarity, that this was his last hope, that if it snapped here there was nothing else left in the world, “except to cut someone’s throat and rob them of three thousand, and nothing else…” It was about half past seven when he rang the bell.

<!-- B08-C03-P006 -->
At first matters seemed to smile on him: he had scarcely been announced before he was received with extraordinary speed. “Just as if she’d been expecting me,” flashed through Mitya’s mind. Then, suddenly, as soon as he had been shown into the drawing room, the mistress of the house almost ran in and told him outright that she had been expecting him…

<!-- B08-C03-P007 -->
“Expecting you, expecting you! Why, I couldn’t even have imagined you would come to see me, you must admit that yourself, and yet I was expecting you. Marvel at my instinct, Dmitri Fyodorovich: all morning I was certain you would come today.”

<!-- B08-C03-P008 -->
“That really is astonishing, madam,” Mitya said, settling himself awkwardly into a chair, “but… I’ve come on an extremely important matter… the most important of important matters, for me, that is, madam, for me alone, and I’m in a hurry…”

<!-- B08-C03-P009 -->
“I know it’s on the most important business, Dmitri Fyodorovich. This isn’t a matter of presentiments, of retrograde yearnings for miracles—have you heard about the elder Zosima?—this is, this is mathematics: you could not fail to come, after everything that happened with Katerina Ivanovna, you could not, could not; it’s mathematics.”

<!-- B08-C03-P010 -->
“The realism of actual life, madam, that’s what it is! But allow me to explain…”

<!-- B08-C03-P011 -->
“Precisely, realism, Dmitri Fyodorovich. I’m all for realism now; I’ve had too sharp a lesson about miracles. Have you heard that the elder Zosima has died?”

<!-- B08-C03-P012 -->
“No, madam, this is the first I’ve heard of it,” Mitya said, a little surprised. Alyosha’s image flashed through his mind.

<!-- B08-C03-P013 -->
“Last night, and just imagine…”

<!-- B08-C03-P014 -->
“Madam,” Mitya interrupted, “all I can imagine is that I’m in the most desperate position and that if you don’t help me, everything will go under, and I’ll be the first. Forgive the vulgar expression, but I’m burning up, I’m feverish…”

<!-- B08-C03-P015 -->
“I know, I know you’re feverish, I know everything; you couldn’t be in any other state of mind, and whatever you may say, I know it all beforehand. I took your fate into consideration long ago, Dmitri Fyodorovich; I follow it and study it… Oh, believe me, I’m an experienced physician of the soul, Dmitri Fyodorovich.”

<!-- B08-C03-P016 -->
“Madam, if you’re an experienced physician, I’m an experienced patient,” Mitya forced himself to say gallantly, “and I have a presentiment that if you follow my fate so closely, you’ll help my fate even as it goes to ruin. But to do so, at last allow me to lay before you the plan with which I’ve ventured to come… and what I hope for from you… I’ve come, madam…”

<!-- B08-C03-P017 -->
“Don’t explain it, that’s secondary. As for helping, you aren’t the first person I’ve helped, Dmitri Fyodorovich. You’ve probably heard about my cousin Belmesova: her husband was being ruined, was going under, as you put it so characteristically, Dmitri Fyodorovich, and what happened? I pointed him toward horse breeding, and now he’s flourishing. Do you know anything about horse breeding, Dmitri Fyodorovich?”

<!-- B08-C03-P018 -->
“Not the slightest thing, madam—oh, madam, not the slightest!” Mitya cried with nervous impatience, even making to rise. “I only beg you, madam, to listen to me; give me just two minutes to speak freely, so I can first explain everything, the whole project I’ve come with. Besides, time matters, I’m in a terrible hurry!” Mitya cried hysterically, feeling that she was about to begin talking again and hoping to shout her down. “I’ve come in despair… in the last extremity of despair, to ask you to lend me three thousand, lend it, but against sound, the soundest collateral, madam, the soundest security! Only let me explain.”

<!-- B08-C03-P019 -->
“All that later, later!” Madame Khokhlakov in her turn waved him aside. “Besides, whatever you may say, I know it all beforehand; I’ve told you that already. You’re asking for a sum of money, you need three thousand, but I’ll give you more, immeasurably more, I’ll save you, Dmitri Fyodorovich, only you must listen to me!”

<!-- B08-C03-P020 -->
Mitya fairly leaped up from his seat again.

<!-- B08-C03-P021 -->
“Madam, can you be so kind!” he exclaimed with extraordinary feeling. “My God, you’ve saved me. You’re saving a man, madam, from a violent death, from a pistol… My everlasting gratitude…”

<!-- B08-C03-P022 -->
“I’ll give you infinitely, infinitely more than three thousand!” cried Madame Khokhlakov, looking at Mitya’s rapture with a radiant smile.

<!-- B08-C03-P023 -->
“Infinitely? But I don’t need that much. All I need is these three thousand that decide my fate, and for my part I’ve come to guarantee that sum with infinite gratitude, and offer you a plan which…”

<!-- B08-C03-P024 -->
“Enough, Dmitri Fyodorovich, said and done,” Madame Khokhlakov cut in with the chaste triumph of a benefactress. “I promised to save you, and I shall. I’ll save you as I saved Belmesova’s husband. What do you think of gold mines, Dmitri Fyodorovich?”

<!-- B08-C03-P025 -->
“Gold mines, madam! I’ve never thought anything about them.”

<!-- B08-C03-P026 -->
“But I’ve thought about them for you! Thought and thought them over! I’ve been watching you for a whole month with this in mind. A hundred times I’ve looked at you as you passed and said to myself: there’s an energetic man who ought to go to the gold mines. I’ve even studied your walk and decided: this man will find a great many gold mines.”

<!-- B08-C03-P027 -->
“From my walk, madam?” Mitya smiled.

<!-- B08-C03-P028 -->
“Why, yes, from your walk as well. Really, do you deny that one can tell character from a person’s walk, Dmitri Fyodorovich? The natural sciences confirm exactly the same thing. Oh, I’m a realist now, Dmitri Fyodorovich. From today, after that whole affair at the monastery which upset me so much, I’m a complete realist, and I want to throw myself into practical activity. I’m cured. Enough![^b08-c03-n001] As Turgenev said.”

<!-- B08-C03-P029 -->
“But madam, those three thousand you so generously promised to lend me…”

<!-- B08-C03-P030 -->
“You shall have them, Dmitri Fyodorovich,” Madame Khokhlakov immediately cut in. “Those three thousand are as good as in your pocket, and not three thousand but three million, Dmitri Fyodorovich, in the shortest possible time! I’ll tell you what your idea is: you’ll discover gold mines, make millions, return, and become a man of action; you’ll move us forward too, guiding us toward the good. Surely we can’t leave everything to the Yids? You’ll put up buildings and start all sorts of enterprises. You’ll help the poor, and they’ll bless you. This is the age of railroads, Dmitri Fyodorovich. You’ll become known and indispensable to the Ministry of Finance, which is in such need just now. The decline of our paper ruble keeps me awake at night, Dmitri Fyodorovich; people know so little of that side of me…”

<!-- B08-C03-P031 -->
“Madam, madam!” Dmitri Fyodorovich interrupted again with a kind of uneasy foreboding. “I may very, very well follow your advice, your wise advice, madam, and perhaps go there… to those gold mines… and I’ll come back to talk to you about it… many times, even… but now those three thousand you so generously… Oh, they would free my hands, and if it were possible today… That is, you see, I haven’t an hour now, not an hour to spare…”

<!-- B08-C03-P032 -->
“Enough, Dmitri Fyodorovich, enough!” Madame Khokhlakov interrupted insistently. “The question is: are you going to the gold mines or not? Have you made up your mind completely? Answer mathematically.”

<!-- B08-C03-P033 -->
“I’m going, madam, later… I’ll go wherever you like, madam, but now…”

<!-- B08-C03-P034 -->
“Just wait!” Madame Khokhlakov cried, jumped up, and rushed to her magnificent bureau with its countless little drawers. She began pulling out one drawer after another, searching for something in a terrible hurry.

<!-- B08-C03-P035 -->
“Three thousand!” Mitya thought, his heart standing still. “And straightaway, without any papers, without a deed… oh, that’s gentlemanly! A magnificent woman, if only she weren’t so talkative…”

<!-- B08-C03-P036 -->
“Here!” Madame Khokhlakov cried joyfully, returning to Mitya. “Here’s what I was looking for!”

<!-- B08-C03-P037 -->
It was a tiny silver icon on a cord, of the kind sometimes worn together with the cross around one’s neck.

<!-- B08-C03-P038 -->
“It’s from Kiev, Dmitri Fyodorovich,” she continued reverently, “from the relics of Barbara the Great Martyr. Allow me to put it around your neck myself, and so bless you for a new life and new achievements.”

<!-- B08-C03-P039 -->
And she really did slip the icon around his neck and began trying to tuck it in. Much embarrassed, Mitya bent down a little and began helping her, finally getting the icon past his tie and shirt collar onto his chest.

<!-- B08-C03-P040 -->
“Now you can go!” Madame Khokhlakov pronounced, solemnly seating herself again.

<!-- B08-C03-P041 -->
“Madam, I’m so touched… and don’t even know how to thank you for such feelings, but… if you only knew how precious my time is now!.. This sum I hope to receive from your generosity… Oh, madam, if you are so kind, so touchingly generous to me,” Mitya suddenly exclaimed in a burst of inspiration, “then allow me to reveal… what, of course, you’ve long known… that I love someone here… I’ve betrayed Katya… Katerina Ivanovna, I mean. Oh, I’ve treated her inhumanly and dishonorably, but I’ve fallen in love with someone else here… a woman, madam, whom perhaps you despise, because you know everything already, but whom I cannot possibly leave, cannot possibly, and so now these three thousand…”

<!-- B08-C03-P042 -->
“Leave it all behind, Dmitri Fyodorovich!” Madame Khokhlakov interrupted in the most decisive tone. “Leave it all, and especially women. Your goal is the gold mines; there’s no need to take women there. Later, when you return rich and famous, you’ll find a companion for your heart in the very highest society. She’ll be a modern girl, knowledgeable and free of prejudice. By then the woman question, just beginning now, will have come to maturity, and the new woman will have appeared…”

<!-- B08-C03-P043 -->
“Madam, that isn’t it, that isn’t it…” Dmitri Fyodorovich began to clasp his hands in supplication.

<!-- B08-C03-P044 -->
“It’s exactly it, Dmitri Fyodorovich, exactly what you need, what you thirst for without knowing it yourself. I am by no means opposed to the woman question of today, Dmitri Fyodorovich. Women’s development, and even a political role for women in the very near future—that is my ideal. I have a daughter myself, Dmitri Fyodorovich, and people know so little of that side of me. I wrote to the writer Shchedrin on the subject. That writer showed me so much, so much about women’s calling, that last year I sent him an anonymous two-line letter: ‘I embrace and kiss you, my writer, for the modern woman. Go on.’ And I signed it ‘a mother.’ I was going to sign ‘a modern mother’ and hesitated, but settled on simply ‘a mother’: greater moral beauty, Dmitri Fyodorovich, and besides, the word ‘modern’ would have reminded them of *The Contemporary*,[^b08-c03-n002] a bitter memory for them, considering the present censorship… Oh, my God, what’s the matter with you?”

<!-- B08-C03-P045 -->
“Madam,” Mitya at last jumped up, pressing his palms together before her in helpless entreaty, “you’ll make me cry, madam, if you keep putting off what you so generously…”

<!-- B08-C03-P046 -->
“Then cry, Dmitri Fyodorovich, cry! Those are beautiful feelings… you have such a journey before you! Tears will ease you, then you’ll return and rejoice. You’ll come galloping back from Siberia expressly to rejoice with me…”

<!-- B08-C03-P047 -->
“But give me a chance too,” Mitya suddenly howled. “For the last time I beg you, tell me, can I receive this sum you promised today? And if not, when exactly should I come for it?”

<!-- B08-C03-P048 -->
“What sum, Dmitri Fyodorovich?”

<!-- B08-C03-P049 -->
“The three thousand you promised… that you so generously…”

<!-- B08-C03-P050 -->
“Three thousand? Rubles, you mean? Oh no, I haven’t got three thousand,” Madame Khokhlakov said with a kind of calm surprise. Mitya was dumbfounded…

<!-- B08-C03-P051 -->
“But how… just now… you said… you even used the expression that it was as good as in my pocket…”

<!-- B08-C03-P052 -->
“Oh no, you misunderstood me, Dmitri Fyodorovich. If that’s what you think, you didn’t understand me. I was speaking about the gold mines… It’s true, I promised you more, infinitely more than three thousand, I remember everything now, but I meant only the gold mines.”

<!-- B08-C03-P053 -->
“And the money? The three thousand?” Dmitri Fyodorovich cried absurdly.

<!-- B08-C03-P054 -->
“Oh, if you meant money, I haven’t got any. I have no money at all just now, Dmitri Fyodorovich. I’m at war with my steward at this very moment, and the other day I borrowed five hundred rubles from Miusov myself. No, no, I have no money. And you know, Dmitri Fyodorovich, even if I had, I wouldn’t give it to you. First, I never lend to anyone. Lending money means quarreling. But to you, to you above all I wouldn’t give it: because I care for you I wouldn’t give it, to save you I wouldn’t give it, because there’s only one thing you need: gold mines, gold mines, and gold mines!..”

<!-- B08-C03-P055 -->
“Oh, the devil take—!” Mitya suddenly roared and struck the table with his fist with all his strength.

<!-- B08-C03-P056 -->
“A-ah!” Madame Khokhlakov cried in fright and flew to the other end of the drawing room.

<!-- B08-C03-P057 -->
Mitya spat and strode out of the room, out of the house, into the street, into the darkness! He walked like a madman, striking his chest, the very same place on his chest that he had struck before Alyosha two days earlier, at their last meeting in the evening darkness on the road. What this beating of his chest at that particular place meant, and what he had intended to indicate by it, was still a secret that no one in the world knew, that he had not revealed even to Alyosha then; but for him the secret held more than disgrace: it held ruin and suicide, so he had already decided, if he did not obtain those three thousand to pay Katerina Ivanovna and thereby remove from his chest, “from that place on his chest,” the disgrace he carried there, which weighed so heavily on his conscience. All this will be fully explained to the reader later; but now, after his last hope had vanished, this physically powerful man had gone only a few steps from Madame Khokhlakov’s house when he suddenly burst into tears like a little child. He walked on, wiping the tears away with his fist without knowing it. In this way he came out onto the square and suddenly felt that he had collided with something bodily. A shrill wail arose from a little old woman whom he had nearly knocked down.

<!-- B08-C03-P058 -->
“Lord, you nearly killed me! Why don’t you watch where you’re going, you scapegrace!”

<!-- B08-C03-P059 -->
“What, is it you?” Mitya cried, making out the little old woman in the darkness. It was the same old serving woman who waited on Kuzma Samsonov, whom Mitya had particularly noticed the day before.

<!-- B08-C03-P060 -->
“And who might you be, sir?” the old woman said in quite a different voice. “I can’t recognize you in the dark.”

<!-- B08-C03-P061 -->
“You live with Kuzma Kuzmich, you wait on him?”

<!-- B08-C03-P062 -->
“That’s right, sir, I just ran over to Prokhorych’s… But somehow I still can’t recognize you.”

<!-- B08-C03-P063 -->
“Tell me, mother, is Agrafena Alexandrovna at your place now?” Mitya said, beside himself with suspense. “I took her there myself earlier.”

<!-- B08-C03-P064 -->
“She was, sir. She came, sat a while, and left.”

<!-- B08-C03-P065 -->
“What? Left?” Mitya cried. “When did she leave?”

<!-- B08-C03-P066 -->
“Why, she left right then. She was with us only a minute. Told Kuzma Kuzmich a story, made him laugh, and ran off.”

<!-- B08-C03-P067 -->
“You’re lying, damn you!” Mitya howled.

<!-- B08-C03-P068 -->
“A-ah!” the little old woman cried, but Mitya was already gone: he ran as fast as he could to Morozova’s house. This was just when Grushenka had driven off to Mokroye; no more than a quarter of an hour had passed since her departure. Fenya was sitting in the kitchen with her grandmother, the cook Matryona, when the “captain” suddenly ran in. At the sight of him Fenya screamed at the top of her lungs.

<!-- B08-C03-P069 -->
“Screaming?” Mitya howled. “Where is she?” But before Fenya, numb with terror, could answer a word, he suddenly threw himself at her feet:

<!-- B08-C03-P070 -->
“Fenya, for the sake of our Lord Christ, tell me where she is!”

<!-- B08-C03-P071 -->
“Sir, I know nothing, dear Dmitri Fyodorovich, I know nothing, kill me if you like, I know nothing,” Fenya swore and called God to witness. “You yourself went out with her earlier…”

<!-- B08-C03-P072 -->
“She came back!”

<!-- B08-C03-P073 -->
“Dear sir, she hasn’t come back, I swear to God, she hasn’t!”

<!-- B08-C03-P074 -->
“You’re lying,” Mitya cried. “Your fright alone tells me where she is!..”

<!-- B08-C03-P075 -->
He rushed out. The frightened Fenya was glad she had got off so lightly, but understood very well that he had simply had no time; otherwise it might have gone badly for her. Yet as he ran out, he startled both Fenya and old Matryona with a wholly unexpected action: a brass mortar stood on the table, with a pestle in it, a small brass pestle only seven inches long. As Mitya ran out, already opening the door with one hand, with the other he suddenly snatched the pestle from the mortar in passing, thrust it into his side pocket, and was gone with it.

<!-- B08-C03-P076 -->
“Oh Lord, he means to kill someone!” Fenya cried, throwing up her hands.
