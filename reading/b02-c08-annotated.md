# Book II, Chapter VIII
## The Scandal

*Part I · Book II: An Inappropriate Gathering*

<!-- B02-C08-P001 -->
As Miusov and Ivan Fyodorovich were going in to see the abbot, a delicate process of sorts rapidly took place within Pyotr Alexandrovich, who was, after all, a genuinely decent and sensitive man: he grew ashamed of being angry. He felt that, properly speaking, his contempt for that vile Fyodor Pavlovich ought to have been so complete that he would never have lost his composure in the elder's cell and made such a fool of himself. “At any rate, the monks aren't to blame,” he suddenly decided on the abbot's steps. “And if these people are decent too—this Father Nikolai, the abbot, seems to come from the nobility himself—why shouldn't I be pleasant, gracious and polite to them? I won't argue; I'll even agree with them, win them over with my courtesy and... and... show them, in the end, that I don't belong with that Aesop, that buffoon, that Pierrot, and that I've been caught out just as badly as the rest of them...”

<!-- B02-C08-P002 -->
As for the disputed timber cutting and the fishing rights—he himself had no idea where any of it was—he decided to yield them to the monks for good, once and for all, that very day, especially since they were worth very little, and to drop all his claims against the monastery.

<!-- B02-C08-P003 -->
All these good intentions grew stronger when they entered the Father Abbot's dining room. In fact, he had no dining room as such, since his entire residence really consisted of just two rooms, though they were considerably larger and more comfortable than the elder's. Their furnishings, however, offered no particular luxury either: leather-covered mahogany furniture in the old style of the 1820s; even the floors were unpainted. Everything, though, was spotlessly clean, and there were many expensive flowers in the windows. The chief luxury at that moment was naturally the lavishly laid table—though even here, lavish was a relative term: a clean tablecloth, gleaming dishes, three kinds of beautifully baked bread, two bottles of wine, two bottles of excellent monastery mead, and a large glass pitcher of the monastery's kvass, famous throughout the neighborhood. There was no vodka at all. Rakitin later reported that five courses had been prepared for the occasion: sterlet soup with fish pies; then poached fish, prepared in some exceptionally fine and unusual way; then sturgeon cutlets, ice cream and fruit compote, and finally a little jelly rather like blancmange. Rakitin had sniffed all this out: unable to resist, he had made a special visit to the abbot's kitchen, where he also had connections. He had connections everywhere, and everywhere he managed to get inside information. His was a very restless and envious heart. He was fully aware of his considerable abilities, but his self-conceit magnified them with feverish intensity. He knew for certain that he would make his mark in one way or another, but Alyosha, who was very fond of him, was troubled by the fact that his friend Rakitin was dishonest and utterly unaware of it. On the contrary, knowing he would never steal money left on a table, he firmly believed himself a man of the highest integrity. There was nothing Alyosha could do about that, nor could anyone else.

<!-- B02-C08-P004 -->
Rakitin was too insignificant a person to be invited to dinner, but Father Joseph and Father Paisius had been invited, along with another hieromonk. They were already waiting in the abbot's dining room when Pyotr Alexandrovich, Kalganov and Ivan Fyodorovich came in. The landowner Maximov was waiting off to one side as well. The Father Abbot stepped into the middle of the room to receive his guests. He was a tall, lean old man, still strong, with black hair heavily streaked with gray and a long, austere, dignified face. He bowed to the guests in silence, but this time they approached him for his blessing. Miusov even ventured to kiss his hand, but somehow the abbot drew it away in time, and the kiss never took place. Ivan Fyodorovich and Kalganov, however, received the full blessing this time—that is, with a thoroughly unselfconscious smack of the lips on his hand, in the simple manner of the common people.

<!-- B02-C08-P005 -->
“We owe you a profound apology, Your Very Reverence,” Pyotr Alexandrovich began, displaying a gracious smile while keeping his voice grave and respectful. “An apology for coming alone, without our companion, Fyodor Pavlovich, whom you also invited. He has been obliged to decline your hospitality, and not without reason. In the venerable Father Zosima's cell, carried away by his unhappy family quarrel with his son, he said several things that were entirely out of place... in short, entirely improper... of which, it seems”—he glanced at the hieromonks—“Your Very Reverence is already aware. And so, acknowledging his fault and sincerely repenting of it, he felt ashamed, and, unable to overcome that shame, asked us, myself and his son Ivan Fyodorovich, to convey to you his sincerest regret, contrition and repentance... In short, he hopes and intends to make amends for everything later, and for now, asking your blessing, he begs you to forget what happened...”

<!-- B02-C08-P006 -->
Miusov fell silent. By the final words of his speech he was so thoroughly pleased with himself that not a trace of his recent irritation remained in his soul. Once again he loved all humanity, fully and sincerely. The abbot, who had listened gravely, inclined his head a little and replied:

<!-- B02-C08-P007 -->
“I am deeply sorry our guest has left. Perhaps over our meal he might have come to love us, and we him. Please, gentlemen, join us at table.”

<!-- B02-C08-P008 -->
He stood before the icon and began the prayer aloud. Everyone bowed their heads respectfully, and the landowner Maximov even pushed himself conspicuously forward, pressing his palms together before him in an excess of reverence.

<!-- B02-C08-P009 -->
And this was when Fyodor Pavlovich played his last trick. It should be said that he really had intended to leave, and really had felt that, after his disgraceful behavior in the elder's cell, it was impossible to go to dinner with the abbot as though nothing had happened. Not that he was so very ashamed of himself or blamed himself; perhaps quite the reverse. All the same, he felt that staying for dinner would be improper. But the moment his rattling carriage was brought to the guesthouse steps, just as he was climbing into it, he suddenly stopped. He remembered his own words to the elder: “Whenever I go anywhere, I always feel that I'm more contemptible than anyone there, and that they all take me for a buffoon—so I'll play the buffoon in earnest, since every last one of you is stupider and more contemptible than I am.” He wanted to take revenge on everyone for his own foul behavior. And now, opportunely, he recalled how someone had once asked him, long ago, “Why do you hate so-and-so so much?” In a fit of his shameless buffoonery he had answered, “I'll tell you why: he never did anything to me, it's true, but I did him a thoroughly dirty turn, and the moment I'd done it I began to hate him for it.” Remembering this, he paused to think and gave a quiet, malicious chuckle. His eyes flashed, and even his lips began to tremble. “Once you've started, you might as well finish,” he suddenly decided. His most private feeling at that instant might have been put into words like these: “There's no redeeming yourself now, so I'll spit in their faces some more, till there's no shame left: I'm not ashamed of you, you see, and that's that!” He told the coachman to wait and hurried back into the monastery, straight to the abbot's. He still did not quite know what he would do, but he knew that he had lost control of himself and that the slightest push would now send him to the very limit of some vile outrage—an outrage, however, and by no means a crime or any prank for which the courts could punish him. In that respect he always knew how to restrain himself, and at times even marveled at his own ability to do so. He appeared in the abbot's dining room at the very moment the prayer ended and everyone began moving toward the table. Stopping in the doorway, he surveyed the company and gave a long, brazen, spiteful chuckle, looking boldly into every face.

<!-- B02-C08-P010 -->
“They thought I'd gone, but here I am!” he shouted across the room.

<!-- B02-C08-P011 -->
For an instant everyone stared at him in silence, and suddenly everyone felt that something disgusting and absurd was about to happen, with a scandal certain to follow. Pyotr Alexandrovich went at once from the most benevolent mood to the most ferocious. Everything that had died away and fallen quiet in his heart came back to life and rose up at once.

<!-- B02-C08-P012 -->
“No, I cannot endure this!” he cried. “I simply cannot and... I absolutely cannot!”

<!-- B02-C08-P013 -->
The blood rushed to his head. He was even losing track of his words, but this was no time to worry about phrasing, and he snatched up his hat.

<!-- B02-C08-P014 -->
“What is it he can't do?” cried Fyodor Pavlovich. “‘Simply cannot, cannot for the life of him’? Your Reverence, am I to come in or aren't I? Will you admit another guest to your table?”

<!-- B02-C08-P015 -->
“We welcome you with all our hearts,” the abbot replied. “Gentlemen! May I venture,” he suddenly added, “to ask you, from the bottom of my heart, to set aside these passing quarrels and join in love and family harmony, with a prayer to the Lord, at our humble table...”

<!-- B02-C08-P016 -->
“No, no, impossible!” cried Pyotr Alexandrovich, as though beside himself.

<!-- B02-C08-P017 -->
“Well, if it's impossible for Pyotr Alexandrovich, it's impossible for me too, and I won't stay. That's what I came to say. From now on I'll go everywhere with Pyotr Alexandrovich: if you leave, Pyotr Alexandrovich, I'll leave; if you stay, I'll stay. You touched him on the raw with that family harmony, Father Abbot: he won't acknowledge me as a relation! Isn't that so, von Sohn? And there's von Sohn himself. Good day, von Sohn.”

<!-- B02-C08-P018 -->
“Do you... mean me, sir?” murmured the astonished landowner Maximov.

<!-- B02-C08-P019 -->
“Of course I mean you!” shouted Fyodor Pavlovich. “Who else? The Father Abbot can't very well be von Sohn!”

<!-- B02-C08-P020 -->
“But I'm not von Sohn either. I'm Maximov.”

<!-- B02-C08-P021 -->
“No, you're von Sohn. Your Reverence, do you know what von Sohn is? There was a criminal trial: he was murdered in a house of fornication—that's what you call those places, I believe—murdered and robbed, and despite his venerable years they crammed him into a crate, nailed it shut and sent him from Petersburg to Moscow in a baggage car, with a number on him. And while they were nailing him up, the wanton dancing girls sang songs and played upon the psaltery—that is, the pianofrollicker. So this is that very same von Sohn. He has risen from the dead. Haven't you, von Sohn?”

<!-- B02-C08-P022 -->
“What is this? How can this be?” voices were heard among the hieromonks.

<!-- B02-C08-P023 -->
“Let's go!” Pyotr Alexandrovich cried to Kalganov.

<!-- B02-C08-P024 -->
“No, sir, allow me!” Fyodor Pavlovich broke in shrilly, taking another step into the room. “Allow me to finish too. Back in the cell they accused me of disrespectful behavior, specifically for shouting about the gudgeons. Pyotr Alexandrovich Miusov, my relation, likes there to be *plus de noblesse que de sincérité* in a man's speech; I, on the contrary, like mine to have *plus de sincérité que de noblesse*, and to hell with *noblesse*![^b02-c08-n001] Isn't that so, von Sohn? Allow me, Father Abbot: I may be a buffoon and play the buffoon, but I am a knight of honor, and I mean to speak out. Yes, sir, a knight of honor, while Pyotr Alexandrovich is nothing but wounded vanity. Perhaps I came here today precisely to have a look and speak my mind. My son Alexei is here saving his soul; I am his father, I care about his fate and it is my duty to care. All along I've been listening and acting a part, quietly watching, and now I want to perform the final act for you. How does it go with us? What falls stays down. Once it has fallen, it must lie there forever. Not so fast! I intend to get up. Holy fathers, I am outraged by you. Confession is a great sacrament, before which even I stand in awe and am ready to fall on my face, and then suddenly, there in that cell, everyone's on their knees confessing out loud. Is it permitted to confess out loud? The Holy Fathers ordained that confession should be whispered in the ear; only then is your confession a sacrament, and so it has been from ancient times. Otherwise, how am I to tell him in front of everyone that I've done this and that... well, this and that, you understand? Sometimes it's indecent even to mention it. Why, it's a scandal! No, fathers, stay here with you and a man might get drawn into the Khlysty sect...[^b02-c08-n002] At the first opportunity I'll write to the Synod, and I'll take my son Alexei home...”

<!-- B02-C08-P025 -->
A note here. Fyodor Pavlovich knew where the bells were ringing. There had once been malicious rumors, which had even reached the bishop—not only about our monastery, but about others where the institution of elders had taken root—that the elders supposedly received excessive reverence, even at the expense of the abbot's office, and that, among other things, the elders supposedly abused the sacrament of confession, and so on and so forth. Absurd accusations, which in due course collapsed of their own accord, both here and everywhere else. But the foolish devil that had seized Fyodor Pavlovich and was driving him on through his own overwrought nerves, farther and farther down into disgrace, prompted him with this old accusation, of which Fyodor Pavlovich himself understood not the first word. He could not even state it coherently, especially since on this occasion no one in the elder's cell had been on their knees or confessed aloud; Fyodor Pavlovich could therefore have seen nothing of the kind himself, and was merely repeating old rumors and gossip he had managed to recall. But once he had uttered his foolishness, he felt he had blurted out absurd nonsense, and was suddenly seized with the desire to prove to his listeners, and above all to himself, that what he had said was no nonsense at all. And though he knew perfectly well that with every word he spoke he would add more and still more absurd nonsense to what he had already said, he could no longer hold himself back and went hurtling downhill.

<!-- B02-C08-P026 -->
“How vile!” cried Pyotr Alexandrovich.

<!-- B02-C08-P027 -->
“Forgive us,” the abbot suddenly said. “It was said of old: ‘And he began to speak many things against me, even things most foul. But I, hearing all this, said within myself: behold, this is the medicine of Jesus, which He hath sent to heal my vainglorious soul.’ And so we too humbly thank you, precious guest!”

<!-- B02-C08-P028 -->
And he bowed low to Fyodor Pavlovich.

<!-- B02-C08-P029 -->
“Tut, tut, tut! Sanctimony and stale phrases! Stale phrases and stale gestures! The same old lies and regulation bowing to the ground! We know those bows! ‘A kiss on the lips and a dagger in the heart,’ as in Schiller's *The Robbers*. I don't like falsehood, fathers. I want truth! But truth is not to be found in gudgeons, and I have proclaimed it! Fathers, monks, why do you fast? Why do you expect a reward in heaven for it? Why, for a reward like that I'd start fasting too! No, holy monk, just you be virtuous in life, do some good for society, without shutting yourself up in a monastery to be fed for nothing, and without expecting a reward up there—that's a little harder. You see, Father Abbot, I too can turn a phrase. What have they cooked up here?” He approached the table. “Old Faktori port, mead bottled by the Yeliseyev brothers—well done, fathers! Doesn't look much like gudgeons. Look at the bottles the fathers have lined up, heh, heh, heh! And who brought all this here? The Russian peasant, the working man, brings you the pennies he has earned with his callused hands, taking them away from his family and the needs of the state! You're sucking the people dry, holy fathers!”

<!-- B02-C08-P030 -->
“This really is quite unworthy of you,” Father Joseph said. Father Paisius remained stubbornly silent. Miusov rushed out of the room, with Kalganov behind him.

<!-- B02-C08-P031 -->
“Well, fathers, I'm off after Pyotr Alexandrovich too! I'll never come here again, even if you beg me on your knees. I sent you a thousand rubles, and now you're looking for more, heh, heh, heh! No, you won't get another penny. I'm avenging my bygone youth, all the humiliation I've suffered!” He began thumping the table with his fist in a fit of manufactured emotion. “This little monastery has played a great part in my life! Many bitter tears I've shed because of it! You turned my wife, the *klikusha*, against me. You cursed me at seven councils and spread it all around the neighborhood! Enough, fathers, this is a liberal age, an age of steamships and railways. Not a thousand rubles, not a hundred, not a hundred kopecks—you'll get nothing from me!”

<!-- B02-C08-P032 -->
Another note. Our monastery had never played any particular part in his life, nor had he shed any bitter tears because of it. But he became so carried away by his manufactured tears that for an instant he almost believed himself; he even began to weep with emotion. At that very moment, though, he felt it was time to turn his cart around. The abbot bowed his head at this malicious lie and again said impressively:

<!-- B02-C08-P033 -->
“It is also said: ‘Bear with understanding and with joy the dishonor that cometh upon thee unbidden, and be not troubled, neither hate him who dishonoreth thee.’ So shall we act.”

<!-- B02-C08-P034 -->
“Tut, tut, tut, they did bethink themselves! And all the rest of that gibberish! Bethink away, fathers, and I'll be off. And by my paternal authority I take my son Alexei from hence forever. Ivan Fyodorovich, my most respectful son, allow me to order you to follow me! Von Sohn, what are you staying here for? Come straight to my house in town. We have a good time at my house. It's only a little verst away; instead of fasting oil I'll give you a suckling pig with porridge. We'll have dinner; I'll bring out some cognac, then a drop of liqueur; there's mamurovka... Hey, von Sohn, don't let your good fortune slip away!”

<!-- B02-C08-P035 -->
He went out shouting and gesticulating. This was the moment when Rakitin saw him leaving and pointed him out to Alyosha.

<!-- B02-C08-P036 -->
“Alexei!” his father shouted from a distance when he caught sight of him. “Move back to my house today, for good. Bring your pillow and mattress, and I don't want even the smell of you left here.”

<!-- B02-C08-P037 -->
Alyosha stopped dead, silently watching the scene with close attention. Fyodor Pavlovich meanwhile climbed into the carriage, and Ivan Fyodorovich, silent and grim, began to climb in after him, without even turning to say goodbye to Alyosha. But there was one more buffoonish, almost unbelievable scene to round off the episode. Suddenly the landowner Maximov appeared beside the carriage step. He had come running, out of breath, so as not to miss them. Rakitin and Alyosha had seen him run. He was in such a hurry that in his impatience he had already raised his foot to the step where Ivan Fyodorovich's left foot still rested, and, clutching the carriage, was starting to hop inside.

<!-- B02-C08-P038 -->
“Me too, me too, I'll come with you!” he cried, hopping up and down, giving little merry chuckles, his face blissful and ready for anything. “Take me too!”

<!-- B02-C08-P039 -->
“Didn't I tell you,” Fyodor Pavlovich shouted in delight, “that this was von Sohn! The real von Sohn, risen from the dead! But how did you get away? What von-Sohnery did you get up to in there, and how did you manage to walk out on dinner? What a brass forehead you must have! I have a forehead myself, my friend, but yours amazes me! Jump in, jump in, quick! Let him in, Vanya, it'll be fun. He can lie down at our feet somehow. Will you lie down, von Sohn? Or shall we squeeze him onto the box with the coachman? Jump up on the box, von Sohn!”

<!-- B02-C08-P040 -->
But Ivan Fyodorovich, who had already taken his seat, suddenly thrust Maximov in the chest with all his strength, without a word, sending him flying a good seven feet. It was only by chance that he did not fall.

<!-- B02-C08-P041 -->
“Drive on!” Ivan Fyodorovich shouted spitefully to the coachman.

<!-- B02-C08-P042 -->
“Now, what are you doing? What are you doing? Why did you treat him like that?” Fyodor Pavlovich burst out, but the carriage was already moving. Ivan Fyodorovich did not answer.

<!-- B02-C08-P043 -->
“Well, would you look at that!” Fyodor Pavlovich said again after two minutes' silence, glancing sideways at his dear son. “You set up this whole monastery business yourself, you egged me on, you approved of it—so what are you angry about now?”

<!-- B02-C08-P044 -->
“Stop spouting nonsense. Give it a rest, at least for now,” Ivan Fyodorovich cut him short sternly.

<!-- B02-C08-P045 -->
Fyodor Pavlovich was silent for another two minutes or so.

<!-- B02-C08-P046 -->
“A drop of cognac would be just the thing now,” he observed sententiously. But Ivan Fyodorovich did not answer.

<!-- B02-C08-P047 -->
“When we get home, you'll have some too.”

<!-- B02-C08-P048 -->
Ivan Fyodorovich remained silent.

<!-- B02-C08-P049 -->
Fyodor Pavlovich waited another two minutes or so.

<!-- B02-C08-P050 -->
“All the same, I am taking Alyoshka out of the monastery, though it will displease you very much, my most respectful Karl von Moor.”

<!-- B02-C08-P051 -->
Ivan Fyodorovich shrugged contemptuously, turned away and looked at the road. After that they said nothing all the way home.
