# Book XI: Brother Ivan Fyodorovich
## V. Not You, Not You!

<!-- B11-C05-P001 -->
On his way to Ivan’s, he had to pass the house where Katerina Ivanovna was lodging. There was light in the windows. He suddenly stopped and decided to go in. He had not seen Katerina Ivanovna for more than a week. But it now occurred to him that Ivan might be with her, especially on the eve of such a day. After ringing, he entered the staircase, dimly lit by a Chinese lantern, and saw a man coming down whom he recognized as his brother when they drew level. So Ivan was already leaving Katerina Ivanovna’s.

<!-- B11-C05-P002 -->
“Oh, it’s only you,” Ivan Fyodorovich said dryly. “Well, goodbye. Are you going to see her?”

<!-- B11-C05-P003 -->
“Yes.”

<!-- B11-C05-P004 -->
“I wouldn’t advise it. She’s ‘agitated,’ and you’ll only upset her more.”

<!-- B11-C05-P005 -->
“No, no!” a voice suddenly called from above, through a door that had flown open. “Alexei Fyodorovich, have you come from him?”

<!-- B11-C05-P006 -->
“Yes, I’ve been to see him.”

<!-- B11-C05-P007 -->
“Did he send me a message? Come in, Alyosha, and you, Ivan Fyodorovich, you must, must come back. Do—you—hear!”

<!-- B11-C05-P008 -->
Such a commanding note rang in Katya’s voice that, after hesitating a moment, Ivan Fyodorovich nevertheless decided to go back upstairs with Alyosha.

<!-- B11-C05-P009 -->
“Listening at the door!” he whispered irritably to himself, but Alyosha heard.

<!-- B11-C05-P010 -->
“Allow me to keep my coat on,” Ivan Fyodorovich said as he entered the drawing room. “I won’t sit down either. I’m staying no more than a minute.”

<!-- B11-C05-P011 -->
“Sit down, Alexei Fyodorovich,” Katerina Ivanovna said, remaining on her feet herself. She had changed little in this time, but her dark eyes flashed with an ominous fire. Alyosha later remembered that she had seemed extraordinarily beautiful to him at that moment.

<!-- B11-C05-P012 -->
“Well, what did he ask you to tell me?”

<!-- B11-C05-P013 -->
“Only one thing,” Alyosha said, looking straight into her face. “That you should spare yourself and give no evidence at the trial about…” He hesitated a little. “About what happened between you… when you first met… in that town…”

<!-- B11-C05-P014 -->
“Ah, the bow to the ground for that money!” she took him up with a bitter laugh. “Well, is he afraid for himself or for me—eh? He said I should spare—whom? Him or myself? Tell me, Alexei Fyodorovich.”

<!-- B11-C05-P015 -->
Alyosha looked at her intently, trying to understand her.

<!-- B11-C05-P016 -->
“Both yourself and him,” he said quietly.

<!-- B11-C05-P017 -->
“Exactly,” she pronounced with a kind of malice, and suddenly blushed. “You don’t know me yet, Alexei Fyodorovich,” she said threateningly, “and I don’t know myself yet either. Perhaps you’ll want to trample me underfoot after tomorrow’s questioning.”

<!-- B11-C05-P018 -->
“You’ll testify honestly,” Alyosha said. “That’s all that’s needed.”

<!-- B11-C05-P019 -->
“A woman is often dishonorable,” she said through clenched teeth. “Only an hour ago I thought I’d be afraid to touch that monster… as if he were a reptile… and yet no, he’s still a human being to me! But did he kill him? Was he the one who killed him?” she suddenly cried hysterically, turning quickly to Ivan Fyodorovich. Alyosha understood at once that she had already asked Ivan this very question, perhaps only a minute before he arrived, and not for the first time but for the hundredth, and that they had ended by quarreling.

<!-- B11-C05-P020 -->
“I went to see Smerdyakov… It was you, you who convinced me he was a parricide. I believed only you!” she went on, still addressing Ivan Fyodorovich. He smiled as if with an effort. Alyosha started at hearing her intimate “you.” He had never suspected such relations between them.

<!-- B11-C05-P021 -->
“Well, enough of this,” Ivan cut her short. “I’m going. I’ll come tomorrow.” He turned at once, left the room, and went straight to the stairs. Katerina Ivanovna suddenly seized both Alyosha’s hands with a commanding gesture.

<!-- B11-C05-P022 -->
“Go after him! Catch him! Don’t leave him alone for a moment,” she whispered rapidly. “He’s mad. Don’t you know he’s gone mad? He has a fever, a nervous fever! The doctor told me. Go, run after him…”

<!-- B11-C05-P023 -->
Alyosha sprang up and hurried after Ivan Fyodorovich. He had not gone fifty paces.

<!-- B11-C05-P024 -->
“What do you want?” Ivan suddenly turned to Alyosha, seeing him catch up. “She told you to run after me because I’m mad. I know it by heart,” he added irritably.

<!-- B11-C05-P025 -->
“Of course she’s mistaken, but she’s right that you’re ill,” Alyosha said. “I was looking at your face just now in her rooms. You look very ill, very, Ivan!”

<!-- B11-C05-P026 -->
Ivan walked on without stopping. Alyosha followed.

<!-- B11-C05-P027 -->
“Do you know how people go mad, Alexei Fyodorovich?” Ivan asked quite suddenly in a quiet voice, no longer irritable at all, in which the most innocent curiosity could suddenly be heard.

<!-- B11-C05-P028 -->
“No, I don’t. I suppose there are many different kinds of madness.”

<!-- B11-C05-P029 -->
“Can you observe yourself going mad?”

<!-- B11-C05-P030 -->
“I don’t think you can follow what’s happening to yourself clearly in such a case,” Alyosha answered in surprise. Ivan fell silent for half a minute.

<!-- B11-C05-P031 -->
“If you want to talk to me about anything, please change the subject,” he said suddenly.

<!-- B11-C05-P032 -->
“Oh, before I forget, there’s a letter for you,” Alyosha said timidly. Taking Lise’s letter from his pocket, he held it out to him. They had just come up to a streetlamp. Ivan recognized the handwriting at once.

<!-- B11-C05-P033 -->
“Ah, from that little devil!” he laughed spitefully, and without opening the envelope suddenly tore it into several pieces and threw it to the wind. The scraps scattered.

<!-- B11-C05-P034 -->
“Not even sixteen yet, I think, and already offering herself!” he said contemptuously, striding down the street again.

<!-- B11-C05-P035 -->
“What do you mean, offering herself?” Alyosha cried.

<!-- B11-C05-P036 -->
“The way loose women offer themselves, obviously.”

<!-- B11-C05-P037 -->
“What are you saying, Ivan, what are you saying?” Alyosha defended her with sorrow and heat. “She’s a child, you’re insulting a child! She’s ill, very ill herself, perhaps she’s going mad too… I couldn’t refuse to give you her letter… On the contrary, I wanted to hear something from you… to save her.”

<!-- B11-C05-P038 -->
“There’s nothing for you to hear from me. If she’s a child, I’m not her nurse. Be quiet, Alexei. Don’t go on. I’m not even thinking about it.”

<!-- B11-C05-P039 -->
They were silent again for a minute.

<!-- B11-C05-P040 -->
“Now she’ll spend the whole night praying to the Mother of God to show her how to act at the trial tomorrow,” he suddenly began again, harshly and spitefully.

<!-- B11-C05-P041 -->
“You… you mean Katerina Ivanovna?”

<!-- B11-C05-P042 -->
“Yes. Is she to appear as Mitenka’s savior or his destroyer? That’s what she’ll pray for, for light to dawn in her soul. She doesn’t know herself yet, you see, she hasn’t had time to prepare. She takes me for a nurse too, wants me to lull her to sleep!”

<!-- B11-C05-P043 -->
“Katerina Ivanovna loves you, brother,” Alyosha said sadly.

<!-- B11-C05-P044 -->
“Perhaps. Only I have no appetite for her.”

<!-- B11-C05-P045 -->
“She’s suffering. Then why do you say… sometimes… things that give her hope?” Alyosha continued with a timid reproach. “I know you’ve given her hope. Forgive me for saying so,” he added.

<!-- B11-C05-P046 -->
“I can’t do the proper thing here, break it off and tell her outright!” Ivan said irritably. “We have to wait till they pronounce sentence on the murderer. If I break with her now, she’ll destroy that scoundrel at the trial tomorrow to avenge herself on me, because she hates him and knows she hates him. It’s all lies, lies upon lies! As things stand, until I break with her, she still hopes and won’t destroy that monster, knowing how I want to get him out of trouble. When will this damned verdict finally come!”

<!-- B11-C05-P047 -->
The words “murderer” and “monster” struck painfully at Alyosha’s heart.

<!-- B11-C05-P048 -->
“But how could she destroy our brother?” he asked, thinking through Ivan’s words. “What could she testify to that would ruin Mitya outright?”

<!-- B11-C05-P049 -->
“You don’t know yet. She has a document in her hands, in Mitenka’s own handwriting, that proves mathematically he killed Fyodor Pavlovich.”

<!-- B11-C05-P050 -->
“That can’t be!” Alyosha exclaimed.

<!-- B11-C05-P051 -->
“What do you mean, it can’t? I’ve read it myself.”

<!-- B11-C05-P052 -->
“There can’t be such a document!” Alyosha repeated passionately. “There can’t be, because he isn’t the murderer. He didn’t kill Father, he didn’t!”

<!-- B11-C05-P053 -->
Ivan Fyodorovich stopped short.

<!-- B11-C05-P054 -->
“Who is the murderer, in your opinion?” he asked with apparent coldness, and even a note of arrogance sounded in the question.

<!-- B11-C05-P055 -->
“You know who it is yourself,” Alyosha said quietly and earnestly.

<!-- B11-C05-P056 -->
“Who? That fable about that mad epileptic idiot? About Smerdyakov?”

<!-- B11-C05-P057 -->
Alyosha suddenly felt himself trembling all over.

<!-- B11-C05-P058 -->
“You know who it is yourself,” escaped him helplessly. He could scarcely breathe.

<!-- B11-C05-P059 -->
“But who, who?” Ivan cried, almost savagely now. All his restraint suddenly vanished.

<!-- B11-C05-P060 -->
“I only know one thing,” Alyosha said, still almost in a whisper. “It wasn’t you who killed Father.”

<!-- B11-C05-P061 -->
“‘Not you’! What does ‘not you’ mean?” Ivan stood dumbfounded.

<!-- B11-C05-P062 -->
“You didn’t kill Father, not you!” Alyosha repeated firmly. The silence lasted half a minute.

<!-- B11-C05-P063 -->
“I know myself it wasn’t me. Are you delirious?” Ivan said with a pale, twisted smile. His eyes seemed to bore into Alyosha. They were both standing by a streetlamp again.

<!-- B11-C05-P064 -->
“No, Ivan, you’ve told yourself several times that you’re the murderer.”

<!-- B11-C05-P065 -->
“When did I say that?… I was in Moscow… When did I say it?” Ivan stammered, utterly lost.

<!-- B11-C05-P066 -->
“You’ve said it to yourself many times when you were alone during these terrible two months,” Alyosha continued, quietly and distinctly as before. But now he spoke as if beside himself, as if not of his own will, obeying some irresistible command. “You’ve accused yourself and admitted to yourself that the murderer is none other than you. But you didn’t kill him, you’re mistaken, you’re not the murderer, do you hear me, not you! God sent me to tell you this.”

<!-- B11-C05-P067 -->
Both fell silent. That silence stretched out for a whole long minute. Both stood looking into each other’s eyes. Both were pale. Suddenly Ivan began shaking all over and gripped Alyosha’s shoulder hard.

<!-- B11-C05-P068 -->
“You’ve been to my rooms!” he said in a grating whisper. “You were there at night, when he came… Admit it… You saw him, saw him?”

<!-- B11-C05-P069 -->
“Who are you talking about… Mitya?” Alyosha asked in bewilderment.

<!-- B11-C05-P070 -->
“Not him, to hell with the monster!” Ivan cried in a frenzy. “Do you know he comes to me? How did you find out? Tell me!”

<!-- B11-C05-P071 -->
“Who is he? I don’t know who you’re talking about,” Alyosha stammered, frightened now.

<!-- B11-C05-P072 -->
“No, you know… otherwise how could you… it’s impossible you don’t know…”

<!-- B11-C05-P073 -->
But suddenly he seemed to check himself. He stood there as though thinking something over. A strange smile twisted his lips.

<!-- B11-C05-P074 -->
“Brother,” Alyosha began again in a trembling voice, “I told you this because you’ll believe my word, I know you will. I’ve said it to you for your whole life: not you! Do you hear? For your whole life. And it was God who put it into my soul to tell you, even if from this hour you hate me forever…”

<!-- B11-C05-P075 -->
But Ivan Fyodorovich seemed to have regained complete control of himself.

<!-- B11-C05-P076 -->
“Alexei Fyodorovich,” he said with a cold smile, “I cannot abide prophets and epileptics, especially messengers of God; you know that only too well. From this moment I am breaking with you, and, I think, forever. I ask you to leave me this instant, at this very crossing. Your way home is down that lane, in any case. Above all, take care not to come to my rooms today! Do you hear?”

<!-- B11-C05-P077 -->
He turned and walked straight on with a firm step, without looking back.

<!-- B11-C05-P078 -->
“Brother,” Alyosha called after him, “if anything happens to you today, think of me first of all!…”

<!-- B11-C05-P079 -->
But Ivan did not answer. Alyosha stood at the crossing by the streetlamp until Ivan had disappeared completely into the darkness. Then he turned and slowly made his way home along the lane. He and Ivan Fyodorovich lived separately, in different lodgings: neither had wished to stay in Fyodor Pavlovich’s empty house. Alyosha rented a furnished room with a family of townspeople; Ivan lived quite far from him and occupied spacious, fairly comfortable quarters in a wing of a fine house belonging to the comfortably off widow of an official. But the only person to wait on him in the entire wing was one ancient, completely deaf old woman, riddled with rheumatism, who went to bed at six in the evening and rose at six in the morning. In these two months Ivan Fyodorovich had become strangely undemanding and very fond of being entirely alone. He even tidied the room he used himself, and seldom so much as entered the other rooms of his lodgings. On reaching the gate of his house, with his hand already on the bell handle, he stopped. He felt that he was still trembling all over with anger. Suddenly he let go of the bell, spat, turned back, and quickly set off again toward the other, opposite end of town, some two versts from his lodgings, to a tiny, leaning log house where Marya Kondratyevna lived, Fyodor Pavlovich’s former neighbor, who used to come to his kitchen for soup and to whom Smerdyakov had sung his songs and played the guitar. She had sold her old little house and now lived with her mother in what was almost a peasant hut; the sick, nearly dying Smerdyakov had lodged with them ever since Fyodor Pavlovich’s death. It was to him that Ivan Fyodorovich was now going, driven by one sudden and irresistible thought.
