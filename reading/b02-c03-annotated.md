# Book II, Chapter III
## Women of Faith

*Part I · Book II: An Inappropriate Gathering*

<!-- B02-C03-P001 -->
Down below, beside the wooden gallery built onto the outside of the enclosure wall, the crowd this time was all women, some twenty peasant women. They had been told that the elder would finally come out, and had gathered to wait. The Khokhlakovs, the two landowning ladies, also came out onto the gallery. They too had been waiting for the elder, but in the room reserved for women of the gentry. They were mother and daughter. Madame Khokhlakov, the mother, was wealthy and always tastefully dressed, still quite young and very pretty, a little pale, with extremely lively, almost black eyes. She was no more than thirty-three and had been a widow for some five years. Her fourteen-year-old daughter suffered from paralysis of the legs. The poor girl had been unable to walk for six months or so and was wheeled about in a long, comfortable wheeled chair. She had a lovely little face, somewhat thin from illness, but cheerful. Something mischievous shone in her large dark eyes with their long lashes. Her mother had been planning to take her abroad since the spring, but business on the estate had delayed their departure that summer. They had been staying in our town for about a week, more on business than on pilgrimage, and had already visited the elder once, three days earlier. Now they had suddenly come again, although they knew that the elder could scarcely receive anyone anymore, and pleaded insistently for one more chance to enjoy “the happiness of beholding the great healer.”

<!-- B02-C03-P002 -->
While they waited for the elder to appear, the mother sat on a chair beside her daughter's wheeled chair. Two paces away stood an old monk, not from this monastery but a visitor from a distant, little-known monastery in the north. He too wished to receive the elder's blessing. When the elder appeared on the gallery, however, he went straight to the common people first. The crowd pressed toward the little porch whose three steps joined the low gallery to the field. The elder stood on the top step, put on his priestly stole, and began blessing the women crowding around him. One *klikusha* was pulled toward him, held by both arms. The moment she caught sight of the elder, she began hiccuping, with strange, absurd squeals, and shook all over as though in convulsions. The elder laid his stole on her head and read a brief prayer over her, and at once she fell quiet and grew calm. I don't know how it is now, but in my childhood I often saw and heard these *klikushi* in villages and at monasteries. They were brought to the liturgy and would shriek or bark like dogs through the whole church; yet when the Eucharistic gifts were brought out and the women were led up to them, their “possession” stopped at once, and the sufferers invariably grew calm for a time. As a child I found this astonishing and deeply puzzling. But even then, in answer to my questions, I heard from certain landowners, and especially from my teachers in town, that it was all a pretense to avoid work and could always be stamped out by sufficient strictness; assorted anecdotes were offered as proof. Later, however, I was surprised to learn from medical specialists that there was no pretense involved at all, that this was a terrible illness among women, seemingly found chiefly here in Russia, which testified to the hard lot of our rural women. It arose from exhausting labor resumed too soon after difficult, badly managed childbirth without any medical assistance, and also from inescapable grief, beatings, and so on, which some women, though others commonly endured them, were nevertheless unable to bear. As for the strange, instantaneous healing of a possessed, convulsing woman the moment she was led up to the holy gifts, I had been told that this too was a pretense, and even a trick arranged almost by the “clericals” themselves. Probably it also came about in an entirely natural way: the women who led her up to the gifts, and above all the sufferer herself, believed as an established truth that the unclean spirit possessing her could never endure it if she were brought before the gifts and made to bow down to them. And so, at the moment she bowed before the gifts, there always occurred—and had to occur—in this nervously afflicted woman, who was of course mentally ill as well, something like a shock that inevitably passed through her whole body, caused by her expectation of an unfailing miracle of healing and her absolute faith that it would take place. And it did take place, if only for a single minute. Just so it had taken place now, as soon as the elder covered the sick woman with his stole.

<!-- B02-C03-P003 -->
Many of the women crowding around him were weeping, overcome by tenderness and rapture at the effect of that moment; others strained to kiss at least the hem of his robe, while some murmured their laments. He blessed them all and spoke with some. He already knew the *klikusha*: she had been brought from nearby, a village only six versts from the monastery, and they had brought her to him before.

<!-- B02-C03-P004 -->
“And here's one who's come a long way!” He pointed to a woman who was still far from old but very thin and worn, her face not so much tanned as blackened all over. She was on her knees, gazing fixedly at the elder. There was something almost frenzied in her eyes.

<!-- B02-C03-P005 -->
“A long way, Father, a long way, three hundred versts from here. A long way, Father, a long way,” the woman said in a singsong voice, gently rocking her head from side to side with her cheek resting on her palm. She spoke as though chanting a lament. Among the common people there is a silent, long-suffering grief: it retreats into itself and says nothing. But there is also a grief that tears its way out. Once it breaks through in tears, from that moment on it finds its outlet in lamentation. This is especially so among women. Yet it is no lighter than silent grief. Lamentation brings relief only by further tormenting and tearing at the heart. Such grief does not even want consolation; it feeds on the sense that it cannot be consoled. Lamentation is simply the need to keep chafing the wound.

<!-- B02-C03-P006 -->
“Of the townsman estate, I suppose?”[^b02-c03-n001] the elder went on, studying her with interest.

<!-- B02-C03-P007 -->
“We're town people, Father, town people. Peasants by estate, but town people—we live in town. I've come to see you, Father. We've heard of you, dear Father, we have. I buried my little boy, my baby, and went to pray to God. I've been to three monasteries, and they told me, ‘Go here too, Nastyushka’—to you, that is, dear heart, to you. So I came. Yesterday I stood through the service, and today I've come to you.”

<!-- B02-C03-P008 -->
“What are you crying for?”

<!-- B02-C03-P009 -->
“I miss my little boy, Father. He was almost three—just three months more and he’d have been three. I'm tormented for my little boy, Father, for my little boy. He was the last one left. Nikitushka and I had four, but our children don't stay with us, they don't, dear heart, they don't. I buried the first three and didn't grieve for them so very much, but I buried this last one and I can't forget him. It's as though he's standing right here in front of me, he never leaves. He's dried my soul out. I look at his little things, his shirt or his boots, and I start howling. I lay out all he's left behind, every single thing of his, and I look and howl. I said to Nikitushka, my husband, ‘Let me go on a pilgrimage, husband.’ He drives a cab. We're not poor, Father, not poor. We run our own cab, with our own horses and carriage. But what use are our belongings now? He's taken to drinking without me, my Nikitushka, I'm sure of it, and even before that, the moment my back was turned he'd give way. But now I don't think of him either. It's the third month I've been away from home. I've forgotten, forgotten everything, and I don't want to remember. What am I to do with him now? I'm done with him, done, done with them all. I never want to look at my house or my belongings again, never want to see anything at all!”

<!-- B02-C03-P010 -->
“Listen, mother,” the elder said. “Long ago, a great saint once saw a mother just like you weeping in church, also for her child, her only one, whom the Lord had also called to him. ‘Do you not know,’ the saint said to her, ‘how boldly these little children stand before the throne of God? There are none in the kingdom of heaven bolder than they. “You gave us life, Lord,” they say to God, “and scarcely had we seen it when you took it back from us.” And they petition and demand so boldly that the Lord at once grants them the rank of angels. Therefore,’ the saint said, ‘rejoice, woman, and do not weep: your child too now dwells with the Lord among his host of angels.’ That is what the saint told the weeping woman in ancient times. And he was a great saint, so he could not have told her an untruth. Know, then, mother, that your child too surely stands now before the Lord's throne, rejoicing and glad, and prays to God for you. And so you too may weep, but rejoice.”

<!-- B02-C03-P011 -->
The woman listened with her cheek resting on her hand and her eyes lowered. She gave a deep sigh.

<!-- B02-C03-P012 -->
“That's just how Nikitushka comforted me, the very same words as yours: ‘You're foolish,’ he says. ‘What are you crying for? Our little boy is surely singing now with the angels before the Lord God.’ He says that to me and cries himself, I can see it, cries just as I do. ‘I know, Nikitushka,’ I say. ‘Where else would he be but with the Lord God? Only he isn't here with us now, Nikitushka, right beside us, the way he used to sit!’ If only I could look at him just once, just one little look at him again. I wouldn't go up to him, wouldn't say a word, I'd hide in a corner, only to see him for a single minute, to hear him playing in the yard. He used to come and call in his little voice, ‘Mama, where are you?’ If only I could hear him walk across the room on his little feet once, just once, his little feet going tap-tap, so quick, so quick. I remember how he'd run to me, calling and laughing. If only I could hear his little feet, hear them, know them! But he isn't here, Father, he isn't, and I'll never hear him again! Here's his little belt, but he isn't here, and now I'll never see him, never hear him again!…”

<!-- B02-C03-P013 -->
She took her boy's little braid-trimmed belt out of the bosom of her dress, and the moment she looked at it she shook with sobs, covering her eyes with her fingers, through which the tears suddenly burst and streamed.

<!-- B02-C03-P014 -->
“And this,” said the elder, “this is Rachel of old, ‘weeping for her children and refusing to be comforted, because they are no more.’[^b02-c03-n002] Such is the lot appointed to you mothers on earth. Don't be comforted, then, you needn't be comforted; don't be comforted, and weep. Only every time you weep, be sure to remember that your little boy is one of God's angels, that he looks down on you from there and sees you, and rejoices at your tears and points them out to the Lord God. This great grief of motherhood will be yours for a long time yet, but in the end it will turn to quiet joy, and your bitter tears will become only tears of gentle tenderness and a cleansing of the heart that saves it from sin. I will pray for the repose of your child's soul. What was his name?”

<!-- B02-C03-P015 -->
“Alexei, Father.”

<!-- B02-C03-P016 -->
“A dear name. Named for Alexei the Man of God?”

<!-- B02-C03-P017 -->
“For the Man of God, Father, for the Man of God—Alexei the Man of God!”

<!-- B02-C03-P018 -->
“What a saint he is! I'll remember him, mother, I'll remember him, and remember your sorrow in my prayers, and pray for your husband's health. Only it is a sin for you to leave him. Go to your husband and care for him. Your boy will see from up there that you've abandoned his father and will weep for you both. Why disturb his blessedness? He is alive, alive, for the soul lives forever. He isn't in the house, yet he is there beside you, unseen. How can he come home if you say you've come to hate your home? Whom will he come to if he doesn't find you together, his father and mother? Now he comes to you in dreams, and you suffer, but then he will send you gentle dreams. Go to your husband, mother, go this very day.”

<!-- B02-C03-P019 -->
“I will, dear Father, at your word I will. You've read my heart. Nikitushka, my Nikitushka, you're waiting for me, dear heart, waiting!” the peasant woman began to lament. But the elder had already turned to a little old woman dressed as a townswoman, not as a pilgrim. It was clear from her eyes that she had some business with him, that she had come to tell him something. She said she was the widow of a noncommissioned officer, from nearby, from our own town. Her son Vasenka had served somewhere in the commissariat, then gone to Siberia, to Irkutsk. He had written twice from there, but now a year had passed without a letter. She had made inquiries about him, though in truth she did not know where to inquire.

<!-- B02-C03-P020 -->
“Then just the other day Stepanida Ilyinishna Bedryagina—she's a merchant's wife, a rich one—said to me, ‘Prokhorovna,’ she says, ‘put your son's name on a list for remembrance, take it to church, and have them pray for the repose of his soul. His soul will grow uneasy,’ she says, ‘and he'll write you a letter. And it's absolutely certain,’ Stepanida Ilyinishna says, ‘it's been tried many times.’ Only I have my doubts… Dear light of our lives, is it true or isn't it, and would it be right to do it?”

<!-- B02-C03-P021 -->
“Don't even think of it. It's shameful even to ask. How can a living soul be prayed for as dead, and by his own mother! It's a great sin, like witchcraft; only your ignorance makes it forgivable. You should pray instead to the Queen of Heaven, swift to intercede and help, for his health, and ask her to forgive you too for thinking so wrongly. And I'll tell you something else, Prokhorovna: either your son himself will soon come back to you, or he will certainly send you a letter. Be sure of that. Go, and from now on be at peace. Your son is alive, I tell you.”

<!-- B02-C03-P022 -->
“Dear Father, may God reward you, our benefactor, you who pray for us all and for our sins…”

<!-- B02-C03-P023 -->
But the elder had already noticed in the crowd two burning eyes straining toward him, the eyes of an exhausted peasant woman who looked consumptive, although she was still young. She watched in silence, her eyes pleading for something, but seemed afraid to come closer.

<!-- B02-C03-P024 -->
“What have you come for, my dear?”

<!-- B02-C03-P025 -->
“Absolve my soul, dear Father,” she said quietly, without haste, and knelt and bowed at his feet.

<!-- B02-C03-P026 -->
“I've sinned, dear Father, I'm afraid of my sin.” The elder sat down on the bottom step, and the woman moved closer without rising from her knees.

<!-- B02-C03-P027 -->
“I've been a widow going on three years,” she began in a half whisper, seeming to shudder as she spoke. “It was hard being married. He was old, he beat me badly. He lay there sick, and I looked at him and thought: what if he gets well, gets up again, what then? And that's when that thought came to me…”

<!-- B02-C03-P028 -->
“Wait,” said the elder, and put his ear right to her lips. The woman went on in a quiet whisper, so that almost nothing could be made out. She soon finished.

<!-- B02-C03-P029 -->
“Going on three years?” the elder asked.

<!-- B02-C03-P030 -->
“Going on three years. At first I didn't think about it, but now I've begun to be ill, and anguish has come over me.”

<!-- B02-C03-P031 -->
“Have you come far?”

<!-- B02-C03-P032 -->
“Five hundred versts from here.”

<!-- B02-C03-P033 -->
“Have you spoken of it in confession?”

<!-- B02-C03-P034 -->
“I have, I've told it twice over.”

<!-- B02-C03-P035 -->
“Were you allowed to take communion?”

<!-- B02-C03-P036 -->
“I was. I'm afraid, afraid to die.”

<!-- B02-C03-P037 -->
“Fear nothing, never be afraid, and do not be anguished. So long as repentance does not fail in you, God will forgive everything. There is no sin, and can be no sin anywhere on earth, that the Lord would not forgive a person who truly repents. Nor can a human being commit a sin so great that it would exhaust God's infinite love. Can there be a sin greater than God's love? See only to your repentance, that it never ceases, and cast out fear altogether. Believe that God loves you as you cannot even imagine, loves you even with your sin and in your sin. And there is more joy in heaven over one who repents than over ten righteous people, as was said long ago. Go, then, and do not be afraid. Do not be bitter toward people, do not be angry at the wrongs they do you. In your heart forgive the dead man every hurt he caused you; be truly reconciled with him. If you repent, you also love. And if you love, you are already God's… Everything is bought with love, everything is saved. If even I, a sinner like you, have been moved to tenderness and pity for you, how much more so God. Love is such a priceless treasure that with it you can buy the whole world, and redeem not only your own sins but the sins of others too. Go, and do not be afraid.”

<!-- B02-C03-P038 -->
He made the sign of the cross over her three times, took a small icon from around his neck, and put it around hers. She bowed to the ground before him in silence. He rose a little and looked cheerfully at a healthy peasant woman with a nursing infant in her arms.

<!-- B02-C03-P039 -->
“From Vyshegorye, dear Father.”

<!-- B02-C03-P040 -->
“That's six versts from here, though. A hard journey with a baby. What do you need?”

<!-- B02-C03-P041 -->
“I've come to have a look at you. I've been here before, or have you forgotten? Your memory can't be much if you've forgotten me already. They said at home that you were ill, and I thought, well then, I'll go and see him myself. And here I am looking at you—what's ill about you? You'll live another twenty years, truly you will, God be with you! And with all the people praying for you, how could you be ill?”

<!-- B02-C03-P042 -->
“Thank you for everything, my dear.”

<!-- B02-C03-P043 -->
“And while I'm here, I've a little favor to ask: here's sixty kopecks. Give it, dear Father, to some woman poorer than I am. On my way here I thought, better to give it through him, he'll know who should have it.”

<!-- B02-C03-P044 -->
“Thank you, my dear, thank you, kind soul. I love you. I'll be sure to do it. Is that a girl in your arms?”

<!-- B02-C03-P045 -->
“A girl, dear light, Lizaveta.”

<!-- B02-C03-P046 -->
“May the Lord bless you both, you and the infant Lizaveta. You've made my heart glad, mother. Farewell, my dears, farewell, my dear, beloved ones.”

<!-- B02-C03-P047 -->
He blessed them all and bowed deeply to them all.

[^b02-c03-n001]: Imperial Russia’s “townsman estate” (*meshchanstvo*) was a legal social category, not merely a description of where someone lived. The woman is registered as a peasant although she lives in town.

[^b02-c03-n002]: The words echo Jeremiah 31:15 and its quotation in Matthew 2:18.
