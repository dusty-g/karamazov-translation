# Book XII: A Judicial Error
## VII. A Historical Survey

“The medical experts sought to prove to us that the defendant is not in his right mind and suffers from mania. I maintain that he is precisely in his right mind, and that this makes it all the worse: if he were out of his mind, he might perhaps turn out to be much wiser. As for his having a mania, I would agree, but on one point alone, the very point to which the experts referred: the defendant’s view of those three thousand supposedly withheld by his father. Nevertheless, we may perhaps find an explanation far closer at hand for the defendant’s constant frenzy over this money than a tendency toward insanity. For my part, I fully agree with the young physician’s opinion that the defendant enjoys, and has enjoyed, the complete and normal use of his mental faculties, and was merely irritated and embittered. That is precisely the point: it was not the three thousand, not the sum itself, that was the object of the defendant’s constant and frenzied bitterness; there was a special reason that inflamed his anger. That reason was jealousy!”

Here Ippolit Kirillovich unfolded at length the whole picture of the defendant’s fatal passion for Grushenka. He began with the very moment when the defendant had gone to the “young person” in order to “beat her,” to use his own words, Ippolit Kirillovich explained, “but instead of beating her, he remained at her feet—that was the beginning of this love. At the same time the old man, the defendant’s father, casts his eye on the same person—an astonishing and fatal coincidence, for both hearts suddenly caught fire at the same time, although both men had known and encountered her before; and both those hearts caught fire with the most unrestrained, the most Karamazov of passions. Here we have her own admission: ‘I,’ she says, ‘made fun of both of them.’ Yes, she suddenly wanted to make fun of them both; she had not wanted to before, but now the intention suddenly flew into her head—and it ended with both men falling before her, defeated. The old man, who worshiped money as a god, immediately set aside three thousand rubles merely for her to visit his abode, but was soon brought to the point where he would have counted it happiness to lay his name and his entire fortune at her feet, if only she would consent to become his lawful wife. For this we have firm testimony. As for the defendant, his tragedy is plain; it is before us. But such was the young person’s ‘game.’ The temptress did not even offer the unfortunate young man any hope, for hope, real hope, was offered to him only at the very last moment, when, kneeling before his tormentor, he stretched out to her hands already stained with the blood of his father and rival. It was in this very position that he was arrested. ‘Send me, me to penal servitude with him! I drove him to it; I’m more to blame than anyone!’ the woman herself cried, now in sincere repentance, at the moment of his arrest. The talented young man who has undertaken to describe this case—the same Mr. Rakitin whom I have already mentioned—defines this heroine’s character in a few concise and distinctive phrases: ‘Early disillusionment, early deception and a fall, betrayal by the seducer-fiancé who abandoned her; then poverty, the curse of an honest family, and finally the protection of a wealthy old man, whom, however, she herself still regards as her benefactor. Anger took root all too early in a young heart that may have contained much good. A calculating character developed, intent on accumulating capital. Mockery developed, and vindictiveness toward society.’ After this description, it is understandable that she could have mocked them both simply for a game, a malicious game. And so, in this month of hopeless love, of moral lapses, of betrayal of his fiancée, of appropriating another’s money entrusted to his honor, the defendant is driven, besides, almost to frenzy, to madness, by continual jealousy—and of whom? His own father! And above all, the crazed old man lures and tempts the object of his passion with those same three thousand that the son regards as his own patrimony, his inheritance from his mother, for which he reproaches his father. Yes, I agree, this was hard to bear! Even a mania might have arisen here. It was not a question of money, but of his happiness being shattered with that very money and with such loathsome cynicism!”

Ippolit Kirillovich then turned to the gradual development in the defendant’s mind of the idea of murdering his father, tracing it through the facts.

“At first we merely shout in taverns—we shout throughout this whole month. Oh, we love to live in public and immediately tell that public everything, even our most infernal and dangerous ideas; we love to share with people, and for some unknown reason demand at once, then and there, that they immediately respond with complete sympathy, enter into all our cares and anxieties, agree with us, and place no restraint on our temperament. Otherwise we grow angry and smash up the whole tavern.” (Here followed the story of Staff Captain Snegiryov.) “Those who saw and heard the defendant during this month finally sensed that this might no longer be mere shouting and threats against his father, that in such a frenzy the threats might perhaps become deeds.” (Here the prosecutor described the family meeting at the monastery, the conversations with Alyosha, and the disgraceful scene of violence in the father’s house, when the defendant burst in after dinner.) “I do not intend to insist,” Ippolit Kirillovich continued, “that before this scene the defendant had already deliberately and with premeditation resolved to finish with his father by murdering him. Nevertheless, the idea had already presented itself to him several times, and he had consciously contemplated it; for this we have facts, witnesses, and his own admission. I confess, gentlemen of the jury,” Ippolit Kirillovich added, “that even until today I hesitated to attribute to the defendant full and conscious premeditation of the crime that kept suggesting itself to him. I was firmly convinced that in his soul he had contemplated the fatal moment ahead many times, but had only contemplated it, imagined it as a possibility, without yet determining either the time of its execution or the circumstances. But I hesitated only until today, until this fatal document presented to the court today by Miss Verkhovtseva. You yourselves heard her cry, gentlemen: ‘It is a plan, it is a program for the murder!’ That was how she characterized the unfortunate defendant’s unfortunate ‘drunken’ letter. And indeed, the letter has all the significance of a program and a premeditated intention. It was written two days before the crime, and thus we now know for certain that two days before carrying out his terrible design, the defendant declared on oath that if he did not obtain money the following day, he would kill his father in order to take the money from beneath his pillow, ‘in a packet with a red ribbon, if only Ivan leaves.’ Do you hear: ‘if only Ivan leaves.’ Everything, then, has already been considered, the circumstances weighed—and what happens? Everything is subsequently carried out as written! Premeditation and deliberation are beyond doubt; the crime was to be committed for the purpose of robbery. This is explicitly declared, written, and signed. The defendant does not deny his signature. They will say he wrote it drunk. But that diminishes nothing and makes it all the more significant: drunk, he wrote what he had planned sober. Had he not planned it sober, he would not have written it drunk. Perhaps they will ask why he shouted about his intention in taverns. A man who deliberately resolves on such a deed keeps silent and conceals it. True, but he shouted when there were as yet no plans or premeditation, only a desire, an urge still ripening. Afterward he shouts about it less. On the evening the letter was written, while drinking at the Capital City tavern, he was unusually silent, did not play billiards, sat apart, spoke to no one, and only drove a local merchant’s clerk from his seat; but that was almost unconscious, from a habit of quarreling which he could no longer do without when he entered a tavern. True, along with his final decision, it must have occurred to the defendant to fear that he had shouted too much throughout the town beforehand, and that this might contribute greatly to his detection and prosecution once he carried out his plan. But what was to be done? The publicity was a fact; it could not be undone. Besides, luck had pulled him through before and would pull him through again. We trusted our star, gentlemen! I must also acknowledge that he did a great deal to avert the fatal moment, that he made considerable efforts to avoid a bloody outcome. ‘Tomorrow I shall ask everyone for three thousand,’ he writes in his distinctive way, ‘and if people won’t give it, blood will be spilled.’ Once again written drunk, and once again carried out sober, just as written!”

Here Ippolit Kirillovich embarked on a detailed account of all Mitya’s attempts to obtain money in order to avoid the crime. He described his adventures with Samsonov and the journey to Pointer, all from the documentary evidence. “Exhausted, mocked, hungry, having sold his watch to make this journey—while supposedly carrying fifteen hundred rubles on him, supposedly, oh, supposedly!—tormented by jealousy over the object of his love left behind in town, suspecting that in his absence she will go to Fyodor Pavlovich, he finally returns to town. Thank God! She has not been to Fyodor Pavlovich’s. He himself escorts her to her protector Samsonov’s. Strange to say, we are not jealous of Samsonov, and that is a most characteristic psychological feature of this case! Then he hastens to his observation post ‘at the back,’ and there—there he learns that Smerdyakov is down with epilepsy, that the other servant is ill: the way is clear, and he has the ‘signals’ in his possession—what a temptation! Nevertheless, he still resists; he goes to Madame Khokhlakov, temporarily residing among us and highly esteemed by us all. Long compassionate toward his plight, this lady offers him the soundest possible advice: to abandon all this carousing, this disgraceful love, this idle wandering from tavern to tavern, this fruitless waste of youthful strength, and go to the goldfields in Siberia. ‘There is the outlet for your turbulent energies, for your romantic nature thirsting for adventure.’” After describing the outcome of the conversation and the moment when the defendant suddenly learned that Grushenka had not been at Samsonov’s at all, after describing the instantaneous frenzy of the unfortunate jealous man, his nerves worn to pieces, at the thought that she had indeed deceived him and was now with him, with Fyodor Pavlovich, Ippolit Kirillovich concluded by drawing attention to the fatal significance of chance: “Had the servant girl managed to tell him that his beloved was at Mokroye, with the ‘former’ and ‘undisputed’ one, nothing would have happened. But she was stupefied with fright and began swearing and calling God to witness, and if the defendant did not kill her then and there, it was because he rushed headlong after the woman who had betrayed him. But note: however beside himself he was, he nevertheless took the brass pestle with him. Why a pestle in particular, why not some other weapon? But if we have been contemplating this scene for an entire month and preparing for it, then the moment something resembling a weapon catches our eye, we seize it as a weapon. And we have already spent a whole month imagining how an object of that kind might serve as a weapon. That is precisely why we recognized it as a weapon so instantly and unquestionably! Thus it was neither unconsciously nor involuntarily that he seized the fatal pestle. Now he is in his father’s garden: the way is clear, there are no witnesses, the night is deep, there is darkness and jealousy. The suspicion that she is here, with him, with his rival, in his arms, perhaps laughing at him at this very moment, takes his breath away. Nor is it merely suspicion—what room is there now for suspicion? The deception is manifest, obvious: she is here, in that lighted room, with him behind the screens—and so the unfortunate man creeps up to the window, respectfully peeps in, meekly submits with perfect propriety, and prudently withdraws, hastening out of harm’s way lest anything dangerous and immoral occur! And they wish to make us believe this: us, who know the defendant’s character and understand his state of mind, a state known to us from the facts; and above all when he had the signals by which he could immediately get the house opened and go inside!” Here, on the subject of the “signals,” Ippolit Kirillovich temporarily left off his accusation and considered it necessary to speak at length about Smerdyakov, in order to deal exhaustively with this whole incidental episode of the suspicion that Smerdyakov had committed the murder and put an end to the notion once and for all. He did so very thoroughly, and everyone understood that, despite all the contempt he displayed for this supposition, he nevertheless regarded it as extremely important.
