# Book III: The Lustful
## VI. Smerdyakov

<!-- B03-C06-P001 -->
He did indeed find his father still at the table. As always, the table had been laid in the drawing room, although the house had a proper dining room too. This was the largest room in the house, furnished with a certain old-fashioned pretension. The furniture was ancient, painted white, with shabby red half-silk upholstery. Between the windows stood mirrors in elaborate old carved frames, also white, with gilding. Two large portraits adorned the walls, whose white wallpaper had cracked in many places: one of some prince who had been governor-general of the region thirty years before, and the other of some bishop, also long since dead. In the icon corner were several icons, with a little lamp lit before them at night . . . less out of devotion than to keep the room lighted. Fyodor Pavlovich went to bed very late, at three or four in the morning; until then he would pace the room or sit in an armchair and think. He had made a habit of it. He often spent the night quite alone in the house, sending the servants off to the annex, but usually the servant Smerdyakov stayed with him, sleeping on a bench in the entrance hall. When Alyosha came in, dinner was over, but preserves and coffee had been served. Fyodor Pavlovich liked sweets after dinner, with a little cognac. Ivan Fyodorovich was there at the table too, also drinking coffee. The servants Grigory and Smerdyakov stood beside the table. Masters and servants alike were visibly animated and in unusually high spirits. Fyodor Pavlovich was laughing and roaring with laughter. Alyosha had heard that shrill laugh, so familiar from the old days, while he was still in the passage, and knew at once from its sound that his father was a long way from drunk: for now he was simply feeling benevolent.

<!-- B03-C06-P002 -->
“Here he is, here he is!” Fyodor Pavlovich yelled, suddenly overjoyed to see Alyosha. “Join us, sit down, have some coffee—it's fit for a fast, fit for a fast, and hot, and delicious! I won't offer you cognac, you're a fasting man, but would you like some, would you? No, I'll give you a little liqueur instead, a splendid one! Smerdyakov, go to the cupboard, second shelf on the right, here are the keys, hurry!”

<!-- B03-C06-P003 -->
Alyosha began to refuse the liqueur.

<!-- B03-C06-P004 -->
“They'll bring it anyway, if not for you, then for us,” said Fyodor Pavlovich, beaming. “But wait, have you had dinner or haven't you?”

<!-- B03-C06-P005 -->
“I have,” said Alyosha, who had in fact eaten only a slice of bread and drunk a glass of kvass in the Father Superior's kitchen. “But I'd gladly have some hot coffee.”

<!-- B03-C06-P006 -->
“My dear boy! Good for you! He'll have some coffee. Shall we warm it up? No, it's still boiling. Splendid coffee, Smerdyakov's own. My Smerdyakov's an artist with coffee and savory filled pies—and fish soup too, that's true. Come over for fish soup one day, let us know beforehand . . . But wait, wait, didn't I tell you earlier to move in here this very day, mattress and pillows and all? Did you drag your mattress along? Heh, heh, heh!”

<!-- B03-C06-P007 -->
“No, I didn't bring it,” said Alyosha, smiling too.

<!-- B03-C06-P008 -->
“But I frightened you, I did frighten you earlier, didn't I? Ah, my dear, could I ever hurt you? Listen, Ivan, I can't bear it when he looks into my eyes like that and laughs, I can't bear it. My whole insides start laughing back at him, I love him! Alyoshka, let me give you a father's blessing.”

<!-- B03-C06-P009 -->
Alyosha rose, but Fyodor Pavlovich had already thought better of it.

<!-- B03-C06-P010 -->
“No, no, I'll just make the sign of the cross over you for now, like this, sit down. Now you're in for a treat, and on your very own subject. You'll have a good laugh. Our Balaam's ass[^b03-c06-1] has started talking, and how he talks, how he talks!”

<!-- B03-C06-P011 -->
Balaam's ass turned out to be the footman Smerdyakov. Still a young man, only about twenty-four, he was terribly unsociable and taciturn. Not that he was wild or ashamed of anything; on the contrary, he was haughty by nature and seemed to despise everyone. But there is no getting around it: we must say at least a few words about him, and now is the time. Marfa Ignatyevna and Grigory Vasilyevich had brought him up, but the boy grew up “without a scrap of gratitude,” as Grigory put it, a wild boy who watched the world from a corner. As a child he was very fond of hanging cats and then burying them with due ceremony. For this he would put on a sheet to serve as a sort of vestment, sing, and wave something over the dead cat as though censing it. All this on the quiet, in the greatest secrecy. Grigory once caught him at it and gave him a painful beating with a switch. He retreated to a corner and glowered from there for a week. “He doesn't love either of us, that monster,” Grigory would tell Marfa Ignatyevna, “and he doesn't love anyone else. Are you even human?” he would suddenly say, turning directly to Smerdyakov. “You're not human, you bred out of the bathhouse slime, that's what you are . . .” As it turned out later, Smerdyakov could never forgive him those words. Grigory taught him to read and write, and when he was about twelve began to teach him sacred history. But that came to nothing almost at once. One day, at only the second or third lesson, the boy suddenly smirked.

<!-- B03-C06-P012 -->
“What's that for?” Grigory asked, peering at him fiercely from beneath his spectacles.

<!-- B03-C06-P013 -->
“Nothing, sir. The Lord God created light on the first day, but the sun, moon, and stars on the fourth. So where did the light shine from on the first day?”

<!-- B03-C06-P014 -->
Grigory was dumbfounded. The boy looked mockingly at his teacher. There was even something arrogant in his gaze. Grigory could not stand it. “Here's where!” he shouted, and struck his pupil furiously across the cheek. The boy took the slap without a word of protest, but once again huddled in a corner for several days. It so happened that a week later he had his first attack of epilepsy, which was to stay with him for the rest of his life. When Fyodor Pavlovich learned of this, his attitude toward the boy seemed to change all at once. Until then he had regarded him rather indifferently, although he never scolded him and always gave him a kopeck when they met. Sometimes, when he was in a good mood, he would send the boy something sweet from his table. But now, on hearing of the illness, he began to take real care of him, called in a doctor, and had treatment begun, though it turned out there was no cure. The fits came on average once a month, at irregular intervals. They varied in severity too: some were mild, others very violent. Fyodor Pavlovich strictly forbade Grigory to punish the boy physically and began allowing him upstairs to his own rooms. He also forbade anyone to teach him anything for the time being. But once, when the boy was about fifteen, Fyodor Pavlovich noticed him lingering by the bookcase, reading the titles through the glass. Fyodor Pavlovich had quite a few books, a hundred volumes or more, though no one had ever seen him reading one himself. He handed Smerdyakov the key to the bookcase at once. “Well then, read, you can be the librarian. Instead of hanging about the yard, sit down and read. Here, read this one.” And Fyodor Pavlovich took out *Evenings on a Farm near Dikanka* for him.

<!-- B03-C06-P015 -->
The lad read it but was displeased. He did not smile once; on the contrary, he finished with a frown.

<!-- B03-C06-P016 -->
“Well? Isn't it funny?” Fyodor Pavlovich asked. Smerdyakov said nothing.

<!-- B03-C06-P017 -->
“Answer me, fool.”

<!-- B03-C06-P018 -->
“It's all about things that aren't true,” Smerdyakov mumbled, with a smirk.

<!-- B03-C06-P019 -->
“Then go to the devil, you lackey's soul. Wait, here's Smaragdov's *Universal History*, that's all true, read it.”

<!-- B03-C06-P020 -->
But Smerdyakov did not get through even ten pages of Smaragdov: he found it dull. And so the bookcase was closed again. Before long Marfa and Grigory reported to Fyodor Pavlovich that little by little Smerdyakov had suddenly developed a terrible sort of fastidiousness: he would sit down to his soup, take a spoon, and search and search through it, bending over, peering into it, scooping up a spoonful and holding it to the light.

<!-- B03-C06-P021 -->
“Is there a cockroach in it?” Grigory would ask.

<!-- B03-C06-P022 -->
“A fly, perhaps,” Marfa would remark.

<!-- B03-C06-P023 -->
The fastidious young man never answered, but it proved to be the same with bread, meat, and every other dish: he would lift a morsel on his fork to the light, examine it as though through a microscope, hesitate a long time, and finally make up his mind to put it in his mouth. “Look at the fine little gentleman we've got ourselves,” Grigory muttered as he watched him. When Fyodor Pavlovich heard about this new quality in Smerdyakov, he decided at once that he should be a cook and sent him to Moscow to learn the trade. He spent several years in training and returned with his face much altered. Somehow he had suddenly aged to an extraordinary degree, grown wrinkled quite beyond his years, turned sallow, and begun to look like a eunuch. In character, however, he returned much as he had been before leaving for Moscow: just as unsociable, feeling not the slightest need for anyone's company. In Moscow, too, people later said, he had always kept silent; Moscow itself had interested him extraordinarily little, so that he learned perhaps a thing or two about it and paid no attention to the rest. He had even been to the theater once, but came back silent and displeased. He did, however, return to us from Moscow in good clothes, with a clean frock coat and linen, and invariably brushed his clothes himself very carefully twice a day. He was particularly fond of polishing his elegant calfskin boots with a special English blacking until they shone like mirrors. He proved an excellent cook. Fyodor Pavlovich gave him a salary, and Smerdyakov spent almost all of it on clothes, pomade, scent, and so forth. But he seemed to despise the female sex as much as the male, conducting himself gravely with women, almost unapproachably. Fyodor Pavlovich began to regard him from another angle too. The fact was that his epileptic fits had grown worse, and on those days Marfa Ignatyevna did the cooking, which did not suit Fyodor Pavlovich at all.

<!-- B03-C06-P024 -->
“Why are your fits getting more frequent?” he would sometimes ask his new cook with a sidelong glance, studying his face. “You might at least marry someone. Shall I find you a wife? . . .”

<!-- B03-C06-P025 -->
But at such remarks Smerdyakov only turned pale with vexation and said nothing. Fyodor Pavlovich would wave a hand and walk away. The main thing was that he trusted his honesty, once and for all: he would never take anything, never steal. Once Fyodor Pavlovich, a little drunk, dropped three “rainbow” banknotes[^b03-c06-2] he had just received into the mud in his own yard, and did not miss them until the next day. He had barely begun searching his pockets when there were the “rainbow” notes, all three of them, on his table. Where had they come from? Smerdyakov had picked them up and brought them in the day before. “Well, my lad, I've never seen the like of you,” Fyodor Pavlovich declared flatly, and gave him ten rubles. It must be added that he not only trusted his honesty but for some reason was even fond of him, although the lad looked just as askance at him as at anyone else, and was always silent. He rarely spoke. If anyone looking at him then had thought to ask what interested the fellow and what was most often on his mind, it would truly have been impossible to tell by looking at him. Yet sometimes, in the house, or perhaps in the yard or the street, he would stop, grow thoughtful, and stand there for as long as ten minutes. Someone skilled in reading faces, studying him, would have said that there was neither reflection nor thought in him, only a kind of contemplation. The painter Kramskoy has a remarkable picture called *The Contemplator*: it shows a forest in winter, and in the forest, on the road, stands a little peasant in a ragged coat and bast shoes, utterly alone, having strayed into the deepest solitude; he stands as though lost in thought, but he is not thinking, he is “contemplating” something. If you nudged him, he would start and look at you as though waking, but without understanding anything. True, he would come to his senses at once, but if you asked what he had been standing there thinking about, he would probably remember nothing; yet he would probably have stored away within him the impression that possessed him during his contemplation. These impressions are precious to him, and he is probably accumulating them, imperceptibly, even without knowing it. What for, to what end, he of course does not know either: perhaps, after accumulating impressions for many years, he will suddenly abandon everything and go off to Jerusalem, to wander and seek salvation; or perhaps he will suddenly burn down his native village; or perhaps he will do both. There are plenty of contemplators among the people. Smerdyakov was probably one of these contemplators too, and probably hoarded his impressions just as greedily, almost without knowing why himself.

[^b03-c06-1]: In Numbers 22, Balaam’s donkey suddenly speaks after being beaten; Fyodor uses the story to mock the usually silent Smerdyakov.

[^b03-c06-2]: “Rainbow” was a name for a hundred-ruble banknote. Three notes here mean three hundred rubles.
