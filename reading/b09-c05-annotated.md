# Book IX: The Preliminary Investigation
## V. The Third Ordeal

<!-- B09-C05-P001 -->
Though Mitya resumed in a harsh tone, he plainly took even greater pains not to forget or omit a single detail of what he was relating. He told them how he had jumped over the fence into his father's garden, how he had approached the window, and finally everything that had happened beneath it. Clearly, precisely, as though stamping out each word, he described the feelings that had stirred him during those moments in the garden, when he had been so desperately eager to know whether Grushenka was with his father. But strangely, this time both the prosecutor and the investigator listened with extraordinary reserve, looked at him coldly, and asked far fewer questions. Mitya could read nothing in their faces. “They're angry and offended,” he thought. “Well, to hell with them!” When he told them how he had finally decided to give his father the signal that Grushenka had come so that he would open the window, the prosecutor and investigator paid no attention whatever to the word “signal,” as though they had failed entirely to understand its significance here; Mitya even noticed this. At last, reaching the moment when, at the sight of his father leaning out the window, he had boiled with hatred and snatched the pestle from his pocket, he suddenly stopped, as though on purpose. He sat staring at the wall, knowing that their eyes had fastened on him.

<!-- B09-C05-P002 -->
“Well, sir,” the investigator said, “you drew your weapon and... and what happened next?”

<!-- B09-C05-P003 -->
“Next? Next I killed him... hit him on the crown of the head and split his skull... That's how you see it, isn't it? Isn't it!” His eyes suddenly blazed. All his anger, which had died down, suddenly rose in his soul with extraordinary force.

<!-- B09-C05-P004 -->
“How we see it,” Nikolai Parfenovich repeated. “Well, and how do you see it?”

<!-- B09-C05-P005 -->
Mitya lowered his eyes and was silent for a long time.

<!-- B09-C05-P006 -->
“As I see it, gentlemen, as I see it, this is what happened,” he began quietly. “Whether it was someone's tears, whether my mother prayed to God, whether a radiant spirit kissed me at that moment—I don't know, but the devil was defeated. I rushed away from the window and ran toward the fence... My father took fright and only then caught sight of me for the first time; he cried out and sprang back from the window—I remember that very well. And I ran through the garden toward the fence... and that was where Grigory caught up with me, when I was already sitting on top of it...”

<!-- B09-C05-P007 -->
Here at last he raised his eyes to his listeners. They seemed to be looking at him with perfectly untroubled attention. A spasm of indignation passed through Mitya's soul.

<!-- B09-C05-P008 -->
“But you're laughing at me this very moment, gentlemen!” he suddenly broke off.

<!-- B09-C05-P009 -->
“What makes you conclude that?” Nikolai Parfenovich observed.

<!-- B09-C05-P010 -->
“You don't believe a single word, that's why! I understand perfectly well that I've reached the crucial point: the old man is lying there now with his head smashed in, and I—after describing so tragically how I wanted to kill him and had already pulled out the pestle, I suddenly run away from the window... Poetry! In verse! We can take the fine fellow's word for it! Ha-ha! You're scoffers, gentlemen!”

<!-- B09-C05-P011 -->
He twisted his whole body around in the chair, making it crack.

<!-- B09-C05-P012 -->
“And did you happen to notice,” the prosecutor suddenly began, as though paying no attention to Mitya's agitation, “as you ran away from the window, did you notice whether the door into the garden, at the other end of the wing, was unlocked or not?”

<!-- B09-C05-P013 -->
“No, it wasn't unlocked.”

<!-- B09-C05-P014 -->
“It wasn't?”

<!-- B09-C05-P015 -->
“It was locked, on the contrary, and who could have opened it? Ah, the door—wait!” he said, as though suddenly coming to himself, almost starting. “Did you find the door unlocked?”

<!-- B09-C05-P016 -->
“Unlocked.”

<!-- B09-C05-P017 -->
“Then who could have opened it, unless you opened it yourselves?” Mitya asked, suddenly astonished.

<!-- B09-C05-P018 -->
“The door stood unlocked, and your father's murderer undoubtedly entered by that door and, after committing the murder, left by the same door,” the prosecutor said slowly and distinctly, as though stamping out each word. “That is perfectly clear to us. The murder evidently took place in the room, not through the window, as is established beyond question by the examination of the scene, the position of the body, and everything else. There can be no doubt on that point.” Mitya was profoundly shaken.

<!-- B09-C05-P019 -->
“But that's impossible, gentlemen!” he cried, utterly bewildered. “I... I didn't go in... I tell you positively, with certainty, that the door was locked the whole time I was in the garden and when I ran away from it. I only stood beneath the window and saw him through the window, that was all, all... I remember to the very last minute. And even if I didn't remember, I'd still know, because only Smerdyakov and I and he, the dead man, knew the signals, and without the signals he wouldn't have opened to anyone in the world!”

<!-- B09-C05-P020 -->
“Signals? What signals?” the prosecutor asked with eager, almost hysterical curiosity, instantly losing all his reserved composure. He put the question as though creeping timidly closer. He had sensed an important fact unknown to him, and at once felt the greatest fear that Mitya might refuse to disclose it in full.

<!-- B09-C05-P021 -->
“So you didn't know!” Mitya winked at him with a mocking, malicious smile. “What if I don't tell you? Who will tell you then? Only the dead man, Smerdyakov, and I knew about the signals, that's all; heaven knew too, but it won't tell you. And it's an interesting little fact—the devil knows what you could build on it, ha-ha! Take heart, gentlemen, I'll reveal it. You're thinking nonsense. You don't know whom you're dealing with! You're dealing with a defendant who gives evidence against himself, to his own harm! Yes, sir, because I'm a knight of honor, and you aren't!”

<!-- B09-C05-P022 -->
The prosecutor swallowed every bitter pill; he was simply trembling with impatience to learn the new fact. Mitya explained precisely and at length everything concerning the signals Fyodor Pavlovich had devised for Smerdyakov. He told them exactly what each knock on the window meant, even tapped out the signals on the table, and when Nikolai Parfenovich asked whether he himself, Mitya, when he knocked at the old man's window, had tapped out precisely the signal meaning “Grushenka has come,” he answered explicitly that yes, that was exactly what he had tapped: “Grushenka has come.”

<!-- B09-C05-P023 -->
“There you are—now build your tower!” Mitya broke off and again turned contemptuously away from them.

<!-- B09-C05-P024 -->
“And only your late father, you, and the servant Smerdyakov knew these signals? No one else?” Nikolai Parfenovich inquired once more.

<!-- B09-C05-P025 -->
“Yes, the servant Smerdyakov, and heaven as well. Write down heaven too; it won't hurt to put that on record. You'll need God yourselves, after all.”

<!-- B09-C05-P026 -->
Of course they began to write it down, but as they did so the prosecutor suddenly said, as though a new idea had just occurred to him:

<!-- B09-C05-P027 -->
“But if Smerdyakov also knew these signals, and you categorically reject any accusation concerning your father's death, could he not have tapped the agreed signals, induced your father to open the door to him, and then... committed the crime?”

<!-- B09-C05-P028 -->
Mitya looked at him with profound mockery and at the same time terrible hatred. He stared silently for so long that the prosecutor's eyes began to blink.

<!-- B09-C05-P029 -->
“Caught the fox again!” Mitya said at last. “Trapped the nasty creature by its tail, heh-heh! I see right through you, Mr. Prosecutor! You thought I'd jump up at once, seize on your suggestion, and shout at the top of my voice: ‘Ah, it's Smerdyakov, there's your murderer!’ Admit that was what you thought, admit it, and then I'll go on.”

<!-- B09-C05-P030 -->
But the prosecutor admitted nothing. He waited in silence.

<!-- B09-C05-P031 -->
“You're mistaken. I won't raise a cry against Smerdyakov!” Mitya said.

<!-- B09-C05-P032 -->
“You don't even suspect him at all?”

<!-- B09-C05-P033 -->
“Do you suspect him?”

<!-- B09-C05-P034 -->
“We suspected him too.”

<!-- B09-C05-P035 -->
Mitya fixed his eyes on the floor.

<!-- B09-C05-P036 -->
“Joking aside,” he said gloomily, “listen: from the very beginning, almost from the moment I ran out to you earlier from behind that curtain, the thought had already flashed through my mind: ‘Smerdyakov!’ I sat here at the table shouting that I was innocent of bloodshed, while all the time I was thinking: ‘Smerdyakov!’ And Smerdyakov would not leave my soul alone. Finally, just now, the same thought suddenly came to me: ‘Smerdyakov,’ but only for a second. At once another thought came alongside it: ‘No, not Smerdyakov!’ This isn't his work, gentlemen!”

<!-- B09-C05-P037 -->
“In that case, do you suspect some other person?” Nikolai Parfenovich ventured cautiously.

<!-- B09-C05-P038 -->
“I don't know who, or what person, the hand of heaven or Satan, but... not Smerdyakov!” Mitya cut him short decisively.

<!-- B09-C05-P039 -->
“But why do you insist so firmly and persistently that it wasn't he?”

<!-- B09-C05-P040 -->
“From conviction. From my impression of him. Because Smerdyakov is a man of the lowest nature and a coward. Not just a coward: he's every kind of cowardice in the world put together and walking on two legs. He was born of a hen. Every time he spoke to me he trembled for fear I'd kill him, though I never even raised a hand. He fell at my feet and wept, he kissed these very boots of mine, literally, begging me ‘not to frighten him.’ Do you hear? ‘Not to frighten him’—what kind of words are those? And I even gave him presents. He's a sickly chicken with the falling sickness, feeble-minded, whom an eight-year-old boy could beat up. Is that any sort of character? Not Smerdyakov, gentlemen, and he doesn't care for money either, never accepted my presents... Besides, why would he kill the old man? He may be his son, after all, his illegitimate son. Do you know that?”

<!-- B09-C05-P041 -->
“We have heard that legend. But you too are your father's son, and you yourself told everyone that you wanted to kill him.”

<!-- B09-C05-P042 -->
“A stone thrown at me! A low, filthy stone! I'm not afraid! Oh, gentlemen, perhaps it's too vile of you to say that to my face! Vile because I told you that myself. Not only did I want to, I could have killed him, and voluntarily brought it on myself by saying I'd nearly killed him! But I didn't kill him, my guardian angel saved me—that's what you haven't taken into account... And that's why it's vile of you, vile! Because I didn't kill him, didn't kill him, didn't kill him! Do you hear, Mr. Prosecutor: I didn't kill him!”

<!-- B09-C05-P043 -->
He was almost choking. At no time during the interrogation had he been so agitated.

<!-- B09-C05-P044 -->
“And what did he tell you, gentlemen—Smerdyakov?” he suddenly concluded after a silence. “May I ask you that?”

<!-- B09-C05-P045 -->
“You may ask us anything,” the prosecutor answered with a cold, stern expression, “anything concerning the facts of the case, and we are, I repeat, even obliged to satisfy you on every question. We found the servant Smerdyakov, about whom you inquire, lying unconscious on his bed in an extremely violent epileptic seizure, perhaps the tenth in succession. The doctor who accompanied us examined the patient and even told us that he might not live until morning.”

<!-- B09-C05-P046 -->
“Well, in that case the devil killed my father!” suddenly escaped Mitya, as though even up to that moment he had still been asking himself: “Smerdyakov or not Smerdyakov?”

<!-- B09-C05-P047 -->
“We shall return to that fact,” Nikolai Parfenovich decided. “For now, perhaps you would care to continue your testimony.”

<!-- B09-C05-P048 -->
Mitya asked for a rest. Permission was politely granted. After resting, he resumed. But it was plainly hard for him. He was exhausted, insulted, and shaken to the soul. Besides, the prosecutor now began, apparently on purpose, to provoke him at every turn by fastening on “trifles.” No sooner had Mitya described how, sitting astride the fence, he had struck Grigory on the head with the pestle when the old man seized his left leg, and then immediately jumped down beside the fallen man, than the prosecutor stopped him and asked for a more detailed description of how he had sat on the fence. Mitya was astonished.

<!-- B09-C05-P049 -->
“Well, I sat like this, astride it, one leg there, the other here...”

<!-- B09-C05-P050 -->
“And the pestle?”

<!-- B09-C05-P051 -->
“I was holding the pestle.”

<!-- B09-C05-P052 -->
“Not in your pocket? You remember it in such detail? Well, did you swing your arm hard?”

<!-- B09-C05-P053 -->
“I must have swung it hard. Why do you want to know?”

<!-- B09-C05-P054 -->
“Perhaps you could sit on a chair exactly as you sat on the fence and demonstrate for us, to make it clear, how you swung and in which direction?”

<!-- B09-C05-P055 -->
“Are you making fun of me?” Mitya asked, looking haughtily at his questioner, who did not so much as blink. Mitya twisted around convulsively, sat astride the chair, and swung his arm:

<!-- B09-C05-P056 -->
“That's how I struck him! That's how I killed him! What more do you want?”

<!-- B09-C05-P057 -->
“Thank you. Now would you be so good as to explain why, precisely, you jumped down, with what purpose and what, precisely, in mind?”

<!-- B09-C05-P058 -->
“Well, the devil... I jumped down to the fallen man... I don't know why!”

<!-- B09-C05-P059 -->
“In such agitation? And while escaping?”

<!-- B09-C05-P060 -->
“Yes, in agitation and while escaping.”

<!-- B09-C05-P061 -->
“You wanted to help him?”

<!-- B09-C05-P062 -->
“What do you mean, help... Yes, perhaps to help, I don't remember.”

<!-- B09-C05-P063 -->
“You were beside yourself? That is, you were even in a state of partial unconsciousness?”

<!-- B09-C05-P064 -->
“Oh, no, not unconscious at all, I remember everything. Every last detail. I jumped down to look and wiped the blood off him with my handkerchief.”

<!-- B09-C05-P065 -->
“We saw your handkerchief. You hoped to restore the man you had struck down to life?”

<!-- B09-C05-P066 -->
“I don't know whether I hoped to. I simply wanted to find out whether he was alive or not.”

<!-- B09-C05-P067 -->
“Ah, so you wanted to make certain? Well, and then?”

<!-- B09-C05-P068 -->
“I'm not a doctor, I couldn't decide. I ran away thinking I'd killed him, and now he's come round.”

<!-- B09-C05-P069 -->
“Excellent, sir,” the prosecutor concluded. “Thank you. That was all I needed. Please continue.”

<!-- B09-C05-P070 -->
Alas, it never occurred to Mitya to say, though he remembered it, that he had jumped down out of pity and, standing over the man he thought he had killed, had even uttered a few pitiful words: “You've had it, old man. Nothing to be done, so lie there.” The prosecutor, meanwhile, drew only one conclusion: that a man had jumped down, “at such a moment and in such agitation,” solely to make absolutely certain whether the only witness to his crime was alive or dead. What strength, determination, coolness, and calculation the man had therefore shown, even at such a moment... and so on, and so on. The prosecutor was pleased: “I provoked a man in a morbid state with ‘trifles,’ and he gave himself away.”

<!-- B09-C05-P071 -->
Mitya continued in torment. But at once Nikolai Parfenovich stopped him again:

<!-- B09-C05-P072 -->
“How could you run in to the maidservant Fedosya Markova with so much blood on your hands and, as it subsequently turned out, on your face?”

<!-- B09-C05-P073 -->
“I hadn't even noticed then that I was covered in blood!” Mitya replied.

<!-- B09-C05-P074 -->
“That's plausible. That does happen,” the prosecutor said, exchanging glances with Nikolai Parfenovich.

<!-- B09-C05-P075 -->
“Exactly, I hadn't noticed. Very good, Mr. Prosecutor,” Mitya suddenly approved as well. But next came the story of Mitya's sudden decision to “remove himself” and “let the happy ones pass him by.” And he could no longer bring himself, as he had earlier, to lay his heart bare again and tell them of “the queen of his soul.” He recoiled from doing so before these cold people who “fastened on him like bedbugs.” And so, in response to their repeated questions, he stated briefly and sharply:

<!-- B09-C05-P076 -->
“Well, I decided to kill myself. Why go on living? The question presented itself of its own accord. Her former lover had appeared, the one whose claim was unquestioned, the man who had wronged her, but who had galloped back in love after five years to bring that wrong to an end in lawful marriage. And so I understood that everything was lost for me... And behind me lay disgrace, and this blood, Grigory's blood... Why live, then? So I went to redeem the pistols I'd pawned, load them, and put a bullet in my head by dawn...”

<!-- B09-C05-P077 -->
“And a grand feast during the night?”

<!-- B09-C05-P078 -->
“A grand feast during the night. Oh, the devil, gentlemen, finish quickly. I certainly meant to shoot myself, not far from here, beyond the outskirts of town, and I'd have done for myself at about five in the morning. I had a note ready in my pocket; I wrote it at Perkhotin's when I loaded the pistol. Here's the note, read it. I'm not telling this for your benefit!” he suddenly added contemptuously. He pulled the note from his waistcoat pocket and tossed it onto the table. The investigators read it with curiosity and, as usual, attached it to the case file.

<!-- B09-C05-P079 -->
“And it still didn't occur to you to wash your hands, even when you went into Mr. Perkhotin's? You weren't afraid of arousing suspicion, then?”

<!-- B09-C05-P080 -->
“What suspicion? Whether anyone suspected me or not, it was all the same: I'd have galloped here and shot myself at five, and there wouldn't have been time to do anything. If it hadn't been for what happened to my father, you wouldn't have learned anything or come here. Oh, the devil did it, the devil killed my father, and it was through the devil that you found out so quickly! How did you get here so fast? It's a marvel, a fantasy!”

<!-- B09-C05-P081 -->
“Mr. Perkhotin told us that when you came in you were holding... in your bloodstained hands... your money... a great deal of money... a bundle of hundred-ruble notes, and that the boy who serves him saw it too!”

<!-- B09-C05-P082 -->
“Yes, gentlemen, I remember that being so.”

<!-- B09-C05-P083 -->
“Now a small question arises. Can you tell us,” Nikolai Parfenovich began with extraordinary gentleness, “where you suddenly obtained so much money, when the evidence shows, even from a calculation of the time, that you did not go home?”

<!-- B09-C05-P084 -->
The prosecutor frowned a little at the question being put so bluntly, but did not interrupt Nikolai Parfenovich.

<!-- B09-C05-P085 -->
“No, I didn't go home,” Mitya answered, apparently very calmly, but looking down.

<!-- B09-C05-P086 -->
“Then allow me to repeat the question,” Nikolai Parfenovich continued, as though creeping closer. “Where could you have obtained such a sum all at once, when, by your own admission, as late as five o'clock that day...”

<!-- B09-C05-P087 -->
“I needed ten rubles and pawned my pistols with Perkhotin, then went to Madame Khokhlakov for three thousand, which she wouldn't give me, and so on, and all the rest of it,” Mitya interrupted sharply. “Yes, there you are, gentlemen, I was in need, and suddenly thousands appeared, eh? You know, gentlemen, you're both afraid just now: what if he won't say where he got it? And that's exactly it: I won't tell you, gentlemen, you've guessed right, you won't find out,” Mitya suddenly pronounced with extraordinary resolution. The investigators were silent for a moment.

<!-- B09-C05-P088 -->
“Please understand, Mr. Karamazov, it is essential for us to know,” Nikolai Parfenovich said quietly and humbly.

<!-- B09-C05-P089 -->
“I understand, but I still won't tell you.”

<!-- B09-C05-P090 -->
The prosecutor intervened too, again reminding him that the person under interrogation could, of course, decline to answer questions if he thought it in his best interest, and so forth, but in view of the damage a suspect might do himself by his silence, and particularly in the face of questions of such importance that...

<!-- B09-C05-P091 -->
“And so on, gentlemen, and so on! Enough, I've heard that sermon before!” Mitya broke in again. “I understand myself how important this is and that it's the most essential point of all, but I still won't tell you.”

<!-- B09-C05-P092 -->
“What is it to us, sir? It's your affair, after all, not ours; you'll be harming yourself,” Nikolai Parfenovich remarked nervously.

<!-- B09-C05-P093 -->
“You see, gentlemen, joking aside,” Mitya raised his eyes and looked firmly at them both, “I had a presentiment from the very beginning that we'd come head to head on this point. But at the beginning, when I started giving evidence earlier, all this lay in a distant mist, everything was floating, and I was even simple enough to begin by proposing ‘mutual trust between us.’ Now I see myself that such trust could never have existed, because we were bound to come up against this accursed fence! Well, here we are! There's no way through, and that's that! Still, I don't blame you; you can't just take my word for it, I understand that!”

<!-- B09-C05-P094 -->
He fell gloomily silent.

<!-- B09-C05-P095 -->
“Could you not, without in any way violating your determination to remain silent about the principal matter, nevertheless give us at least the slightest hint as to what motives could be powerful enough to lead you to remain silent at so dangerous a moment in your present testimony?”

<!-- B09-C05-P096 -->
Mitya smiled sadly and somewhat pensively.

<!-- B09-C05-P097 -->
“I'm much kinder than you think, gentlemen. I'll tell you why, and give you that hint, though you don't deserve it. I keep silent, gentlemen, because it means disgrace for me. The answer to the question of where I got this money contains such disgrace for me that even murdering and robbing my father couldn't compare with it, if I had murdered and robbed him. That's why I can't speak. I can't, for shame. What is it, gentlemen? You want to write that down?”

<!-- B09-C05-P098 -->
“Yes, we'll write it down,” Nikolai Parfenovich stammered.

<!-- B09-C05-P099 -->
“You shouldn't write that down, the part about ‘disgrace.’ I only told you out of the kindness of my heart; I needn't have told you at all. I gave it to you as a present, so to speak, and at once you seize on every last scrap. Well, write, write whatever you please,” he concluded with contempt and disgust. “I'm not afraid of you and... I hold my head high before you.”

<!-- B09-C05-P100 -->
“Perhaps you could say what kind of disgrace it is?” Nikolai Parfenovich ventured falteringly.

<!-- B09-C05-P101 -->
The prosecutor frowned terribly.

<!-- B09-C05-P102 -->
“No, no, *c'est fini*[^b09-c05-n001], don't trouble yourselves. It isn't worth getting dirty over. I've already dirtied myself enough through contact with you. You aren't worth it, neither you nor anyone... Enough, gentlemen, I'm stopping.”

<!-- B09-C05-P103 -->
This was spoken with unmistakable resolve. Nikolai Parfenovich stopped insisting, but from Ippolit Kirillovich's glances he instantly gathered that the latter had not yet lost hope.

<!-- B09-C05-P104 -->
“Can you at least state how large a sum you held when you went into Mr. Perkhotin's with it—that is, exactly how many rubles?”

<!-- B09-C05-P105 -->
“I can't state that either.”

<!-- B09-C05-P106 -->
“You seem to have told Mr. Perkhotin of three thousand supposedly received from Madame Khokhlakov?”

<!-- B09-C05-P107 -->
“Perhaps I did. Enough, gentlemen, I won't tell you how much.”

<!-- B09-C05-P108 -->
“Then please describe your journey here and everything you did after arriving.”

<!-- B09-C05-P109 -->
“Oh, ask everyone here about that. Still, if you like, I'll tell you too.”

<!-- B09-C05-P110 -->
He told them, but we shall not reproduce his account. He spoke dryly and rapidly. He said nothing at all about the raptures of his love. He did, however, explain how his determination to shoot himself had passed, “in view of new facts.” He gave no reasons as he spoke and went into no detail. Nor did the investigators trouble him much this time: plainly, for them too, this was no longer the principal point.

<!-- B09-C05-P111 -->
“We shall verify all this, and return to everything when we question the witnesses, which will of course take place in your presence,” Nikolai Parfenovich said, concluding the interrogation. “For now, allow me to ask you to place here on the table everything you have on you, and above all, all the money currently in your possession.”

<!-- B09-C05-P112 -->
“The money, gentlemen? Certainly, I understand it's necessary. I'm even surprised you weren't curious earlier. True, I couldn't have gone anywhere, I'm sitting in plain sight. Well, here's my money, count it, take it, I think that's all.”

<!-- B09-C05-P113 -->
He took everything out of his pockets, even the small change, drawing two twenty-kopeck pieces from the side pocket of his waistcoat. They counted the money: it came to eight hundred and thirty-six rubles and forty kopecks.

<!-- B09-C05-P114 -->
“And this is everything?” the investigator asked.

<!-- B09-C05-P115 -->
“Everything.”

<!-- B09-C05-P116 -->
“During your testimony just now, you stated that you left three hundred rubles at Plotnikov's shop, gave Perkhotin ten, the driver twenty, lost two hundred here, then...”

<!-- B09-C05-P117 -->
Nikolai Parfenovich enumerated everything. Mitya willingly helped him. They recalled and included every kopeck in the reckoning. Nikolai Parfenovich quickly added it up.

<!-- B09-C05-P118 -->
“So, with these eight hundred, you originally had about fifteen hundred in all?”

<!-- B09-C05-P119 -->
“So it seems,” Mitya snapped.

<!-- B09-C05-P120 -->
“How is it, then, that everyone says there was much more?”

<!-- B09-C05-P121 -->
“Let them say it.”

<!-- B09-C05-P122 -->
“And you said so yourself.”

<!-- B09-C05-P123 -->
“And I said so myself.”

<!-- B09-C05-P124 -->
“We shall verify all this through the testimony of other people who have not yet been questioned. Don't worry about your money; it will be kept in the proper place and will be at your disposal when all this... that has begun... is over, if it turns out—or, so to speak, is proved—that you have an indisputable right to it. Well, sir, and now...”

<!-- B09-C05-P125 -->
Nikolai Parfenovich suddenly rose and firmly announced to Mitya that he was “compelled and obliged” to carry out the most detailed and thorough examination “both of your clothing and of everything...”

<!-- B09-C05-P126 -->
“Certainly, gentlemen, I'll turn out every pocket if you wish.”

<!-- B09-C05-P127 -->
And he actually began turning out his pockets.

<!-- B09-C05-P128 -->
“You will have to remove your clothes as well.”

<!-- B09-C05-P129 -->
“What? Undress? Pah, the devil! Search me as I am! Can't you do it like this?”

<!-- B09-C05-P130 -->
“Quite impossible, Dmitri Fyodorovich. You must remove your clothes.”

<!-- B09-C05-P131 -->
“As you please,” Mitya submitted gloomily, “only not here, please, but behind the curtains. Who will examine me?”

<!-- B09-C05-P132 -->
“Behind the curtains, of course,” Nikolai Parfenovich bowed his head in agreement. His little face even assumed an air of special gravity.
