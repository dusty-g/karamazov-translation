# Book II, Chapter VI
## Why Does Such a Man Live!

*Part I · Book II: An Inappropriate Gathering*

<!-- B02-C06-P001 -->
Dmitri Fyodorovich was a young man of twenty-eight, of medium height and with a pleasant face, yet he looked much older than his years. He was muscular, and one could guess at his considerable physical strength; nevertheless, there was something almost sickly in his face. It was lean, with hollow cheeks and an unhealthy yellowish tinge. His rather large, prominent dark eyes seemed to look out with firm determination, yet there was something unfocused about them. Even when he grew excited and spoke irritably, his eyes seemed not to obey his inward mood but to express something else, sometimes something quite at odds with the moment. “It's hard to tell what he's thinking,” people who spoke with him would sometimes say. Some, seeing something brooding and gloomy in his eyes, would suddenly be startled by his laughter, which revealed the cheerful, playful thoughts he had been entertaining at the very moment he looked so gloomy. Still, the somewhat sickly look of his face just now was understandable: everyone knew or had heard of the extraordinarily troubled, “carousing” life he had been leading here of late, and everyone also knew of the extraordinary pitch of anger he had reached in his quarrels with his father over the disputed money. Several stories about it were already going around town. It was true that he was irritable by nature, too, “with an abrupt and erratic mind,” as our justice of the peace, Semyon Ivanovich Kachalnikov, had characteristically described him at a gathering here. He came in impeccably and smartly dressed, his frock coat buttoned, wearing black gloves and carrying a top hat. Having only recently retired from military service, he wore a mustache and still shaved his beard. His dark blond hair was cropped short and somehow brushed forward at the temples. He walked with long, decisive military strides. He paused a moment on the threshold, glanced around at everyone, then went straight to the elder, guessing him to be the host. He bowed deeply and asked for his blessing. The elder rose a little and blessed him; Dmitri Fyodorovich respectfully kissed his hand and said with extraordinary agitation, almost irritably:

<!-- B02-C06-P002 -->
“Please be generous and forgive me for keeping you waiting so long. But when I pressed the servant Smerdyakov, whom Father sent, about the time, he told me twice, in the most definite terms, that it was set for one o'clock. Now I suddenly learn…”

<!-- B02-C06-P003 -->
“Don't trouble yourself,” the elder interrupted. “It's all right, you've been a little delayed, no harm done…”

<!-- B02-C06-P004 -->
“I'm exceedingly grateful, and could have expected no less of your kindness.” Dmitri Fyodorovich cut the matter short with these words and bowed again; then, turning abruptly toward his “dear father,” he gave him the same deep, respectful bow. It was clear that he had thought out this bow beforehand, and sincerely, considering it his duty to express his respect and good intentions in this way. Though caught off guard, Fyodor Pavlovich immediately recovered in his own fashion: in response to Dmitri Fyodorovich's bow, he sprang out of his armchair and returned his son a bow just as deep. His face suddenly assumed a solemn, imposing air, which, however, made him look positively malicious. Then, with a silent bow to everyone else in the room, Dmitri Fyodorovich walked over to the window in his long, decisive strides, sat down on the only chair left, not far from Father Paisius, and, leaning his whole body forward in the chair, at once prepared to listen to the conversation he had interrupted.

<!-- B02-C06-P005 -->
Dmitri Fyodorovich's arrival had taken no more than a couple of minutes, and the conversation was bound to resume. This time, however, Pyotr Alexandrovich did not consider it necessary to answer Father Paisius's insistent, almost irritable question.

<!-- B02-C06-P006 -->
“Allow me to drop the subject,” he said with a certain worldly nonchalance. “It's a complicated subject, besides. Ivan Fyodorovich is smirking at us: no doubt he has something interesting to say about this, too. Ask him.”

<!-- B02-C06-P007 -->
“Nothing in particular, apart from one small observation,” Ivan Fyodorovich replied at once, “which is that European liberalism in general, and even our own Russian liberal dabbling, has long had a habit of confusing the ultimate outcomes of socialism with those of Christianity. This preposterous inference is, of course, characteristic. It turns out, however, that liberals and dabblers aren't the only ones to confuse socialism with Christianity: in many cases the gendarmes do, too—foreign gendarmes, of course. Your Paris anecdote is quite characteristic, Pyotr Alexandrovich.”

<!-- B02-C06-P008 -->
“Again, I ask leave to drop the subject altogether,” Pyotr Alexandrovich repeated, “and instead, gentlemen, I'll tell you another anecdote, about Ivan Fyodorovich himself, an exceedingly interesting and characteristic one. Only five days or so ago, at a local gathering consisting mainly of ladies, he solemnly declared, in the course of an argument, that there is absolutely nothing on earth that compels people to love their fellow human beings, that no such law of nature exists as that a man should love mankind, and that if there is, or ever has been, love on earth, it comes from no natural law, but solely from people's belief in their own immortality. Ivan Fyodorovich added, in parentheses, that this is precisely what the whole law of nature amounts to, so that if you destroy mankind's belief in its own immortality, not only love but every living force that sustains the life of the world will immediately dry up. What's more, nothing will be immoral anymore; everything will be permitted, even anthropophagy. Nor did he stop there: he concluded by asserting that for every individual who believes neither in God nor in his own immortality—one of us here, for instance—the moral law of nature must immediately turn into the complete opposite of the former, religious law, and that selfishness, even to the point of wickedness, must not only be permitted to a man but be recognized as the necessary, the most reasonable, almost the noblest course open to him in his situation. From such a paradox, gentlemen, you may judge all the rest of what our dear eccentric and lover of paradoxes, Ivan Fyodorovich, is pleased to proclaim, and may yet intend to proclaim.”

<!-- B02-C06-P009 -->
“Wait!” Dmitri Fyodorovich suddenly cried out. “Let me be sure I heard correctly: ‘Wickedness must not only be permitted but be recognized as the most necessary and most intelligent way out of the situation for any atheist’! Is that right or not?”

<!-- B02-C06-P010 -->
“Exactly right,” said Father Paisius.

<!-- B02-C06-P011 -->
“I'll remember that.”

<!-- B02-C06-P012 -->
Having said this, Dmitri Fyodorovich fell silent as suddenly as he had burst into the conversation. Everyone looked at him with curiosity.

<!-- B02-C06-P013 -->
“Do you really believe that this is what will happen when people's faith in the immortality of their souls dries up?” the elder suddenly asked Ivan Fyodorovich.

<!-- B02-C06-P014 -->
“Yes, that was my assertion. There is no virtue if there is no immortality.”

<!-- B02-C06-P015 -->
“Blessed are you if that is your faith—or else you are very unhappy!”

<!-- B02-C06-P016 -->
“Why unhappy?” Ivan Fyodorovich smiled.

<!-- B02-C06-P017 -->
“Because, in all likelihood, you yourself believe neither in the immortality of your soul nor even in what you have written about the church and the question of the church.”

<!-- B02-C06-P018 -->
“Perhaps you're right!… Still, I wasn't entirely joking…” Ivan Fyodorovich suddenly made this strange admission, flushing as he did so.

<!-- B02-C06-P019 -->
“Not entirely joking, that is true. This idea has not yet been settled in your heart, and it torments your heart. But even a sufferer sometimes likes to amuse himself with his despair, out of despair, as it were. For the time being, you too amuse yourself out of despair—with magazine articles and arguments in society—without believing in your own dialectic, smiling at it to yourself with an aching heart… This question remains unsettled within you, and that is your great sorrow, for it urgently demands an answer…”

<!-- B02-C06-P020 -->
“But can it be settled within me? Settled in the affirmative?” Ivan Fyodorovich went on with his strange questions, still looking at the elder with that inexplicable smile.

<!-- B02-C06-P021 -->
“If it cannot be settled in the affirmative, it will never be settled in the negative either. You know this quality of your own heart, and therein lies all its torment. But thank the Creator for giving you a lofty heart capable of suffering such torment, of ‘setting your mind on things above and seeking things above, for our citizenship is in heaven.’ May God grant that the matter be resolved in your heart while you are still on earth, and may God bless your paths!”

<!-- B02-C06-P022 -->
The elder raised his hand and was about to make the sign of the cross over Ivan Fyodorovich from his seat. But Ivan suddenly rose from his chair, went up to him, received his blessing, kissed his hand, and returned silently to his place. He looked firm and serious. This action, and indeed the whole preceding conversation with the elder, so unexpected from Ivan Fyodorovich, somehow struck everyone by its mysteriousness and even a kind of solemnity, so that for a moment they all fell silent, while something almost like fear appeared on Alyosha's face. But Miusov suddenly shrugged, and at that same moment Fyodor Pavlovich sprang up from his chair.

<!-- B02-C06-P023 -->
“Divine and most holy elder!” he cried, pointing at Ivan Fyodorovich. “This is my son, flesh of my flesh, my most beloved flesh! This is my most respectful Karl Moor, so to speak, while the son who has just come in, Dmitri Fyodorovich, against whom I seek redress from you, is my most disrespectful Franz Moor—both out of Schiller's *The Robbers*—and I, I myself, in that case, am the *Regierender Graf von Moor*![^b02-c06-n001] Judge between us and save us! We need not only your prayers but your prophecies.”

<!-- B02-C06-P024 -->
“Speak without playing the holy fool, and do not begin by insulting your family,” the elder replied in a weak, exhausted voice. He was visibly growing more and more tired, and his strength was plainly failing.

<!-- B02-C06-P025 -->
“A disgraceful farce, just as I suspected on my way here!” Dmitri Fyodorovich exclaimed indignantly, also springing to his feet. “Forgive me, reverend Father,” he said to the elder. “I'm an uneducated man and don't even know how to address you, but you've been deceived, and you've been too kind in allowing us to meet here. All my father wants is a scandal; what for is his own calculation. He always has a calculation of his own. But I think I now know what for…”

<!-- B02-C06-P026 -->
“They all accuse me, every one of them!” Fyodor Pavlovich shouted in his turn. “Pyotr Alexandrovich accuses me, too. You did, Pyotr Alexandrovich, you did!” He suddenly turned on Miusov, who had not even thought of interrupting him. “They accuse me of hiding the children's money in my boot and taking as much as I gave; but excuse me, don't we have courts? They'll reckon it all up for you there, Dmitri Fyodorovich, from your own receipts, letters, and agreements: how much you had, how much you've squandered, and how much you have left! Why does Pyotr Alexandrovich decline to give his judgment? Dmitri Fyodorovich is no stranger to him. Because they're all against me, whereas when everything's reckoned up, Dmitri Fyodorovich actually owes me money, and no trifling sum either, but several thousand, sir, for which I have all the documents! Why, the whole town is ringing and roaring with his carousals! And where he used to serve, he paid out a thousand, two thousand at a time for seducing respectable young girls. We know all about that, Dmitri Fyodorovich, sir, down to the most secret details, and I can prove it, sir… Most holy Father, would you believe it, he made the noblest of young women fall in love with him, a girl from a good family, with a fortune, the daughter of his former commanding officer, a brave colonel with a distinguished record, who wore the Order of St. Anne with Swords around his neck. He compromised the girl by offering her his hand; now she's here, now she's an orphan, his fiancée, and under her very eyes he goes visiting a local seductress. But although this seductress did live, so to speak, in a civil marriage with a respectable man, she has an independent character, a fortress no one can breach, just as good as a lawful wife, because she is virtuous—yes, sir! Holy fathers, she is virtuous! And Dmitri Fyodorovich wants to unlock this fortress with a golden key, which is why he bullies me now, trying to wring money out of me, and meanwhile he's already thrown away thousands on this seductress. That's why he's always borrowing money, and from whom, among others, do you suppose? Shall I tell them, Mitya, or shall I not?”

<!-- B02-C06-P027 -->
“Be quiet!” Dmitri Fyodorovich shouted. “Wait until I've left, and don't you dare soil the name of that noble girl in my presence… Your daring even to mention her is a disgrace to her. I won't allow it!”

<!-- B02-C06-P028 -->
He was gasping for breath.

<!-- B02-C06-P029 -->
“Mitya! Mitya!” Fyodor Pavlovich cried in a trembling voice, squeezing out tears. “And what about a parent's blessing? What if I curse you? What then?”

<!-- B02-C06-P030 -->
“You shameless fraud!” Dmitri Fyodorovich roared furiously.

<!-- B02-C06-P031 -->
“That's to his father, his father! What must he do to other people? Gentlemen, imagine this: there's a poor but respectable man here, a retired captain. He met with misfortune and was dismissed from the service, but without public disgrace, without a trial, his honor quite intact, and he's burdened with a large family. Three weeks ago, our Dmitri Fyodorovich seized him by the beard in a tavern, dragged him out into the street by that very beard, and beat him there in front of everyone, all because he serves as my confidential agent in a little matter.”

<!-- B02-C06-P032 -->
“It's all a lie! True on the outside, a lie inside!” Dmitri Fyodorovich was trembling all over with anger. “Father! I don't justify my actions; yes, I admit it before everyone: I treated that captain like a beast, and now I regret it and loathe myself for my beastly rage. But this captain of yours, this agent of yours, went to that very lady whom you call a seductress and proposed on your behalf that she take the promissory notes of mine that you hold and bring a claim against me, to have me locked up on those notes if I pestered you too much about settling the accounts of my property. And now you reproach me for having a weakness for this lady, when you yourself taught her to lure me in! Why, she tells it to my face, she told me herself, laughing at you! And you want me locked up only because you're jealous of me over her, because you've started making advances to this woman yourself, with your love, and I know all about that, too, and again she laughed—do you hear?—she told me about it laughing at you. So there you have him, holy men, this man, this father reproaching his dissolute son! Gentlemen who witness this, forgive my anger, but I had a foreboding that this cunning old man had called you all here for a scandal. I came intending to forgive him, if he held out his hand to me, to forgive and ask forgiveness! But since he has just insulted not only me but the noblest of young women, whose very name I dare not take in vain out of reverence for her, I have decided to expose his whole game in public, even if he is my father!…”

<!-- B02-C06-P033 -->
He could go no further. His eyes flashed; he struggled for breath. But everyone in the cell was agitated now. All except the elder rose uneasily from their seats. The hieromonks looked stern, but waited for the elder's wishes. He sat there very pale now, from the weakness of his illness rather than agitation. A pleading smile shone on his lips; now and then he raised a hand, as if wishing to stop the raging men, and surely one gesture from him would have been enough to end the scene. But he himself seemed still to be waiting for something and was watching intently, as if he wanted to understand something more, as if something were not yet clear to him. At last Pyotr Alexandrovich Miusov felt utterly humiliated and disgraced.

<!-- B02-C06-P034 -->
“We are all to blame for this scandal!” he said heatedly. “Still, I had no foreboding of it on my way here, though I knew whom I was dealing with… This must stop at once! Your Reverence, believe me, I did not know the precise details of everything that has come out here; I refused to believe them, and only now am I learning them for the first time… A father is jealous of his son over a woman of bad character, and conspires with this same creature to have his son put in prison… And this is the company I was forced to come here with… I've been deceived, I declare to everyone that I've been deceived as much as anyone else…”

<!-- B02-C06-P035 -->
“Dmitri Fyodorovich!” Fyodor Pavlovich suddenly bawled in a voice that scarcely sounded like his own. “If only you weren't my son, I'd challenge you to a duel this instant… with pistols, at three paces… across a handkerchief! Across a handkerchief!” he finished, stamping both feet.

<!-- B02-C06-P036 -->
Old liars who have spent their whole lives acting have moments when they get so carried away with their performance that they truly tremble and weep with emotion, even though at that very moment—or just one second later—they could whisper to themselves: “You're lying, you shameless old man; you're acting even now, for all your ‘holy’ anger and this ‘holy’ moment of rage.”

<!-- B02-C06-P037 -->
Dmitri Fyodorovich scowled terribly and looked at his father with inexpressible contempt.

<!-- B02-C06-P038 -->
“I thought… I thought,” he said in a strangely quiet, restrained voice, “that I would come home with the angel of my soul, my fiancée, to cherish him in his old age, and all I see is a depraved lecher and the vilest playactor!”

<!-- B02-C06-P039 -->
“A duel!” the wretched old man bawled again, choking and spraying spittle with every word. “And you, Pyotr Alexandrovich Miusov, let me tell you, sir, that perhaps in your entire family there is not and never has been a woman nobler and more honorable—do you hear, more honorable—than this ‘creature’ of yours, as you just dared to call her! And you, Dmitri Fyodorovich, traded your fiancée for this same ‘creature,’ which means you've judged for yourself that your fiancée isn't worth the sole of her shoe. That's the sort of creature she is!”

<!-- B02-C06-P040 -->
“For shame!” burst suddenly from Father Joseph.

<!-- B02-C06-P041 -->
“Shameful and disgraceful!” Kalganov suddenly cried. He had been silent all this time; now he was scarlet, and his boyish voice trembled with emotion.

<!-- B02-C06-P042 -->
“Why does such a man live!” Dmitri Fyodorovich growled in a low voice, almost beside himself with rage now, his shoulders raised so high that he was almost hunched over. “No, tell me, can he still be allowed to disgrace the earth with his presence?” He looked around at them all, pointing at the old man. He spoke slowly and evenly.

<!-- B02-C06-P043 -->
“Do you hear, do you monks hear the parricide?” Fyodor Pavlovich rounded on Father Joseph. “There's your answer to ‘For shame’! What is there to be ashamed of? This ‘creature,’ this ‘woman of bad character,’ may be holier than you yourselves, gentlemen hieromonks seeking salvation! She may have fallen in her youth, ground down by her surroundings, but she ‘loved much,’ and Christ forgave the woman who loved much…”

<!-- B02-C06-P044 -->
“Christ did not forgive her for that kind of love…” the gentle Father Joseph burst out impatiently.

<!-- B02-C06-P045 -->
“Yes, for that kind, that very kind, monks, that kind! You sit here saving your souls on cabbage and think you're righteous! You eat your little gudgeons, a gudgeon a day, and think you can buy God with gudgeons!”

<!-- B02-C06-P046 -->
“Impossible, impossible!” came from every side of the cell.

<!-- B02-C06-P047 -->
But this whole scene, which had reached such a disgraceful pitch, ended in the most unexpected way. Suddenly the elder rose. Almost beside himself with fear for the elder and for everyone, Alyosha nevertheless managed to support him by the arm. The elder stepped toward Dmitri Fyodorovich and, coming right up to him, sank to his knees before him. Alyosha thought he had fallen from weakness, but that was not it. On his knees, the elder bowed at Dmitri Fyodorovich's feet—a full, distinct, deliberate bow—and even touched the ground with his forehead. Alyosha was so astonished that he did not even manage to help him as he rose. A faint smile barely glimmered on his lips.

<!-- B02-C06-P048 -->
“Forgive me! Forgive me, everyone!” he said, bowing to his guests on every side in farewell.

<!-- B02-C06-P049 -->
For a few moments Dmitri Fyodorovich stood as if stunned: a bow at his feet—what did it mean? Then he suddenly cried, “Oh God!” and, covering his face with his hands, rushed out of the room. All the guests poured out after him in a crowd, too embarrassed even to say goodbye or bow to their host. Only the hieromonks went up to him again for his blessing.

<!-- B02-C06-P050 -->
“What was that bowing at his feet, then? Some sort of emblem?” Fyodor Pavlovich ventured to begin a conversation, suddenly subdued for some reason, though he dared address no one in particular. They were all passing out of the hermitage enclosure just then.

<!-- B02-C06-P051 -->
“I'm not responsible for a madhouse or its madmen,” Miusov replied bitterly at once, “but I shall rid myself of your company, Fyodor Pavlovich, and forever, believe me. Where's that monk from earlier?…”

<!-- B02-C06-P052 -->
But “that monk,” the one who had invited them to dine with the superior earlier, did not keep them waiting. He had met the guests there the moment they came down the steps from the elder's cell, as though he had been waiting for them all along.

<!-- B02-C06-P053 -->
“Be so kind, reverend Father, as to convey my deepest respects to Father Superior, and offer my personal apologies—Miusov's—to His Very Reverence, for unforeseen circumstances having suddenly arisen, I cannot possibly have the honor of joining him at his table, much as I sincerely wish to,” Pyotr Alexandrovich told the monk irritably.

<!-- B02-C06-P054 -->
“And the unforeseen circumstance is me!” Fyodor Pavlovich immediately put in. “Do you hear, Father? Pyotr Alexandrovich doesn't want to remain in my company, otherwise he'd go at once. And you shall go, Pyotr Alexandrovich, do go along to Father Superior's, and—a good appetite to you! Let me tell you, I'm the one declining, not you. Home, home, I'll eat at home; I don't feel equal to it here, Pyotr Alexandrovich, my dearest relative.”

<!-- B02-C06-P055 -->
“I'm no relative of yours and never have been, you vile man!”

<!-- B02-C06-P056 -->
“I said it on purpose to provoke you, because you refuse to acknowledge the relationship, although you're still a relative, however you wriggle. I'll prove it from the calendar of saints. I'll send horses for you in due course, Ivan Fyodorovich; you stay too, if you wish. As for you, Pyotr Alexandrovich, propriety itself demands that you go to Father Superior's now. We must apologize for the way you and I carried on in there…”

<!-- B02-C06-P057 -->
“Are you really leaving, though? You're not lying?”

<!-- B02-C06-P058 -->
“Pyotr Alexandrovich, how could I dare, after what happened! I got carried away, forgive me, gentlemen, carried away! And besides, I'm shaken! And ashamed, too. Gentlemen, one man has a heart like Alexander the Great's, another like little Fido's. Mine is like little Fido's. I've lost my nerve! How could I go to dinner after such an escapade, to wolf down monastery sauces? I'm ashamed, I can't, forgive me!”

<!-- B02-C06-P059 -->
“The devil knows—what if he's lying!” Miusov stopped, pondering, and followed the departing buffoon with a bewildered look. Fyodor Pavlovich turned, and, seeing that Pyotr Alexandrovich was watching him, blew him a kiss.

<!-- B02-C06-P060 -->
“Are you going to the superior's?” Miusov asked Ivan Fyodorovich curtly.

<!-- B02-C06-P061 -->
“Why not? Besides, the superior gave me a special invitation yesterday.”

<!-- B02-C06-P062 -->
“Unfortunately, I really do feel almost obliged to attend this damned dinner,” Miusov went on with the same bitter irritation, not even noticing that the monk was listening. “At least we ought to apologize there for what we've done here, and explain that it wasn't us… What do you think?”

<!-- B02-C06-P063 -->
“Yes, we ought to explain that it wasn't us. Besides, Father won't be there,” Ivan Fyodorovich observed.

<!-- B02-C06-P064 -->
“Your father there on top of everything! This damned dinner!”

<!-- B02-C06-P065 -->
And yet they all went. The monk listened in silence. On the way through the little wood he observed only once that Father Superior had been expecting them for a long time, and that they were more than half an hour late. No one answered him. Miusov looked at Ivan Fyodorovich with hatred.

<!-- B02-C06-P066 -->
“And he goes to dinner as though nothing had happened!” he thought. “A brazen face and a Karamazov conscience.”
