# Book X: Boys
## I. Kolya Krasotkin

<!-- B10-C01-P001 -->
Early November. The frost had reached eleven degrees here, and the roads were glazed with ice. A little dry snow fell on the frozen ground during the night, and the wind, “dry and cutting,” lifts it and sweeps it along the dreary streets of our little town, especially across the market square. The morning is murky, but the snow has stopped. Not far from the square, near the Plotnikovs’ shop, stands the small house of an official’s widow, Madame Krasotkin, very neat both inside and out. Provincial Secretary Krasotkin himself died long ago, almost fourteen years back, but his widow, a thirty-year-old woman still quite pretty, is alive and living in her neat little house “on her own capital.” She lives respectably and timidly, a gentle but fairly cheerful creature. She was left a widow at about eighteen, having lived with her husband barely a year and only just borne him a son. From the moment of his death she devoted herself entirely to bringing up this little darling of hers, the boy Kolya; and though she had loved him to distraction for all fourteen years, she had certainly endured incomparably more suffering on his account than she had known joy, trembling and all but dying of fear almost every day that he would fall ill, catch cold, get into mischief, climb onto a chair and fall off, and so on, and so on. When Kolya began going to school, and afterward to our junior gymnasium, his mother threw herself into studying every subject alongside him, to help him and go over his lessons; she threw herself into getting acquainted with the teachers and their wives, and even petted and fawned on Kolya’s schoolfellows so that they would not pick on him, make fun of him, or beat him up. She carried this so far that the boys really did begin making fun of him because of her, teasing him for being a mama’s boy. But the boy knew how to stand up for himself. He was a bold youngster, “frightfully strong,” as word soon went round and became firmly established in his class; he was agile, stubborn, and daring and enterprising in spirit. He did well at his lessons, and there was even talk that in arithmetic and world history he could stump the teacher Dardanelov himself. Yet although the boy looked down his nose at everyone, he was a good companion and did not put on airs. He accepted the other boys’ respect as his due, but was friendly with them. Above all, he knew where to stop, knew how to restrain himself when necessary, and in his dealings with authority never crossed that final, inviolable line beyond which a misdemeanor can no longer be tolerated, becoming disorder, rebellion, and lawlessness. And yet he was very, very far from averse to mischief whenever a suitable opportunity arose, mischief worthy of the most ordinary little scamp—or rather, less mischief than some ingenious stunt, some improbable trick, something with “extra spice,” something dashing to show himself off. Above all, he was intensely proud. He had even managed to put his mama in a subordinate position, treating her almost despotically. And she submitted—oh, she had submitted long ago; there was only one thought she could never bear: that her boy “didn’t love her much.” She was forever imagining that Kolya was “unfeeling” toward her, and sometimes, streaming with hysterical tears, she would begin reproaching him for his coldness. The boy disliked this, and the more they demanded outpourings of affection from him, the more unyielding he became, as though on purpose. But it was not on purpose; he could not help it—that was simply his nature. His mother was mistaken: he loved his mama very much; what he disliked was “slobbering sentiment,” as he called it in his schoolboy language. His father had left a bookcase containing a number of books; Kolya loved reading and had already read some of them on his own. His mother saw no harm in this, though she sometimes wondered how the boy could stand for hours at the bookcase over some book instead of going out to play. And so Kolya read certain things he should not yet have been allowed to read at his age. Lately, however, although he disliked taking his pranks beyond a certain point, he had begun playing pranks that seriously frightened his mother—nothing immoral, to be sure, but desperate, reckless escapades. Just that summer, in July, during the holidays, mama and her boy happened to go for a week’s visit to a distant relation in another district, seventy versts away, whose husband worked at a railway station—the same station, the nearest to our town, from which Ivan Fyodorovich Karamazov left for Moscow a month later. There Kolya began by inspecting the railway in detail and studying its routine, realizing how he could shine with his new knowledge among his schoolfellows at the junior gymnasium once he got home. As it happened, several other boys were there at the time, and he made friends with them; some lived at the station, others nearby—six or seven young people altogether, aged from twelve to fifteen, two of them from our own town. The boys played and got up to mischief together, and on the fourth or fifth day of the visit to the station these foolish youngsters made an utterly outrageous bet for two rubles. It was this: Kolya, one of the youngest of them and therefore somewhat despised by the older boys, proposed, out of pride or sheer brazen daring, that at night, when the eleven-o’clock train came, he would lie facedown between the rails and remain motionless while the train rushed over him at full speed. True, they had first checked and found that it really was possible to stretch out and flatten oneself between the rails so that the train would pass over without touching the person lying there; but to stay lying there all the same! Kolya insisted that he would. At first they laughed at him, called him a little liar and a braggart, but that only spurred him on. Above all, these fifteen-year-olds had been far too high and mighty with him, and at first had not even wanted to accept him as a companion because he was “little”—an insult past bearing. So they decided to go out that evening a verst beyond the station, so the train would have time to gather full speed after pulling out. The boys assembled. It was a moonless night, not merely dark but almost black. At the appointed time Kolya lay down between the rails. The other five who had made the bet waited in the bushes beside the track at the foot of the embankment, their hearts sinking, and at last in fear and remorse. Finally the train rumbled in the distance as it pulled out of the station. Two red lamps flashed out of the darkness; the approaching monster thundered. “Run! Get off the rails!” the boys screamed from the bushes, nearly dead with fright, but it was too late: the train bore down and swept past. The boys rushed to Kolya: he lay motionless. They began tugging at him and trying to lift him. Suddenly he got up and walked silently down the embankment. Once below, he declared that he had pretended to be unconscious on purpose to frighten them, but the truth was that he really had lost consciousness, as he himself admitted to his mama much later. His reputation as a “daredevil” was thus established forever. He returned to the house at the station white as a sheet. The next day he had a slight nervous fever, but was enormously cheerful, happy, and pleased with himself. The story did not get out at once, only later, back in our town, where it reached the junior gymnasium and came to the notice of its authorities. But here Kolya’s mama rushed to plead with them for her boy, and in the end the respected and influential teacher Dardanelov defended him and interceded for him, and the matter was dropped as though it had never happened. This Dardanelov, an unmarried man still fairly young, had for years been passionately in love with Madame Krasotkin; a year or so earlier, with the utmost respect and scarcely breathing for fear and delicacy, he had even ventured to offer her his hand. She had refused him outright, considering that to accept would be a betrayal of her boy, although certain mysterious signs perhaps gave Dardanelov some right to dream that he was not altogether repugnant to the charming but excessively chaste and tenderhearted little widow. Kolya’s mad escapade seems to have broken the ice, and in return for his intervention Dardanelov was given a hint of hope—distant hope, it is true, but Dardanelov himself was a marvel of purity and delicacy, and for the present even this was enough to make his happiness complete. He loved the boy, though he would have considered it degrading to curry favor with him, and was strict and demanding with him in class. Kolya, for his part, kept him at a respectful distance, prepared his lessons excellently, stood second in the class, and addressed Dardanelov dryly; and the whole class firmly believed that Kolya was so strong in world history that he could “stump” Dardanelov himself. Indeed, Kolya once asked him, “Who founded Troy?” Dardanelov could only reply in general terms about peoples, their movements and migrations, the depths of antiquity, and mythology; but who exactly had founded Troy—which particular individuals—he could not answer, and for some reason even considered the question idle and unsound. The boys, however, remained convinced that Dardanelov did not know who had founded Troy. Kolya had read about its founders in Smaragdov, which was kept in the bookcase left by his father. In the end even all the other boys became interested: who exactly had founded Troy? But Krasotkin would not disclose his secret, and his reputation for knowledge remained unshakable.

After the incident on the railway, a certain change took place in Kolya’s relations with his mother. When Anna Fyodorovna—the widow Krasotkin—learned of her boy’s exploit, she almost went mad with horror. She had such terrible hysterical fits, recurring at intervals over several days, that Kolya, now seriously frightened himself, gave her his solemn word of honor that such pranks would never happen again. He swore on his knees before the icon, and swore by his father’s memory, as Madame Krasotkin herself demanded; whereupon the “manly” Kolya himself burst into tears like a six-year-old boy, overcome by “feelings,” and all that day mother and son kept throwing themselves into each other’s arms and weeping with convulsive sobs. The next day Kolya woke up as “unfeeling” as ever, but became quieter, more modest, more serious, more thoughtful. True, a month and a half later he was caught up in another prank, and his name even became known to our justice of the peace; but this was an entirely different sort of prank, a funny, rather silly one, and it turned out that he had not even done it himself, but had merely been involved. More of that another time. His mother went on trembling and tormenting herself, and as her anxieties increased, Dardanelov grew more and more hopeful. It must be said that Kolya understood and saw through Dardanelov in this respect and, needless to say, profoundly despised him for his “feelings”; formerly he had even been tactless enough to display this contempt before his mother, with distant hints that he understood what Dardanelov was after. But after the railway incident his behavior changed in this respect too: he no longer allowed himself any hints, even the most distant, and began speaking of Dardanelov more respectfully in his mother’s presence. The sensitive Anna Fyodorovna understood this at once, with boundless gratitude in her heart; yet at the slightest, most casual mention of Dardanelov, even by some visitor, if Kolya was present she would suddenly flush rose-red with shame. At such moments Kolya would either stare frowning out of the window, inspect his boots to see whether they were gaping open, or shout fiercely for Perezvon, a fairly large, shaggy, mangy dog he had suddenly acquired somewhere a month before, dragged into the house, and for some reason kept secretly indoors without showing him to any of his friends. He tyrannized dreadfully over the dog, teaching him all sorts of tricks and accomplishments, until the poor creature howled without him when he went off to school, and when he came back squealed with delight, leaped about like a mad thing, begged, fell to the floor and played dead, and so on—in short, performed all the tricks he had been taught, no longer at anyone’s command but solely from the ardor of his delighted feelings and grateful heart.
