# Book IV: Strains
## VII. And Out in the Fresh Air

<!-- B04-C07-P001 -->
“The air is fresh, sir, whereas in my mansion it really is rather stale, in every sense. Let us walk a little, sir. I should very much like to interest you in my case.”

<!-- B04-C07-P002 -->
“I have an extraordinary matter to discuss with you myself...” Alyosha remarked. “Only I don't know how to begin.”

<!-- B04-C07-P003 -->
“How could I fail to know that you had some business with me, sir? Without business you would never have looked in on me. Or had you really only come to complain about the boy, sir? That's hardly credible. And speaking of the boy: I couldn't explain everything in there, sir, but now, out here, I'll describe that scene for you. You see, the scrubber was thicker only a week ago—I'm speaking of my beard, sir; it's my beard they've nicknamed ‘the scrubber,’ mostly the schoolboys, sir. Well then, your brother Dmitri Fyodorovich was pulling me by my wretched beard, dragged me out of the tavern into the square, and just then the schoolboys were coming out of school, with Ilyusha among them. When he saw me in that state, he rushed up to me: ‘Papa!’ he cries, ‘Papa!’ He clings to me, puts his arms around me, tries to pull me free, and shouts to the man insulting me, ‘Let him go, let him go, that's my papa, my papa, forgive him!’ Yes, that was what he shouted: ‘Forgive him.’ And he caught hold of him too with his little hands, and kissed his hand, that very hand of his, sir... I remember what his little face looked like at that moment, sir. I haven't forgotten, and I never will!..”

<!-- B04-C07-P004 -->
“I swear,” Alyosha exclaimed, “my brother will express his repentance to you as sincerely and fully as possible, even if he has to go down on his knees in that very square... I'll make him, or he's no brother of mine!”

<!-- B04-C07-P005 -->
“Aha, so it's still a project. It doesn't come straight from him, but only from the nobility of your own ardent heart, sir. You should have said so. No, in that case allow me to finish telling you about your brother's own supreme nobility as a knight and an officer, since he gave expression to it then, sir. When he'd finished dragging me by the scrubber, he let me go free. ‘You're an officer,’ he says, ‘and I'm an officer. If you can find a second who's a decent man, send him to me—I'll give you satisfaction, even though you're a scoundrel!’ That was what he said, sir. A truly chivalrous spirit! Ilyusha and I went away then, but that ancestral family portrait has been imprinted forever on Ilyusha's inmost memory. No, how can we go on being gentlemen, sir? And judge for yourself: you have just visited my mansion—what did you see? Three ladies sitting there, sir: one without the use of her legs and feeble-minded; another without the use of her legs and hunchbacked; and the third with legs, but far too clever, a student, sir, eager to get back to Petersburg and seek out the rights of Russian women there on the banks of the Neva. I won't speak of Ilyusha; he's only nine, sir, alone in all this—for if I die, what will become of all those depths? That's the one thing I ask you, sir. And if that's so, suppose I challenge him to a duel and he kills me straight away—what then? What will become of all of them then, sir? Worse still, suppose he doesn't kill me but only cripples me: I couldn't work, yet my mouth would still be there. Who would feed it then, that mouth of mine, and who would feed all of them? Or should I send Ilyusha out begging every day instead of to school? So that is what challenging him to a duel means for me, sir: a foolish phrase and nothing more.”

<!-- B04-C07-P006 -->
“He will ask your forgiveness, he will bow down at your feet in the middle of the square!” Alyosha cried again, his eyes blazing.

<!-- B04-C07-P007 -->
“I wanted to take him to court,” the staff captain continued, “but open our law code: how much satisfaction would I get from the man who personally insulted me, sir? Then suddenly Agrafena Alexandrovna summons me and shouts, ‘Don't you dare think of it! If you take him to court, I'll arrange it so that the whole world finds out publicly that he beat you for your own fraud, and then you'll be the one they put on trial.’ And God alone sees who that fraud came from, sir, and on whose orders I acted as a mere small fry—wasn't it her own orders and Fyodor Pavlovich's? ‘Besides,’ she adds, ‘I'll dismiss you for good, and you'll never earn another penny from me. I'll tell my merchant too’—that's what she calls the old man, ‘my merchant’—‘and he'll dismiss you as well.’ So I think: if the merchant dismisses me too, what then, who can I earn a living from? Those two are all I have left, since your father Fyodor Pavlovich has not only stopped trusting me, for an unrelated reason, sir, but actually means to drag me into court himself, having got hold of my signed receipts. As a result of all this I quieted down, sir, and you have seen the depths. Now allow me to ask: did he bite your finger very badly earlier, did Ilyusha? In the mansion I didn't dare go into that detail in front of him.”

<!-- B04-C07-P008 -->
“Yes, very badly, and he was very angry. He took revenge on me for you because I'm a Karamazov; I see that clearly now. But if only you'd seen him exchanging stones with his schoolmates! It's very dangerous; they could kill him. They're children, they don't think. A stone flies through the air and could break his skull.”

<!-- B04-C07-P009 -->
“He's already been hit, sir, if not in the head then in the chest, just above the heart. A stone struck him today, left a bruise; he came home crying and groaning, and now he's ill.”

<!-- B04-C07-P010 -->
“And you know, he's the one who attacks them all first. He's become bitter on your behalf. They say he stabbed one boy, Krasotkin, in the side with a penknife earlier...”

<!-- B04-C07-P011 -->
“I've heard about that too. Dangerous, sir: Krasotkin is an official here; there may still be trouble over it...”

<!-- B04-C07-P012 -->
“I would advise you,” Alyosha went on ardently, “not to send him to school at all for a while, until he settles down... and this anger in him passes...”

<!-- B04-C07-P013 -->
“Anger, sir!” the staff captain took up. “Exactly—anger. Such great anger in such a little creature. You don't know the whole of it, sir. Allow me to explain this particular story. The thing is, after that incident all the boys at school began teasing him, calling him ‘Scrubber.’ Schoolchildren are merciless people: angels of God on their own, but together, especially at school, very often merciless. They began teasing him, and Ilyusha's noble spirit rose up. An ordinary boy, a weak son, would have submitted, would have been ashamed of his father, but this one stood up for his father alone against them all. For his father and for truth, sir, for justice. Because what he endured then, when he kissed your brother's hands and cried, ‘Forgive Papa, forgive Papa’—only God and I know that, sir. And that is how our children—I mean not yours, but ours, sir, the children of despised but honorable beggars—learn the truth of life on earth at nine years old. What do the rich know? They never explore such depths in all their lives; but my Ilyushka, at that very moment in the square, when he was kissing his hands, passed through the whole truth in that one instant. That truth entered him, sir, and crushed him forever,” the staff captain said heatedly, once again almost beside himself, striking his right fist into his left palm as though to give a visible demonstration of how “the truth” had crushed his Ilyusha. “That very day he had a fever, sir, and was delirious all night. He hardly spoke to me all day, was quite silent even; I only noticed him looking and looking at me from his corner. Mostly he kept close to the window, pretending to study his lessons, but I could see it wasn't his lessons he had on his mind. The next day I drank, sir, and don't remember much—a sinful man, out of grief, sir. Mama began crying too—I love Mama very much, sir—and so, out of grief, I drank away my last pennies. Don't despise me, sir: here in Russia our drunken people are the kindest. Our kindest people are also the drunkest. I lay there and don't remember much of Ilyusha that day, and that was the very day the boys began making fun of him at school, first thing in the morning. ‘Scrubber!’ they shout at him. ‘They dragged your father out of the tavern by his scrubber, and you ran beside him begging forgiveness.’ The third day he came home from school again, and I looked—he was white as a sheet. ‘What's the matter?’ I ask. Not a word. Well, we couldn't talk in the mansion, or Mama and the young ladies would get involved at once—and the young ladies already knew everything, had known from the very first day. Varvara Nikolavna had already begun grumbling: ‘Fools, clowns, can anything sensible ever come of you?’ ‘Quite so,’ I say, ‘Varvara Nikolavna, can anything sensible ever come of us?’ That got me out of it for the time being. Toward evening I took the boy out for a walk. You must know, sir, that even before this we went out walking every evening, along exactly this route we're taking now, from our own gate to that great stone over there, lying all alone like an orphan beside the wattle fence, where the town pasture begins. A lovely, deserted place, sir. Ilyusha and I were walking, his hand in mine as usual; such a tiny hand he has, such thin, cold little fingers—he suffers with his chest, you see. ‘Papa,’ he says, ‘Papa!’ ‘What?’ I ask; I see his eyes are shining. ‘Papa, what he did to you then, Papa!’ ‘What can we do, Ilyusha?’ I say. ‘Don't make peace with him, Papa, don't. The schoolboys say he gave you ten rubles for it.’ ‘No, Ilyusha,’ I say, ‘I wouldn't take money from him now for anything.’ He began shaking all over, seized my hand in both of his and kissed it again. ‘Papa,’ he says, ‘Papa, challenge him to a duel. At school they tease me that you're a coward and won't challenge him, that you'll take ten rubles from him instead.’ ‘I can't challenge him to a duel, Ilyusha,’ I answer, and briefly explain to him everything I've just explained to you about that. He listened. ‘Papa,’ he says, ‘Papa, don't make peace with him all the same. I'll grow up, I'll challenge him myself and kill him!’ His eyes were shining and burning. Well, for all that, I am his father; I had to tell him a word of truth. ‘It's a sin to kill,’ I tell him, ‘even in a duel.’ ‘Papa,’ he says, ‘Papa, when I'm big I'll knock him down. I'll knock his saber out of his hand with my saber, rush at him, knock him down, raise my saber over him and say: I could kill you now, but I forgive you—there!’ Do you see, sir, do you see what a process had been going on in that little head over those two days? Day and night he had been thinking of this very revenge with the saber, and that must have been what he raved about in the night. Only he began coming home from school badly beaten; I learned the whole story the day before yesterday, and you're right, sir: I won't send him to that school anymore. I discovered he was going against the whole class by himself, challenging everyone; he had turned angry himself, his heart was on fire, and then I grew frightened for him. Again we're out walking. ‘Papa,’ he asks, ‘Papa, aren't rich people the most powerful people in the world?’ ‘Yes, Ilyusha,’ I say, ‘there's nobody in the world more powerful than a rich man.’ ‘Papa,’ he says, ‘I'll get rich, I'll become an officer and defeat everyone, the tsar will reward me, I'll come home, and then nobody will dare...’ Then he was quiet a while and said—his little lips were still trembling—‘Papa,’ he says, ‘what a bad town ours is, Papa!’ ‘Yes, Ilyushechka,’ I say, ‘our town isn't very good.’ ‘Papa, let's move to another town, a good town,’ he says, ‘where they don't even know about us.’ ‘We will,’ I say, ‘we will, Ilyusha, as soon as I've saved some money.’ I was glad of the chance to turn him away from dark thoughts, and we began dreaming together of how we'd move to another town, buy a horse of our own and a cart. We'll put Mama and your sisters in it, cover them up, and we'll walk alongside; now and then I'll lift you in, while I walk beside you, because we must spare our horse, we can't all ride at once, and that's how we'll set off. He was delighted with this, especially with having a horse of his own and getting to ride it himself. And of course a Russian boy is born together with a horse. We talked for a long time. Thank God, I thought, I've distracted him, comforted him. That was the evening before last; yesterday evening it was different. He went to that school again in the morning and came back gloomy, very gloomy. In the evening I took his hand and led him out for a walk. He was silent, wouldn't speak. A breeze had come up, the sun was hidden, there was a breath of autumn in the air, and it was already growing dark. We walked, both of us sad. ‘Well, my boy,’ I say, ‘how shall we get ready for our journey?’ I was hoping to bring him back to yesterday's conversation. Not a word. Only I felt his little fingers tremble in my hand. ‘Ah,’ I thought, ‘this is bad; there's something new.’ We reached this very stone, just as we have now, and I sat on it. There were kites up in the sky, humming and rattling; you could see thirty of them. It's kite season now, sir. ‘Look, Ilyusha,’ I say, ‘it's time we flew last year's kite too. Let me mend it—where have you hidden it?’ My boy was silent, looking away, standing sideways to me. Then the wind suddenly boomed and the sand blew up... All at once he flung himself at me, put both little arms around my neck and held me tight. You know, when children are silent and proud and keep fighting back their tears for a long time, and then suddenly break down because some great grief has come, the tears don't simply flow, sir; they gush out in streams. Those warm splashes suddenly soaked my whole face. He began sobbing as though in convulsions, shaking, holding me tight while I sat on the stone. ‘Papa!’ he cries, ‘Papa, dear Papa, how he humiliated you!’ Then I began sobbing too, sir, and we sat there shaking in each other's arms. ‘Papa,’ he says, ‘Papa!’ ‘Ilyusha,’ I say, ‘Ilyushechka!’ Nobody saw us then, sir; only God saw, and perhaps he'll enter it in my service record. Thank your brother, Alexei Fyodorovich. No, sir, I will not flog my boy to give you satisfaction!”

<!-- B04-C07-P014 -->
He ended with the same spiteful, clownish twist as before. Alyosha felt, however, that the man now trusted him, and that had someone else been in his place, he would not have “talked” to that person like this or told him what he had just told Alyosha. This encouraged Alyosha, whose soul was trembling with tears.

<!-- B04-C07-P015 -->
“Oh, how I wish I could make peace with your boy!” he exclaimed. “If only you could arrange it.”

<!-- B04-C07-P016 -->
“Quite so, sir,” the staff captain murmured.

<!-- B04-C07-P017 -->
“But that isn't it now, that isn't it at all. Listen,” Alyosha went on exclaiming, “listen. I have an errand to you: this same brother of mine, this Dmitri, has also insulted his fiancée, a most noble girl whom you've probably heard of. I have the right to tell you about her humiliation; indeed, I must, because when she heard about the insult to you and learned all about your unhappy circumstances, she asked me just now... earlier... to bring you this assistance from her—but from her alone, not from Dmitri, who has abandoned her too, certainly not, and not from me, his brother, or from anyone else, but from her, from her alone! She begs you to accept her help... You have both been wronged by the same man... She only thought of you when she had suffered the same kind of insult from him—as severe an insult—as you had! This means a sister is coming to help a brother... She specifically asked me to persuade you to accept these two hundred rubles from her as from a sister. Nobody will know about it, there can be no unfair gossip. Here are the two hundred rubles, and I swear you must accept them, otherwise... otherwise everyone in the world must be enemies! But there are brothers in the world too. You have a noble soul, you must understand that, you must!”

<!-- B04-C07-P018 -->
And Alyosha held out two brand-new rainbow-colored hundred-ruble notes. They were standing just by the great stone near the fence, and there was nobody around. The banknotes seemed to have an overwhelming effect on the staff captain. He started, though at first apparently only in surprise: he had never imagined anything like this, had never expected such an outcome. Help from anyone, let alone so much help, had never occurred to him even in a dream. He took the notes and for almost a minute could not answer; something entirely new flashed across his face.

<!-- B04-C07-P019 -->
“For me, this is for me, sir, all this money, two hundred rubles! Good heavens! I haven't seen so much money for four years, Lord! And she says she's a sister... and it's true, is it true?”

<!-- B04-C07-P020 -->
“I swear to you that everything I've told you is true!” Alyosha cried. The staff captain blushed.

<!-- B04-C07-P021 -->
“Listen, my dear fellow, listen, sir: if I accept, I won't be a scoundrel, will I? In your eyes, Alexei Fyodorovich, I won't be, I won't be a scoundrel? No, sir, Alexei Fyodorovich, hear me out, hear me out,” he hurried on, continually touching Alyosha with both hands. “You persuade me to accept by saying a ‘sister’ has sent it, but inside, to yourself, you won't feel contempt for me if I accept, will you?”

<!-- B04-C07-P022 -->
“No, no! I swear by my salvation I won't! And nobody will ever know, only us: me, you and her, and one other lady, a very close friend of hers...”

<!-- B04-C07-P023 -->
“What does the lady matter! Listen, Alexei Fyodorovich, hear me out, sir. The moment has come when you must hear me out, because you can't even understand what these two hundred rubles could mean to me now,” the poor man continued, gradually entering a kind of confused, almost wild rapture. He seemed bewildered, and spoke in a frantic hurry, as though afraid he would not be allowed to say everything. “Besides the fact that this has been honestly obtained, from such a respected and saintly ‘sister,’ do you know that now I can get treatment for Mama and Ninochka—my little hunchbacked angel, my daughter? Doctor Herzenstube came to see me out of the kindness of his heart and examined them both for a whole hour. ‘I don't understand anything,’ he says. But all the same, mineral water, which they have at the pharmacy here—he prescribed it—will certainly benefit her; he also prescribed medicated footbaths. The mineral water costs thirty kopecks, and she may need to drink forty jugs. So I took the prescription and put it on the shelf under the icons, and there it lies. For Ninochka he prescribed baths in some kind of solution, hot baths, every morning and evening. But how could we contrive such treatment, sir, in our mansion, without a servant, without help, without vessels or water? And Ninochka is full of rheumatism—I haven't told you yet. At night her whole right side aches, she suffers, and would you believe it, the angel of God holds out so as not to disturb us, doesn't moan for fear of waking us. We eat whatever comes our way, whatever we can get, and she takes the very worst scrap, something you could only throw to a dog, as though to say, ‘I don't deserve this piece, I'm taking it from you, I sit here as a burden to you.’ That's what her angelic eyes are trying to express. We attend to her and it weighs on her: ‘I don't deserve it, I don't deserve it, I'm an unworthy cripple, useless.’ And she not deserve it, sir, when she has won God's mercy for us all through her angelic gentleness! Without her, without her quiet word, ours would be a hell, sir; she's softened even Varya. And don't judge Varvara Nikolavna either, sir; she's an angel too, and wronged too. She came to us in the summer with sixteen rubles she had earned giving lessons and put aside for her journey, so she could return to Petersburg in September—that is, now. But we took her money and lived on it, and now she has nothing to go back on, that's how it is, sir. And she couldn't go back anyway, because she works for us like a convict—we've harnessed and saddled her like a worn-out horse. She looks after everyone, mends, washes, sweeps the floor, puts Mama to bed, and Mama is capricious, sir, Mama is tearful, sir, Mama is mad!.. But now, with these two hundred rubles, I can hire a servant, sir; do you understand, Alexei Fyodorovich? I can undertake the treatment of my dear ones, sir, send our student to Petersburg, buy beef, give us a new diet. Lord, why, it's a dream!”

<!-- B04-C07-P024 -->
Alyosha was immensely glad that he had brought so much happiness and that the poor man had agreed to be made happy.

<!-- B04-C07-P025 -->
“Wait, Alexei Fyodorovich, wait!” The staff captain seized on another dream that had suddenly occurred to him and began rattling on again at a frantic pace. “Do you know, perhaps Ilyushka and I really can make our dream come true now: buy a horse and a covered cart—a black horse, he insisted it must be black—and set off just as we described the day before yesterday. I know a lawyer in K. Province, sir, a friend from childhood. A reliable person passed word to me that if I came, he would supposedly give me a post as a clerk in his office; well, who knows, perhaps he would. So I could put Mama in, put Ninochka in, set Ilyushechka to drive, and I would walk along, walk along, and take them all away, sir. Lord, if I could only collect one bad debt here, there might even be enough for that!”

<!-- B04-C07-P026 -->
“There will be enough, enough!” Alyosha exclaimed. “Katerina Ivanovna will send you more, as much as you like, and you know, I have money too. Take as much as you need, as from a brother, as from a friend; you can pay it back later... You'll get rich, you'll get rich! And you know, you couldn't possibly think of anything better than moving to another province! That will be your salvation, above all for your boy—and you know, the sooner the better, before winter, before the cold. You could write to us from there, and we would remain brothers. No, this isn't a dream!”

<!-- B04-C07-P027 -->
Alyosha was so pleased that he was about to embrace him. But when he looked at him he suddenly stopped. The captain stood with his neck stretched out and his lips protruding, his face frantic and pale, moving his lips in a whisper as though trying to say something; no sound came, but his lips kept whispering. It was somehow strange.

<!-- B04-C07-P028 -->
“What's the matter?” Alyosha suddenly started, for some reason.

<!-- B04-C07-P029 -->
“Alexei Fyodorovich... I... you...” the staff captain mumbled and faltered, staring strangely and wildly straight into his face, looking like a man who had resolved to fling himself off a mountain, while his lips seemed almost to smile. “I, sir... you, sir... Would you like me to show you a little trick now, sir?” he suddenly whispered in a quick, firm whisper; his words no longer faltered.

<!-- B04-C07-P030 -->
“What little trick?”

<!-- B04-C07-P031 -->
“A trick, a bit of hocus-pocus,” the staff captain kept whispering. His mouth twisted to the left, his left eye narrowed, and he went on staring at Alyosha without looking away, as if chained to him.

<!-- B04-C07-P032 -->
“But what's the matter with you? What trick?” Alyosha cried, now thoroughly frightened.

<!-- B04-C07-P033 -->
“This one—look!” the staff captain suddenly shrieked.

<!-- B04-C07-P034 -->
He showed him the two rainbow-colored banknotes, which throughout their entire conversation he had held together by one corner between the thumb and forefinger of his right hand; then, with a sudden kind of frenzy, he snatched them up, crumpled them and clenched them tightly in his right fist.

<!-- B04-C07-P035 -->
“Did you see, sir, did you see?” he shrieked at Alyosha, pale and frenzied, and suddenly raised his fist and hurled both crumpled banknotes onto the sand with all his might. “Did you see, sir?” he shrieked again, pointing at them. “Well then, take that, sir!..”

<!-- B04-C07-P036 -->
Suddenly raising his right foot, he began furiously stamping on them with his heel, crying out and gasping with every blow of his foot.

<!-- B04-C07-P037 -->
“There's your money, sir! There's your money, sir! There's your money, sir! There's your money, sir!” Suddenly he sprang backward and straightened up before Alyosha. His whole figure expressed an indescribable pride.

<!-- B04-C07-P038 -->
“Report to those who sent you that the scrubber does not sell his honor, sir!” he cried, flinging out his arm into the air. Then he turned quickly and broke into a run, but before he had gone five paces he turned all the way around again and suddenly waved to Alyosha. And again, before he had run another five paces, he turned for the last time; now there was no twisted laughter in his face—on the contrary, it was shaking all over with tears. In a weeping, breaking, choking rush he shouted:

<!-- B04-C07-P039 -->
“What would I tell my boy if I took your money for our disgrace?” And with that he ran off, this time without looking back. Alyosha watched him go with inexpressible sadness. Oh, he understood that until the very last instant the man himself had not known he would crumple up the banknotes and throw them away. The fleeing man never once turned, and Alyosha had known that he would not. He did not want to follow him or call him back; he knew why. When the captain had disappeared from view, Alyosha picked up both banknotes. They were badly crumpled, flattened and pressed into the sand, but perfectly whole; they even crackled like new as Alyosha unfolded and smoothed them. Having smoothed them, he folded them, put them in his pocket and went to Katerina Ivanovna to report on the success of her errand.
