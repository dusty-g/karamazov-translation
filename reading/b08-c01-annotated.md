# Book VIII: Mitya
## I. Kuzma Samsonov

<!-- B08-C01-P001 -->
Meanwhile Dmitri Fyodorovich, to whom Grushenka, as she flew off into a new life, had “ordered” her last greeting to be delivered, charging him to remember that little hour of her love forever, knew nothing of what had happened to her and was himself, at that moment, in terrible agitation and turmoil. For the last two days he had been in such an unimaginable state that he really might have developed inflammation of the brain, as he later said himself. Alyosha had been unable to find him the previous morning, and his brother Ivan had been unable to arrange a meeting with him at the tavern that same day. On his instructions, the people from whom he rented his little apartment concealed his whereabouts. For those two days he had literally been rushing in every direction, “fighting his fate and saving himself,” as he later put it; he had even dashed out of town for several hours on urgent business, though it terrified him to go away and leave Grushenka unwatched for even a minute. All this was subsequently established in the greatest detail, with documentary evidence, but for now we shall set down only the essential facts from those two dreadful days of his life that preceded the terrible catastrophe which broke so suddenly upon his fate.

<!-- B08-C01-P002 -->
It was true that Grushenka had loved him truly and sincerely for one little hour, but at the same time she sometimes tormented him with real cruelty and mercilessness. The chief difficulty was that he could make nothing at all of her intentions; nor was there any possibility of coaxing or forcing them out of her: she would never give in, but would only grow angry and turn away from him altogether. He understood that very clearly at the time. He suspected, quite correctly, that she herself was caught in some struggle, some extraordinary indecision, trying to make up her mind about something and never managing it; and so, with a sinking heart and not without reason, he supposed that at certain moments she must simply hate him and his passion. Perhaps that was so; but what exactly was troubling Grushenka he still did not understand. As far as he was concerned, the whole question tormenting him came down to just two possibilities: “Either he, Mitya, or Fyodor Pavlovich.” Here, incidentally, one definite fact must be recorded: he was entirely convinced that Fyodor Pavlovich would certainly offer Grushenka lawful marriage, if he had not offered it already, and did not believe for a moment that the old sensualist hoped to get off with a mere three thousand. Mitya had reached this conclusion from his knowledge of Grushenka and her character. That was why it could sometimes seem to him that all her anguish and indecision, too, came solely from not knowing which of them to choose and which would be more advantageous to her. Strangely enough, in those days he never even thought of the approaching return of the “officer,” the man who had played that fatal part in Grushenka’s life, whose arrival she was awaiting in such agitation and fear. It was true that in these last few days Grushenka had been very silent with him on that subject. Yet he knew perfectly well, from her own lips, about the letter she had received a month earlier from this former seducer of hers; he even knew something of its contents. In a spiteful moment she had shown him the letter, but to her surprise he had attached almost no importance to it. Why, it would be very difficult to explain: perhaps simply because, oppressed by all the ugliness and horror of his struggle with his own father over this woman, he could no longer imagine anything more terrible or dangerous for himself, at least at that time. He simply did not believe in this suitor who had suddenly popped up from somewhere after disappearing for five years, and especially did not believe that he would soon arrive. Besides, that first letter from the “officer,” the one shown to Mitenka, spoke very vaguely of this new rival’s arrival: the letter was very obscure, very high-flown, and full of nothing but sentiment. It should be noted that on that occasion Grushenka concealed the letter’s last few lines from him, which spoke somewhat more definitely of the return. Moreover, Mitenka later recalled that at the time he seemed to catch a certain involuntary, proud contempt for this message from Siberia in Grushenka’s own face. After that she told him nothing about any further communications with this new rival. And so, little by little, he actually forgot the officer altogether. He thought only that, whatever happened and however things turned out, his approaching final collision with Fyodor Pavlovich was very near and must be settled before anything else. His heart sinking, he waited every minute for Grushenka’s decision, always believing that it would come suddenly, as if by inspiration. All at once she would tell him, “Take me, I’m yours forever,” and everything would be settled: he would seize her and carry her off to the ends of the earth at once. Oh, at once, as far, as far away as possible; if not to the ends of the earth, then somewhere on the edge of Russia. There he would marry her and settle down with her incognito, so that nobody would know anything about them, here or there or anywhere. Then, oh, then an entirely new life would begin at once! He dreamed every moment, with a kind of frenzy, of this other, renewed and now “virtuous” life—“certainly, certainly virtuous.” He thirsted for that resurrection and renewal. The vile morass in which he had mired himself of his own free will weighed on him too heavily; and like so many people in such circumstances, he placed his greatest faith in a change of scene: if only these people weren’t here, if only these circumstances weren’t here, if only he could fly away from this accursed place, everything would be reborn and take a new course! That was what he believed in and longed for.

But that applied only to the first, happy resolution of the question. There was another resolution; another, dreadful outcome presented itself. What if she suddenly told him, “Go away. I’ve just settled things with Fyodor Pavlovich, and I’m going to marry him. I don’t want you”—and then… but then… Mitya, however, did not know what would happen then; he did not know until the very last hour, and on that point he must be acquitted. He had no definite intentions, had planned no crime. He merely watched, spied, and suffered, yet prepared himself only for the first, happy outcome of his fate. He even drove away every other thought. But here an entirely different torment began: an entirely new and unrelated circumstance arose, equally fatal and insoluble.

<!-- B08-C01-P004 -->
Namely, if she said to him, “I’m yours, take me away,” how was he to take her away? Where were his means, his money? Just at this point all his income from Fyodor Pavlovich’s handouts, which had continued without interruption for so many years, had run dry. Of course Grushenka had money, but on this subject Mitya suddenly proved terribly proud: he wanted to carry her off himself and begin their new life on his own means, not hers. He could not even imagine taking her money from her, and the very thought made him suffer to the point of agonized revulsion. I am not enlarging on this fact here or analyzing it, merely recording it: such was the state of his soul at that moment. All this might have arisen indirectly, and even unconsciously, from the secret torments of his conscience over Katerina Ivanovna’s money, which he had appropriated like a thief. “I’m a scoundrel toward one woman, and I’ll turn out a scoundrel toward the other straightaway,” he thought then, as he later admitted himself. “And if Grushenka finds out, she won’t want a scoundrel like that herself.” So where was he to find the means, where was he to find this fatal money? Otherwise everything would be lost and nothing would come to pass, “solely because there wasn’t enough money—oh, the disgrace!”

<!-- B08-C01-P005 -->
I am looking ahead: the point is that he perhaps did know where to get this money, perhaps knew where it lay. I shall say nothing more definite for now, since everything will be explained later; but this was his chief predicament, and I shall state it, however obscurely: to take those funds lying somewhere, to have the right to take them, he first had to return Katerina Ivanovna’s three thousand. Otherwise, “I’m a pickpocket, I’m a scoundrel, and I won’t begin my new life as a scoundrel,” Mitya decided. And so he resolved to turn the whole world upside down if necessary, but at any cost, and before anything else, to give Katerina Ivanovna those three thousand back. This resolve had taken its final form during what were, so to speak, the very last hours of his life: since his last meeting with Alyosha, two evenings earlier, on the road, after Grushenka had insulted Katerina Ivanovna and Mitya, hearing about it from Alyosha, had acknowledged that he was a scoundrel and told him to pass that on to Katerina Ivanovna, “if it can give her any relief.” That same night, after parting from his brother, in his frenzy he had felt that it would be better even “to murder and rob someone, but repay my debt to Katya.” “Better to be a murderer and a thief toward that murdered and robbed man, and in everyone’s eyes, and go to Siberia, than for Katya to have the right to say I betrayed her and stole her money too, and ran away with Grushenka on her money to start a virtuous life! I can’t bear that!” So Mitya declared through gritted teeth, and he really could imagine at times that it would end in inflammation of the brain. But for the moment he went on fighting…

A strange thing: with such a resolve, it would seem that nothing remained for him but despair; for where could he suddenly find that kind of money, a penniless man like him? Yet all that time, to the very end, he kept hoping he would get those three thousand, that somehow they would come, fly to him of their own accord, even from heaven. But that is precisely what happens to people who, like Dmitri Fyodorovich, have known all their lives only how to spend and squander inherited money for nothing, and have no idea how money is earned. The most fantastic whirlwind had risen in his head immediately after he parted from Alyosha the day before yesterday, tangling all his thoughts. The result was that he began with the wildest undertaking of all. Indeed, perhaps it is precisely in such situations, with such people, that the most impossible, fantastic undertakings first appear the most feasible. He suddenly decided to go to the merchant Samsonov, Grushenka’s protector, and offer him a “plan,” to obtain the entire sum he needed at once on the strength of this “plan.” He had no doubts at all about its commercial merits; his only doubt was how Samsonov himself would regard his move, should he choose to view it from something other than a purely commercial standpoint. Though Mitya knew the merchant by sight, he was not acquainted with him and had never even spoken to him. But for some reason, and for a long time now, he had become convinced that this old corrupter, now on his last legs, might at present have no objection to Grushenka making some honest arrangement for her life and marrying a “reliable man.” More than that: he would not merely refrain from objecting, he wanted it himself and would help bring it about if the opportunity arose. From some rumor or some words of Grushenka’s, Mitya had also concluded that the old man might prefer him to Fyodor Pavlovich as a husband for her. Perhaps many readers of our story will think it excessively crude and undiscriminating of Dmitri Fyodorovich to count on help of this sort and intend to receive his bride, so to speak, from her protector’s hands. I can only observe that to Mitya, Grushenka’s past was utterly past. He looked upon that past with boundless compassion and had decided, with all the fire of his passion, that once Grushenka told him she loved him and would marry him, an entirely new Grushenka would begin at once, and with her an entirely new Dmitri Fyodorovich, free of every vice and possessing nothing but virtues: they would forgive each other and begin their lives altogether anew. As for Kuzma Samsonov, Mitya considered him a fatal figure in that vanished former past of Grushenka’s, a man she had never loved, however, and who—this was the main thing—had himself already “passed,” was finished, so that now he no longer existed at all. Besides, Mitya could no longer even count him as a man, since everybody in town knew he was merely a sick ruin, whose relations with Grushenka had become, so to speak, purely paternal, no longer resting on their former basis; and that this had been so for a long time, almost a year. In any case there was much simpleheartedness in this on Mitya’s part, for with all his vices he was a very simplehearted man. It was owing to this simpleheartedness, among other things, that he seriously believed old Kuzma, preparing to depart for the next world, felt sincere remorse for his past with Grushenka, and that she now had no more devoted protector and friend than this harmless old man.

The very next day after his conversation with Alyosha in the fields, following which he had scarcely slept all night, he appeared at Samsonov’s house at about ten in the morning and asked to be announced. It was an old, gloomy, very large two-story house, with outbuildings and a separate wing. Downstairs lived Samsonov’s two married sons with their families, his elderly sister, and an unmarried daughter. In the wing lived two of his clerks, one of whom also had a large family. Both the children and the clerks were crowded into their quarters, while the old man occupied the upper floor alone and would not let even his daughter live there, though she nursed him and had to run upstairs from below each time, both at fixed hours and whenever he called, despite her longstanding shortness of breath. This “upper floor” consisted of many large reception rooms furnished in the old merchant style, with long, dreary rows of ungainly mahogany armchairs and chairs along the walls, crystal chandeliers draped in covers, and gloomy mirrors between the windows. All these rooms stood quite empty and uninhabited, for the sick old man huddled in just one little room, his remote little bedroom, where he was waited on by an old serving woman with her hair in a kerchief, and a “boy” who stayed on a bench in the entrance hall. His swollen legs had now made him almost entirely unable to walk; only occasionally did he rise from his leather armchair, and the old woman, supporting him under the arms, would lead him once or twice around the room. He was stern and uncommunicative even with her. When the arrival of the “captain” was announced, he immediately ordered him turned away. But Mitya insisted and asked to be announced again. Kuzma Kuzmich questioned the boy closely: what did he look like, was he drunk? Was he making a disturbance? The answer was that he was “sober, but won’t go away.” The old man again ordered him refused. Mitya, who had foreseen all this and brought paper and a pencil expressly for the purpose, then wrote one line clearly on a scrap: “On a matter of the utmost necessity, closely concerning Agrafena Alexandrovna,” and sent it in to the old man. After thinking for a little, the old man told the boy to show the visitor into the drawing room and sent the old woman downstairs with orders for his younger son to come up immediately. This younger son, a man some six feet five in height and of enormous strength, who shaved his face and dressed in the German style—Samsonov himself wore a caftan and a beard—appeared at once, without a word. They all trembled before their father. The father had summoned this strapping fellow not out of fear of the captain—he was by no means timid—but simply as a precaution, chiefly to have a witness. Accompanied by his son, who took his arm, and the boy, he at last sailed into the drawing room. Presumably he felt a rather powerful curiosity. The drawing room in which Mitya waited was an enormous, gloomy room, crushing to the soul in its dreariness, two stories high, with a gallery, walls painted “like marble,” and three enormous crystal chandeliers draped in covers. Mitya sat on a little chair by the entrance, awaiting his fate with nervous impatience. When the old man appeared in the opposite doorway, some seventy feet from Mitya’s chair, Mitya suddenly jumped up and advanced to meet him with his firm, long military strides. He was respectably dressed, in a buttoned frock coat, with a round hat in his hands and black gloves, exactly as he had been some three days earlier at the monastery, in the elder’s room, at the family meeting with Fyodor Pavlovich and his brothers. The old man stood waiting for him with stern dignity, and Mitya immediately felt that he had been inspected from head to foot on his approach. He was also struck by how terribly Kuzma Kuzmich’s face had swollen of late: his lower lip, thick enough already, now looked like a drooping pancake. With silent dignity the old man bowed to his guest, pointed to an armchair beside the sofa, and slowly began to lower himself onto the sofa opposite Mitya, leaning on his son’s arm and grunting with pain. Seeing these painful efforts, Mitya immediately felt remorse in his heart, and a sensitive shame at his own present insignificance before so important a person whom he had disturbed.

“What is it you wish of me, sir?” the old man said when he had finally seated himself, slowly, distinctly, sternly, but politely.

<!-- B08-C01-P009 -->
Mitya started and made to jump up, but sat down again. Then he immediately began speaking loudly, quickly, nervously, with gestures and in a state of outright frenzy. Clearly this man had reached the limit, was ruined, was seeking his last way out; if it failed, he might as well throw himself into the water that very minute. Old Samsonov probably understood all this at a glance, though his face remained as unchanging and cold as an idol’s.

<!-- B08-C01-P010 -->
“The most honorable Kuzma Kuzmich has probably heard more than once about my differences with my father, Fyodor Pavlovich Karamazov, who robbed me of my own mother’s inheritance… since the whole town is chattering about it already… because here everyone chatters about what they shouldn’t… And besides, you may have heard something from Grushenka… I beg your pardon: from Agrafena Alexandrovna… from Agrafena Alexandrovna, whom I respect and esteem so highly…” Thus Mitya began, breaking down almost at his first words. But we shall not reproduce his entire speech word for word, merely give its substance. The matter was this: three months earlier he, Mitya, had consulted a lawyer in the provincial capital “expressly”—he actually said “expressly,” not “on purpose”—“the famous lawyer, Kuzma Kuzmich, Pavel Pavlovich Korneplodov, whom you’ve probably heard of? An ample forehead, almost a statesman’s mind… knows you too… spoke in the highest terms…” Mitya broke down a second time. But these breakdowns did not stop him: he immediately leaped over them and plunged further and further on. This same Korneplodov, after questioning him closely and examining the documents Mitya had been able to produce—Mitya spoke vaguely about the documents and hurried particularly at this point—had said that a lawsuit could indeed be brought over the village of Chermashnya, which ought to belong to him, Mitya, through his mother, and so give that disgraceful old man a nasty surprise… “because not every door is shut, and justice knows where to squeeze through.” In short, there were grounds to hope for even another six thousand from Fyodor Pavlovich, or as much as seven, since Chermashnya was worth at least twenty-five thousand—indeed, a good twenty-eight—“thirty, thirty, Kuzma Kuzmich, and imagine, I haven’t even got seventeen out of that cruel man!..” So he, Mitya, had dropped the matter at the time, since he didn’t know how to handle justice, and on arriving here had been stunned by a counterclaim—here Mitya again got tangled and again made an abrupt leap. So would the most honorable Kuzma Kuzmich care to take over all his claims against this monster, and in return give him only three thousand?.. There was no possibility of losing, he swore on his honor, his honor; on the contrary, instead of three thousand, Kuzma Kuzmich might make six or seven… And the main thing was to settle it “this very day.” “I’ll go before a notary for you, or whatever it is… In short, I’m ready for anything, I’ll give you any documents you require, sign everything… and we could draw up the paper straightaway, and if it were possible, if only it were possible, this very morning… You would give me those three thousand… for who can compare with you as a man of capital in this little town… and so save me from… in short, save my poor head for a most honorable purpose, for a most exalted purpose, one might say… because I cherish the most honorable feelings toward a certain person whom you know only too well and care for like a father. Otherwise I wouldn’t have come, if it weren’t like a father. And if you like, three of us have knocked our heads together here, because fate is a monster, Kuzma Kuzmich! Realism, Kuzma Kuzmich, realism! But since you ought to have been left out of the reckoning long ago, that leaves two heads, as I put it, perhaps clumsily, but I’m no man of letters. That is, one head is mine and the other is that monster’s. So choose: me or the monster? Everything’s in your hands now—three destinies and two lots… Forgive me, I’ve lost my thread, but you understand… I can see by your venerable eyes that you’ve understood… And if you haven’t, then into the water today, there!”

Mitya cut short his absurd speech with that “there,” jumped up, and waited for an answer to his foolish proposal. With his last phrase he suddenly felt, hopelessly, that everything had collapsed, and above all that he had piled up a terrible load of nonsense. “Strange: on the way here it all seemed so good, and now it’s nonsense!” suddenly flashed through his despairing head. Throughout his speech the old man had sat motionless, watching him with an icy look. After keeping him waiting for about a minute, however, Kuzma Kuzmich finally pronounced in a most decisive and disheartening tone:

“Beg pardon, sir, we don’t deal in that sort of business.”

“What am I to do now, Kuzma Kuzmich?” he muttered with a pale smile. “I’m ruined now, aren’t I? What do you think?”

“Beg pardon, sir…”

Mitya remained standing, staring fixedly at him, and suddenly noticed something move in the old man’s face. He started.

“You see, sir, that sort of business isn’t in our line,” the old man said slowly. “Lawsuits, lawyers—it’s nothing but trouble! But if you like, there’s a man here you could turn to…”

“My God, who is he!.. You’re bringing me back to life, Kuzma Kuzmich,” Mitya suddenly babbled.

“He’s not from here, this man, and he isn’t here just now either. A peasant by station, deals in timber, goes by the name of Pointer. For a year now he’s been bargaining with Fyodor Pavlovich for the wood at that Chermashnya of yours, but they can’t agree on the price. Perhaps you’ve heard. He’s just come back now and is staying with the priest at Ilyinskoye, twelve versts or so from Volovya station, in the village of Ilyinskoye. He wrote to me here too about this very matter, the wood, that is, asking my advice. Fyodor Pavlovich means to go and see him himself. So if you got ahead of Fyodor Pavlovich and offered Pointer the same thing you’ve told me about, then perhaps he…”

“A stroke of genius!” Mitya interrupted ecstatically. “Exactly the man, it’s made for him! He’s bargaining, Fyodor Pavlovich is asking too much, and here he gets a document giving him ownership itself, ha ha ha!” And Mitya suddenly burst into his short, wooden laugh, so unexpectedly that even Samsonov’s head twitched.

“How can I thank you, Kuzma Kuzmich?” Mitya gushed.

Mitya made to seize the old man’s hand and shake it, but something malicious flashed in the old man’s eyes. Mitya withdrew his hand, then immediately reproached himself for imagining things. “He’s tired…” flashed through his mind.

“For her! For her, Kuzma Kuzmich! You understand, it’s for her!” he suddenly roared across the drawing room. He bowed, turned sharply, and hurried toward the exit without looking back, with those same quick, long strides. He was trembling with delight. “Everything was going to ruin, and then a guardian angel saved me,” rushed through his mind. “And if a businessman like that old man—a most honorable old man, and what bearing!—has shown me this road, then… then of course it’s the winning road. I’ll fly there now. I’ll be back before night, I’ll be back in the night, but the battle’s won. Surely the old man couldn’t have been making a fool of me?” So Mitya exclaimed as he strode toward his apartment, and of course his mind could see it in no other way: either it was sound advice—from such a businessman!—given with a knowledge of the business, a knowledge of this Pointer—what a strange name!—or, or the old man had made a fool of him! Alas, that last thought was the only correct one. Later, long afterward, when the whole catastrophe had already occurred, old Samsonov himself admitted with a laugh that he had made a fool of the “captain.” He was a malicious, cold, mocking man, with morbid antipathies into the bargain. Whether it was the captain’s ecstatic air, the foolish conviction of this “squanderer and spendthrift” that he, Samsonov, could be taken in by such nonsense as his “plan,” or a feeling of jealousy over Grushenka, in whose name “that scapegrace” had come to him with some wild scheme to ask for money—I do not know exactly what prompted the old man then. But at the moment when Mitya stood before him, feeling his legs grow weak and senselessly exclaiming that he was ruined, the old man looked at him with boundless malice and conceived the idea of making a fool of him. When Mitya had gone, Kuzma Kuzmich, pale with spite, turned to his son and ordered him to see that henceforth there was not a trace of that ragamuffin about the place: he was not even to be let into the yard, or else…

He did not finish saying what he threatened, but even his son, who had often seen him angry, shuddered with fear. A whole hour later the old man was still trembling all over with spite, and toward evening he fell ill and sent for “the doctor.”
