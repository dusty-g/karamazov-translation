# Book XI — Brother Ivan Fyodorovich
## X. “It Was He Who Said It!”

<!-- B11-C10-P001 -->
On entering, Alyosha told Ivan Fyodorovich that a little over an hour before Marya Kondratyevna had come running to his lodgings and announced that Smerdyakov had taken his own life. “I go in to clear away the samovar, and there he is hanging from a nail by the wall.” When Alyosha asked whether she had reported it to the proper people, she answered that she had told no one, but “rushed straight to you first and ran all the way.” She was like a madwoman, Alyosha said, trembling all over like a leaf. When Alyosha hurried back to their house with her, he found Smerdyakov still hanging. A note lay on the table: “I destroy my life of my own will and desire, so that no one is to be blamed.” Alyosha left the note on the table as it was and went straight to the district police chief, where he reported everything, “and from there straight to you,” he concluded, looking intently into Ivan's face. Throughout his account he had not taken his eyes off him, as though greatly struck by something in his expression.

<!-- B11-C10-P002 -->
“Brother,” he suddenly cried, “you must be terribly ill! You're looking at me as though you don't understand what I'm saying.”

<!-- B11-C10-P003 -->
“It's good you've come,” Ivan said, apparently absorbed in thought and as though he had not heard Alyosha's exclamation at all. “But I knew he'd hanged himself.”

<!-- B11-C10-P004 -->
“Who told you?”

<!-- B11-C10-P005 -->
“I don't know who. But I knew. Did I know? Yes, he told me. He was just telling me...”

<!-- B11-C10-P006 -->
Ivan stood in the middle of the room, speaking in the same thoughtful way and looking at the floor.

<!-- B11-C10-P007 -->
“Who is ‘he’?” Alyosha asked, involuntarily looking around.

<!-- B11-C10-P008 -->
“He slipped away.”

<!-- B11-C10-P009 -->
Ivan raised his head and smiled quietly.

<!-- B11-C10-P010 -->
“He was afraid of you, of you, my dove. You're a ‘pure cherub.’ Dmitri calls you a cherub. A cherub... The thunderous cry of delight from the seraphim! What is a seraph? Perhaps a whole constellation. Or perhaps the whole constellation is only some chemical molecule... Is there a constellation of the Lion and Sun, do you know?”

<!-- B11-C10-P011 -->
“Brother, sit down!” Alyosha said in alarm. “For God's sake, sit on the sofa. You're delirious; lie back on the pillow, like that. Would you like a wet towel on your head? Perhaps it would help?”

<!-- B11-C10-P012 -->
“Give me the towel, there on the chair. I threw it there earlier.”

<!-- B11-C10-P013 -->
“It isn't here. Don't worry, I know where it is. Here it is,” Alyosha said, finding a clean, still folded and unused towel in the other corner of the room by Ivan's dressing table. Ivan looked strangely at the towel; his memory seemed to return in a flash.

<!-- B11-C10-P014 -->
“Wait,” he half rose from the sofa. “Earlier, an hour ago, I took that very towel from that very place and wet it with water. I put it on my head and threw it here... How can it be dry? There wasn't another one.”

<!-- B11-C10-P015 -->
“You put this towel on your head?” Alyosha asked.

<!-- B11-C10-P016 -->
“Yes, and walked about the room, an hour ago... Why have the candles burned down so far? What time is it?”

<!-- B11-C10-P017 -->
“Nearly twelve.”

<!-- B11-C10-P018 -->
“No, no, no!” Ivan suddenly cried. “It wasn't a dream! He was here, he sat here, on that sofa over there. When you knocked at the window, I threw a glass at him... that one... Wait, I was asleep before too, but this dream isn't a dream. It happened before as well. I have dreams now, Alyosha... but they aren't dreams, they're while I'm awake: I walk, speak, and see... and I'm asleep. But he sat here, he was here, on this very sofa... He's terribly stupid, Alyosha, terribly stupid,” Ivan suddenly laughed, and began pacing the room.

<!-- B11-C10-P019 -->
“Who's stupid? Who are you talking about, brother?” Alyosha asked again in anguish.

<!-- B11-C10-P020 -->
“The devil! He's taken to visiting me. Twice now, almost three times, even. He teased me, saying I was angry because he was just a devil, not Satan with scorched wings, amid thunder and radiance. But he isn't Satan; he's lying about that. He's an impostor. He's just a devil, a rotten little devil. He goes to the bathhouse. Strip him and you're sure to find a tail, long and smooth like a Great Dane's, an arshin long, brown... Alyosha, you're chilled, you've been in the snow. Would you like tea? What? It's cold? Shall I have some made? C'est à ne pas mettre un chien dehors—you wouldn't send a dog out in weather like this.”

<!-- B11-C10-P021 -->
Alyosha hurried to the washstand, wet the towel, persuaded Ivan to sit down again, and wrapped his head in it. He sat beside him.

<!-- B11-C10-P022 -->
“What were you telling me earlier about Lise?” Ivan began again. He was becoming very talkative. “I like Lise. I said something nasty about her to you. I lied; I like her... I'm afraid for Katya tomorrow, afraid most of all. For the future. Tomorrow she'll abandon me and trample me underfoot. She thinks I'm ruining Mitya out of jealousy over her! Yes, she thinks that! Well, I'm not! Tomorrow the cross, but not the gallows. No, I won't hang myself. Do you know I could never take my own life, Alyosha! Is it from baseness? I'm no coward. From thirst for life! How did I know Smerdyakov had hanged himself? Yes, he told me...”

<!-- B11-C10-P023 -->
“And you're firmly convinced someone was sitting here?” Alyosha asked.

<!-- B11-C10-P024 -->
“On that sofa, in the corner. You'd have driven him away. But you did drive him away: he disappeared when you appeared. I love your face, Alyosha. Did you know I love your face? And he—he's me, Alyosha, my own self. Everything low in me, everything base and contemptible! Yes, I'm a ‘romantic’; he noticed that... though it's slander too. He's terribly stupid, but that's how he gets at me. He's cunning, cunning like an animal; he knew how to drive me mad. He kept teasing me that I believed in him, and made me listen that way. He took me in like a schoolboy. Still, he told me a great deal of truth about myself. I'd never have said it to myself. You know, Alyosha, you know,” Ivan added very seriously and as though in confidence, “I'd very much like him really to be himself, not me!”

<!-- B11-C10-P025 -->
“He's tormented you,” Alyosha said, looking compassionately at his brother.

<!-- B11-C10-P026 -->
“He teased me! And you know, cleverly, cleverly: ‘Conscience! What is conscience? I make it myself. Why do I suffer, then? From habit. From the universal human habit of seven thousand years. So let's break the habit and become gods.’ It was he who said it, he who said it!”

<!-- B11-C10-P027 -->
“And not you, not you?” Alyosha cried irrepressibly, looking clearly at his brother. “Then let him! Leave him alone and forget him! Let him carry away everything you're cursing now, and never come back!”

<!-- B11-C10-P028 -->
“Yes, but he's malicious. He laughed at me. He was insolent, Alyosha,” Ivan said, shuddering with wounded feeling. “But he slandered me, slandered me in many things. Lied about me to my very face. ‘Oh, you're going to perform a feat of virtue, announce that you killed your father, that the lackey killed your father at your instigation...’”

<!-- B11-C10-P029 -->
“Brother,” Alyosha interrupted, “hold on: you didn't kill him. That's not true!”

<!-- B11-C10-P030 -->
“It's he who says it, he, and he knows: ‘You're going to perform a feat of virtue, yet you don't believe in virtue—that's what angers and torments you, that's why you're so vindictive.’ That's what he told me about myself, and he knows what he's saying...”

<!-- B11-C10-P031 -->
“It's you saying it, not him!” Alyosha exclaimed sorrowfully. “You're saying it in illness, in delirium, tormenting yourself!”

<!-- B11-C10-P032 -->
“No, he knows what he's saying. You're going from pride, he says. You'll stand up and say: ‘I killed him, and why are you writhing in horror? You're lying! I despise your opinion, I despise your horror.’ That's what he says about me, and suddenly he says: ‘But you know, you want them to praise you: a criminal, a murderer, they'll say, but what generous feelings he has—he wanted to save his brother and confessed!’ Now that's a lie, Alyosha!” Ivan suddenly cried, his eyes flashing. “I don't want the rabble's praise! He lied, Alyosha, lied, I swear to you! That's why I threw the glass at him, and it smashed against his face.”

<!-- B11-C10-P033 -->
“Brother, calm down, stop!” Alyosha pleaded.

<!-- B11-C10-P034 -->
“No, he knows how to torment; he's cruel,” Ivan continued without listening. “I always sensed why he came. ‘Granted you were going from pride,’ he says, ‘but you also hoped they'd convict Smerdyakov and send him to penal servitude, that Mitya would be acquitted, and you condemned only morally’—do you hear, he laughed there!—‘while others would even praise you. But now Smerdyakov's dead, he's hanged himself—so who in court will believe you alone now? Yet you're going, you're going, you'll go all the same, you've decided to go. Why are you going, after that?’ It's terrible, Alyosha. I can't bear such questions. Who dares ask me such questions!”

<!-- B11-C10-P035 -->
“Brother,” Alyosha interrupted, faint with fear but still apparently hoping to bring Ivan to his senses, “how could he have told you about Smerdyakov's death before I arrived, when no one knew about it yet, and there hadn't been time for anyone to find out?”

<!-- B11-C10-P036 -->
“He told me,” Ivan said firmly, admitting no doubt. “If you like, that was all he talked about. ‘And if only you believed in virtue,’ he said: ‘let them not believe me, I'm going for the principle. But you're a little pig like Fyodor Pavlovich, and what's virtue to you? Why drag yourself there if your sacrifice serves no purpose? Because you don't know why you're going yourself! Oh, you'd give a great deal to know why you're going! And do you think you've decided? You haven't decided yet. You'll sit all night deciding: to go or not? But you'll go all the same, and you know you'll go; you know yourself that whatever you decide, the decision no longer depends on you. You'll go because you don't dare not go. Why you don't dare—work that out for yourself; there's your riddle!’ He got up and left. You came, and he left. He called me a coward, Alyosha! Le mot de l'énigme—the answer to the riddle—is that I'm a coward! ‘Eagles like that aren't meant to soar above the earth!’ That's what he added, what he added! And Smerdyakov said the same. He must be killed! Katya despises me; I've seen it for a month now, and Lise will begin despising me too! ‘You're going so they'll praise you’—that's a brutal lie! And you despise me too, Alyosha. Now I'll hate you again. And I hate the monster, hate the monster! I don't want to save the monster; let him rot in penal servitude! He's started singing a hymn! Oh, tomorrow I'll go, stand in front of them, and spit in all their faces!”

<!-- B11-C10-P037 -->
He leaped up in a frenzy, flung off the towel, and began pacing the room again. Alyosha remembered his earlier words: “As though I'm asleep while awake... I walk, speak, and see, and I'm asleep.” It seemed to be precisely what was happening now. Alyosha stayed beside him. The thought flashed through his mind of running for the doctor and bringing him back, but he was afraid to leave his brother alone: there was absolutely no one to entrust him to. At last Ivan’s memory began to fail him completely. He kept talking, talking without stopping, but now quite incoherently. He could hardly pronounce the words, and suddenly swayed heavily where he stood. But Alyosha caught him in time. Ivan let himself be led to bed; Alyosha somehow undressed him and laid him down. He himself sat over him for another two hours. The sick man slept soundly, without moving, breathing quietly and evenly. Alyosha took a pillow and lay on the sofa without undressing. As he fell asleep, he prayed for Mitya and Ivan. Ivan's illness was becoming clear to him: “The torments of a proud decision, a deep conscience!” God, in whom he did not believe, and His truth were overcoming a heart that still refused to submit. “Yes,” passed through Alyosha's mind, his head already on the pillow, “yes, if Smerdyakov is dead, no one will believe Ivan's evidence now; but he'll go and testify!” Alyosha smiled quietly. “God will prevail!” he thought. “Either he'll rise in the light of truth, or... perish in hatred, taking revenge on himself and everyone for having served what he doesn't believe in,” Alyosha added bitterly, and prayed for Ivan again.
