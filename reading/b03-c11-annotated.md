# Book III: The Lustful
## XI. Another Ruined Reputation

<!-- B03-C11-P001 -->
The monastery was only a little over a verst from town. Alyosha hurried along the road, deserted at this hour. It was almost night; at thirty paces it was already hard to make things out. Halfway there was a crossroads. Beneath a solitary willow at the crossroads, a figure could be seen. The moment Alyosha reached the crossing, the figure sprang from its place, rushed at him, and shouted in a savage voice:

<!-- B03-C11-P002 -->
“Your money or your life!”

<!-- B03-C11-P003 -->
“So it's you, Mitya!” Alyosha exclaimed in surprise, though he had given a violent start.

<!-- B03-C11-P004 -->
“Ha, ha, ha! You weren't expecting that? I thought: where should I wait for you? Outside her house? Three roads lead away from there, and I might miss you. At last I decided to wait here, because he's bound to pass this way, there's no other road to the monastery. Well, tell me the truth, crush me like a cockroach . . . But what's the matter with you?”

<!-- B03-C11-P005 -->
“Nothing, brother . . . it's just the fright. Oh, Dmitri! Father's blood earlier . . .” Alyosha began to cry. He had wanted to cry for a long time, and now something seemed suddenly to give way inside him. “You almost killed him . . . you cursed him . . . and now . . . now . . . you're making jokes . . . ‘Your money or your life!’”

<!-- B03-C11-P006 -->
“Oh, well, what of it? Is it improper? Doesn't suit the occasion?”

<!-- B03-C11-P007 -->
“No, no . . . I just . . .”

<!-- B03-C11-P008 -->
“Wait. Look at the night: see how dark it is, those clouds, what a wind has risen! I hid here under the willow, waiting for you, and suddenly I thought—God's my witness!—why go on tormenting myself, what am I waiting for? Here's a willow, I've got a handkerchief, I've got a shirt, I can twist a rope in no time, with my suspenders thrown in, and stop burdening the earth, stop disgracing it with my vile presence! Then I heard you coming—Lord, it was as though something suddenly descended on me: so there is someone I love, after all, here he is, here's the one, my dear little brother, the person I love more than anyone in the world, the only person I love! And I loved you so much, I felt such love that instant, I thought: I'll throw my arms around his neck! Then a stupid idea came to me: ‘I'll amuse him, give him a fright.’ And like a fool I yelled, ‘Your money!’ Forgive my foolishness—it's just nonsense, and what's in my soul . . . that's quite proper too . . . Well, damn it, tell me, what happened there? What did she say? Crush me, strike me down, don't spare me! Did she fly into a frenzy?”

<!-- B03-C11-P009 -->
“No, it wasn't that . . . It was quite different, Mitya. There. I just found both of them there.”

<!-- B03-C11-P010 -->
“Both of whom?”

<!-- B03-C11-P011 -->
“Grushenka at Katerina Ivanovna's.” Dmitri Fyodorovich stood dumbfounded.

<!-- B03-C11-P012 -->
“Impossible!” he cried. “You're delirious! Grushenka at her house?”

<!-- B03-C11-P013 -->
Alyosha told him everything that had happened from the moment he entered Katerina Ivanovna's. He spoke for about ten minutes, not exactly fluently or coherently, but he seems to have conveyed it clearly, catching the most important words and gestures and vividly expressing his own feelings, often with a single touch. His brother Dmitri listened in silence, staring straight at him with a terrible fixity, but Alyosha could see that he had already understood everything, grasped the whole event. Yet as the story went on, his face became not so much gloomy as menacing. He knitted his brows and clenched his teeth; his fixed gaze seemed to grow still more fixed, more insistent, more terrible . . . All the more unexpected, then, was the inexplicably swift transformation that suddenly came over his whole face, angry and ferocious until that moment: his tight lips parted, and Dmitri Fyodorovich burst into the most unrestrained, the most genuine laughter. He was literally overcome with laughter; for a long time he could not even speak for laughing.

<!-- B03-C11-P014 -->
“So she never kissed her hand! She never kissed it, she just ran off!” he cried in a kind of feverish delight—one might have called it brazen delight too, had it not been so artless. “So the other one shouted that she was a tigress! A tigress is just what she is! So she ought to go to the scaffold? Yes, yes, she ought to, she ought to, I think so myself, long overdue! You see, brother, let it be the scaffold, but first one has to get well. I understand that queen of insolence, she's all there, she showed her whole self over that little hand, the she-devil! She's the queen of all the she-devils you could ever imagine in this world! A delight in her own way! So she ran home? I'll go at once . . . ah . . . I'll run to her! Alyoshka, don't blame me, I agree that strangling her wouldn't be enough . . .”

<!-- B03-C11-P015 -->
“And Katerina Ivanovna!” Alyosha exclaimed sadly.

<!-- B03-C11-P016 -->
“I see her too, I see right through her too, as I never have before! It's like discovering all four corners of the earth—five, I mean! Such a step! That's the very same Katenka, the boarding-school girl who wasn't afraid to run to a ridiculous, boorish officer with her noble idea of saving her father, risking a dreadful insult! But our pride, our need to take a risk, our challenge to fate, a challenge without limits! You say that aunt of hers tried to stop her? That aunt, you know, is a tyrant herself. She's the sister of that general's wife in Moscow, and used to hold her nose even higher than the other one; but her husband was caught embezzling state funds and lost everything, his estate and all, and his proud wife suddenly lowered her tone and never raised it again. So she tried to hold Katya back, and Katya wouldn't listen. ‘I can conquer everything,’ she thought, ‘everything is in my power; if I choose, I'll bewitch Grushenka too.’ And she believed it herself, she was showing off to herself—who's to blame? Do you think she kissed Grushenka's hand first on purpose, as a cunning calculation? No, it was real, she really fell in love with Grushenka—or rather, not with Grushenka but with her own dream, her own delirium, precisely because it was her dream, her delirium! Dear Alyosha, how did you escape from them, women like that? Did you hitch up your undercassock and run away? Ha, ha, ha!”

<!-- B03-C11-P017 -->
“Brother, you don't seem even to have noticed how you hurt Katerina Ivanovna by telling Grushenka about that day. She just threw it in her face that she herself ‘used to slip off to gentlemen to sell her beauty!’ Brother, what could be more insulting?” What tormented Alyosha most was the thought that his brother seemed actually glad of Katerina Ivanovna's humiliation, although of course that could not be so.

<!-- B03-C11-P018 -->
“Ah!” Dmitri Fyodorovich suddenly frowned terribly and struck his forehead with his palm. Only now had he taken it in, although Alyosha had told him everything together earlier, both the insult and Katerina Ivanovna's cry, “Your brother is a scoundrel!” “Yes, indeed, perhaps I did tell Grushenka about that ‘fateful day,’ as Katya calls it. Yes, that's right, I told her, I remember! It was that time in Mokroye, I was drunk, the Gypsy women were singing . . . But I was sobbing myself, sobbing then, I was on my knees, I was worshipping Katya's image, and Grushenka understood that. She understood everything then, I remember, she cried herself . . . Ah, damn it! Could it have been any different now? Then she cried, and now . . . Now it's ‘a dagger to the heart’! That's women for you.” He lowered his eyes and fell into thought.

<!-- B03-C11-P019 -->
“Yes, I'm a scoundrel! A scoundrel beyond any doubt,” he suddenly said in a dark voice. “It makes no difference whether I cried or not, I'm a scoundrel all the same! Tell her I accept the name, if that's any comfort. And that's enough, goodbye, why go on talking? There's nothing cheerful in it. You go your way and I'll go mine. And I don't want to see you again until some final moment. Goodbye, Alexei!” He gripped Alyosha's hand hard and, still looking down, without raising his head, abruptly broke away and strode off toward town. Alyosha watched him go, unable to believe that he had really left so suddenly.

<!-- B03-C11-P020 -->
“Wait, Alexei, one more confession, to you alone!” Dmitri Fyodorovich suddenly turned back. “Look at me, look closely: you see, right here, right here, a terrible dishonor is taking shape.” As he said “right here,” Dmitri Fyodorovich struck his chest with his fist, with such a strange expression that it was as though the dishonor lay stored there, somewhere on his chest, perhaps in a pocket, or hung sewn up around his neck. “You know me already: a scoundrel, a self-confessed scoundrel! But know this: whatever I've done before, or do now or in the future, nothing, nothing can compare in vileness with the dishonor I'm carrying here on my chest now, at this very moment, right here, here, a dishonor already at work, already being committed, which I have complete power to stop. I can stop it or carry it through—remember that! Well, then, know that I'll carry it through, I won't stop it. I told you everything earlier, but I didn't tell you this, because even I couldn't find the brass for it! I can still stop. If I stop, tomorrow I can regain a whole half of my lost honor, but I won't stop, I'll carry out my vile plan, and you must bear witness from now on that I say this beforehand, knowing what I say! Ruin and darkness! There's nothing to explain, you'll find out in time. A stinking alley and a she-devil! Goodbye. Don't pray for me, I'm not worth it, and there's no need at all, no need at all . . . I don't need it one bit! Away!”

<!-- B03-C11-P021 -->
And he suddenly went off, this time for good. Alyosha walked toward the monastery. “How can it be, how can I never see him again, what is he saying?” The thought ran wildly through his mind. “I'll certainly see him tomorrow, I'll find him, I'll go out of my way to find him. What is he saying!”

<!-- B03-C11-P022 -->
He went around the monastery and through the pine grove straight to the hermitage. They opened the door for him, although at this hour no one was admitted anymore. His heart trembled as he entered the elder's cell. Why, why had he gone out, why had the elder sent him “into the world”? Here there was peace, here there was holiness, while out there was turmoil, out there was darkness in which one would immediately lose oneself and go astray . . .

<!-- B03-C11-P023 -->
In the cell were the novice Porfiry and the hieromonk Father Paisius, who had been coming every hour throughout the day to ask after Father Zosima's health. Alyosha learned with fear that he was growing worse and worse. Even the usual evening talk with the brethren could not take place this time. Ordinarily, every evening after the service, before retiring for the night, the monastery's brethren would gather in the elder's cell, and each would confess aloud his sins of that day, his sinful imaginings, thoughts, and temptations, even quarrels among themselves, if any had occurred. Some confessed on their knees. The elder absolved them, reconciled them, counseled them, imposed penances, blessed them, and sent them away. It was these “confessions” of the brethren that the opponents of eldership protested against, calling them a profanation of confession as a sacrament, almost blasphemy, though this was something quite different. They even represented to the diocesan authorities that such confessions not only failed to serve a good purpose but actually and deliberately led men into sin and temptation. Many of the brethren, they said, found it burdensome to visit the elder and went against their will, because everyone else went, lest they be thought proud and rebellious in their minds. It was said that some of the brethren, on their way to evening confession, would agree beforehand: “I'll say I was angry with you this morning, and you back me up”—just to have something to say and get it over with. Alyosha knew that this really did sometimes happen. He also knew that some of the brethren deeply resented the custom by which even letters from relatives received by the hermitage's monks were first brought to the elder, for him to open before their recipients did. All this was, of course, supposed to be done freely and sincerely, with all one's heart, in the name of voluntary humility and instruction for the soul's salvation; in practice, it turned out, it was sometimes done most insincerely, in a spirit quite the reverse: contrived and false. But the older and more experienced brethren stood their ground, arguing that “for those who sincerely entered these walls to seek salvation, all these obediences and spiritual labors will undoubtedly lead to salvation and do them great good. Those, on the contrary, who find them burdensome and grumble are as good as no monks at all, and came to the monastery for nothing: their place is in the world. And since there is no escaping sin and the devil even in church, let alone in the world, there is no reason to indulge sin.”

<!-- B03-C11-P024 -->
“He's grown weak, and drowsiness has come over him,” Father Paisius told Alyosha in a whisper after blessing him. “It's hard even to wake him. But there's no need to wake him. He woke for about five minutes and asked that his blessing be taken to the brethren, and that they pray for him tonight. He intends to take communion once more tomorrow. He remembered you, Alexei, and asked whether you'd gone out; we told him you were in town. ‘That is what I gave him my blessing for; his place is there, and for now not here.’ Those were his words about you. He spoke of you with love and concern. Do you understand what an honor you've been given? Only, why has he appointed you a time in the world for now? It means he foresees something in your fate! Understand, Alexei, that even if you return to the world, it will be to fulfill an obedience laid upon you by your elder, not for vain frivolity or worldly pleasures . . .”

<!-- B03-C11-P025 -->
Father Paisius left. To Alyosha there was no doubt that the elder was dying, although he might live another day or two. Alyosha resolved firmly and fervently that, despite his promise to see his father, the Khokhlakovs, his brother, and Katerina Ivanovna, he would not leave the monastery at all tomorrow, but remain with his elder until his death. His heart burned with love, and he bitterly reproached himself for having been able, even for a moment out there in town, to forget the man he had left on his deathbed at the monastery, whom he revered above everyone in the world. He went into the elder's little bedroom, knelt, and bowed to the ground before the sleeping man. The elder slept quietly, motionless, his faint breathing steady and almost imperceptible. His face was peaceful.

<!-- B03-C11-P026 -->
Returning to the other room, the one where the elder had received his visitors that morning, Alyosha lay down almost fully dressed, having taken off only his boots, on the hard, narrow leather sofa. He had long slept there every night, bringing only a pillow. He had long since given up laying out the mattress his father had shouted about earlier. He would simply take off his undercassock and use it as a blanket. Before sleeping, however, he fell to his knees and prayed for a long time. In his fervent prayer he did not ask God to make sense of his confusion; he longed only for joyful tenderness, the old tenderness that always visited his soul after the praise and glorification of God that ordinarily made up his whole prayer before sleep. This joy brought light, peaceful sleep in its wake. As he prayed now, his hand suddenly chanced upon the little pink envelope in his pocket, the one given to him by Katerina Ivanovna's maid when she caught up with him on the road. He grew embarrassed, but finished his prayer. Then, after some hesitation, he opened the envelope. Inside was a little letter addressed to him, signed Lise—the very young daughter of Madame Khokhlakov who had laughed at him so much that morning in the elder's presence.

<!-- B03-C11-P027 -->
“Alexei Fyodorovich,” she wrote, “I'm writing to you in secret from everyone, even Mama, and I know how wrong it is. But I can't go on living unless I tell you what has been born in my heart, and no one but the two of us must know about it for now. But how can I tell you what I want so much to say? They say paper doesn't blush; I assure you that isn't true, and it's blushing just as much as I am now, all over. Dear Alyosha, I love you, I've loved you since childhood, since Moscow, when you were quite different from what you are now, and I love you for the rest of my life. My heart has chosen you, to join my life with yours and finish our lives together in old age. On condition, of course, that you leave the monastery. As for our ages, we'll wait as long as the law requires. By then I'll certainly be well, I'll walk and dance. There can be no question about that.

<!-- B03-C11-P028 -->
“You see how I've thought everything out. There's just one thing I can't work out: what will you think of me when you read this? I'm always laughing and being naughty, and I made you angry earlier, but I assure you that just now, before picking up my pen, I prayed before the icon of the Mother of God, and I'm praying even now, and almost crying.

<!-- B03-C11-P029 -->
“My secret is in your hands. When you come tomorrow, I don't know how I'll even look at you. Oh, Alexei Fyodorovich, what if I can't help myself again, like a fool, and start laughing when I look at you, the way I did earlier? You'll take me for a horrid little mocker and won't believe my letter. And so I beg you, my dear, if you have any compassion for me, when you come in tomorrow, don't look me too straight in the eye, because if my eyes meet yours I may certainly burst out laughing, and besides, you'll be wearing that long dress . . . Even now I go cold all over when I think about it, so when you come in don't look at me at all for a little while, look at Mama or out the window . . .

<!-- B03-C11-P030 -->
“There, I've written you a love letter. My God, what have I done! Alyosha, don't despise me, and if I've done something very wrong and upset you, forgive me. The secret of my reputation, perhaps ruined forever, is now in your hands.

<!-- B03-C11-P031 -->
“I'm sure to cry today. Goodbye, until our dreadful meeting. Lise.

<!-- B03-C11-P032 -->
“P.S. Alyosha, only you must, must, must come! Lise.”

<!-- B03-C11-P033 -->
Alyosha read it in astonishment, read it twice, thought for a moment, and suddenly laughed softly and sweetly. He started: that laugh seemed sinful to him. But a moment later he laughed again, just as softly and just as happily. Slowly he put the letter back in its little envelope, crossed himself, and lay down. The turmoil in his soul suddenly passed. “Lord, have mercy on them all, all those from today; protect them, unhappy and turbulent as they are, and guide them. You have your ways: by the ways you know, save them. You are love, you will send joy to them all too!” Alyosha murmured, crossing himself as he drifted into peaceful sleep.
