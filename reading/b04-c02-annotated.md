# Book IV: Strains
## II. At His Father's

<!-- B04-C02-P001 -->
Alyosha went to his father first. As he approached, he remembered how strongly his father had insisted the day before that he somehow slip in without his brother Ivan knowing. “But why?” Alyosha suddenly wondered. “If Father wants to tell me something privately, in secret, why should I have to come in secretly? He must have meant to say something else yesterday, but in his excitement never got it out,” he decided. Even so, he was very glad when Marfa Ignatyevna, who opened the gate for him, answered his question by saying that Ivan Fyodorovich had gone out two hours earlier. Grigory, it turned out, was ill and in bed in the servants' wing.

<!-- B04-C02-P002 -->
“And Father?”

<!-- B04-C02-P003 -->
“He's up, having his coffee,” Marfa Ignatyevna answered rather dryly.

<!-- B04-C02-P004 -->
Alyosha went in. The old man was sitting alone at the table, in slippers and an old coat, looking over some accounts to amuse himself, though without much attention. He was entirely alone in the house: Smerdyakov too had gone out, to buy provisions for dinner. But the accounts were not what occupied his mind. Although he had risen early and was making an effort to seem lively, he still looked tired and weak. His forehead, where enormous purplish bruises had spread overnight, was tied with a red handkerchief. His nose had also swollen considerably during the night, and several patches of bruising had appeared on it too. Slight as they were, they certainly gave his whole face a peculiarly vicious and irritable look. The old man knew this himself and gave Alyosha an unfriendly glance as he came in.

<!-- B04-C02-P005 -->
“The coffee's cold,” he shouted sharply. “I'm not offering you any. I'm having nothing but fish soup for the fast today, my boy, and I'm not inviting anyone. What brings you here?”

<!-- B04-C02-P006 -->
“To see how you are,” said Alyosha.

<!-- B04-C02-P007 -->
“Yes. And besides, I told you myself yesterday to come. It's all nonsense. You needn't have troubled yourself. Though I knew very well you'd come dragging yourself here straightaway...”

<!-- B04-C02-P008 -->
He said this with the utmost hostility. Meanwhile, he got up and anxiously examined his nose in the mirror, perhaps for the fortieth time that morning. He also began arranging the red handkerchief more becomingly on his forehead.

<!-- B04-C02-P009 -->
“Red's better. White makes you look as though you're in the hospital,” he remarked sententiously. “Well, what's your news? How's your elder?”

<!-- B04-C02-P010 -->
“He's very ill. He may die today,” Alyosha answered. But his father did not even hear him properly and at once forgot his own question.

<!-- B04-C02-P011 -->
“Ivan's gone out,” he suddenly said. “He's doing everything he can to steal Mitka's fiancée. That's what he's living here for,” he added spitefully, twisting his mouth and looking at Alyosha.

<!-- B04-C02-P012 -->
“Did he really tell you that himself?” Alyosha asked.

<!-- B04-C02-P013 -->
“Yes, and a good while ago. What do you think of that? Three weeks ago, he told me. Surely he hasn't come here to cut my throat on the quiet too? He must have come for something?”

<!-- B04-C02-P014 -->
“What are you saying! Why do you talk like that?” Alyosha was terribly disconcerted.

<!-- B04-C02-P015 -->
“He doesn't ask for money, that's true, but he won't get a damn thing from me all the same. I intend to live as long as I possibly can, my dearest Alexei Fyodorovich, let that be understood, so I need every kopeck, and the longer I live, the more I'll need it,” he went on, pacing from one corner of the room to the other, his hands in the pockets of his loose, greasy coat of yellow summer linen. “For now I'm still a man, only fifty-five, but I mean to stay in service as a man another twenty years. Only I'll grow old, I'll turn disgusting, and then they won't come to me of their own free will, and that's when I'll need my money. So now I'm putting away more and more, all for myself, my dear son Alexei Fyodorovich, let that be understood, because I mean to live in my filth to the very end, let that be understood. Life's sweeter in filth: everybody condemns it and everybody lives in it, only they do it secretly and I do it openly. That's why all those filthy creatures have turned on me: because I'm so straightforward. And I don't want your paradise, Alexei Fyodorovich, let that be understood. It would even be improper for a decent man to go into your paradise, supposing it exists at all. My view is: you fall asleep and don't wake up, and there's nothing. Remember me if you like, and if you don't, the devil take you. That's my philosophy. Ivan spoke well here yesterday, though we were all drunk. Ivan's a braggart, and he isn't so very learned... no particular education either. He keeps quiet and smiles at you without a word—that's all he's got to get by on.”

<!-- B04-C02-P016 -->
Alyosha listened without speaking.

<!-- B04-C02-P017 -->
“Why doesn't he talk to me? And when he does, it's all posing. Your Ivan's a scoundrel! As for Grushka, I'll marry her this instant if I feel like it. Because with money you only have to want something, Alexei Fyodorovich, and there it is. That's exactly what Ivan's afraid of, and he keeps watch over me to stop me marrying. That's why he's pushing Mitka to marry Grushka: that way he can keep me away from Grushka—as though I'd leave him any money if I didn't marry her!—and, on the other hand, if Mitka marries Grushka, Ivan can take his rich fiancée for himself. That's his calculation! Your Ivan's a scoundrel!”

<!-- B04-C02-P018 -->
“You're so irritable. It's because of yesterday. You ought to go and lie down,” Alyosha said.

<!-- B04-C02-P019 -->
“Now, when you say that,” the old man suddenly observed, as though the thought had only just occurred to him, “you say it, and I don't get angry with you. But if Ivan said the very same thing, I'd be angry. You're the only person I've ever had any kindly moments with. I'm a wicked man otherwise.”

<!-- B04-C02-P020 -->
“You're not wicked. You're warped,” Alyosha said with a smile.

<!-- B04-C02-P021 -->
“Listen, I was going to have that bandit Mitka locked up today, and even now I don't know what I'll decide. Of course, in these fashionable times fathers and mothers are supposed to be a mere prejudice, but I believe even nowadays the law doesn't let you drag your old father about by the hair and kick him in the face with your heels as he lies on the floor, in his own house, and threaten to come back and murder him—all in front of witnesses, mind you. If I wanted to, I could bring him to his knees and have him locked up this minute for what he did yesterday.”

<!-- B04-C02-P022 -->
“So you don't want to make a complaint, do you?”

<!-- B04-C02-P023 -->
“Ivan talked me out of it. I'd tell Ivan to go to hell, only I know a thing or two myself...”

<!-- B04-C02-P024 -->
Leaning toward Alyosha, he went on in a confidential half-whisper:

<!-- B04-C02-P025 -->
“If I have the scoundrel locked up, she'll hear I've had him locked up and run straight to him. But if she hears today that he beat me half to death, a feeble old man, then perhaps she'll leave him and come to see me... That's the sort of nature we've been blessed with—anything to be contrary. I know her through and through! What do you say to some cognac? Have a little cold coffee, and I'll pour in a quarter of a small spirits glass for you. Gives it a nice flavor, my boy.”

<!-- B04-C02-P026 -->
“No, I don't want any, thank you. I'll take this roll with me, if you'll let me,” said Alyosha. He picked up a three-kopeck French roll and put it in the pocket of his undercassock. “And you oughtn't to drink cognac either,” he advised cautiously, looking closely into the old man's face.

<!-- B04-C02-P027 -->
“You're right: it agitates me without giving me any peace. But just one little glass... I'll only get it from the cupboard...”

<!-- B04-C02-P028 -->
He unlocked the “cupboard,” poured a small glass, drank it, then locked the cupboard and put the key back in his pocket.

<!-- B04-C02-P029 -->
“And that's enough. One glass won't kill me.”

<!-- B04-C02-P030 -->
“There, you're kinder already,” Alyosha said with a smile.

<!-- B04-C02-P031 -->
“Hm! I love you without cognac too, but with scoundrels I'm a scoundrel myself. Vanka won't go to Chermashnya—why not? He needs to spy: how much will I give Grushenka if she comes? They're all scoundrels! I don't acknowledge Ivan as mine at all. Where did someone like that come from? His soul's nothing like ours. As though I'd leave him anything! I won't even leave a will, let that be understood. And I'll crush Mitka like a cockroach. At night I crush the black cockroaches with my slipper: they crack when you step on them. Your Mitka will crack too. Your Mitka, because you love him. You love him, and I'm not afraid of your loving him. But if Ivan loved him, I'd fear for myself because he loved him. Only Ivan doesn't love anyone. Ivan isn't one of us. People like Ivan, my boy, aren't our sort of people; they're dust that's been stirred up... The wind will blow, and the dust will pass... Yesterday, when I told you to come today, a stupid notion came into my head. I wanted to find out through you about Mitka: if I were to count out a thousand for him now, say two thousand, would he agree, the beggar and scoundrel, to clear out of here altogether, for five years, or better still thirty-five, without Grushka, and give her up completely, eh?”

<!-- B04-C02-P032 -->
“I... I'll ask him...” Alyosha murmured. “If it were all three thousand, then perhaps he...”

<!-- B04-C02-P033 -->
“You're lying! Don't ask him now, there's no need for anything! I've changed my mind. It was just a stupid notion that got into my stupid head yesterday. I won't give him anything, not a thing. I need my money myself,” the old man said, waving his hand. “I'll squash him like a cockroach without that. Don't say anything to him, or he'll start hoping. And you've no business here either. Off you go. That fiancée, Katerina Ivanovna, the one he's been so careful to keep hidden from me all this time—is she going to marry him or not? You went to see her yesterday, I think?”

<!-- B04-C02-P034 -->
“She won't leave him for anything.”

<!-- B04-C02-P035 -->
“That's who these tender young ladies love: rakes and scoundrels! Trash, I tell you, these pale young ladies. Give me... Well! If I had his youth and the face I had then—because I was better-looking than he is when I was twenty-eight—I'd make conquests just as he does. He's a rogue! But he won't get Grushenka all the same, no he won't, no he won't... I'll grind him into the dirt!”

<!-- B04-C02-P036 -->
The last words set him raging again.

<!-- B04-C02-P037 -->
“Off you go too. You've nothing to do here today,” he snapped.

<!-- B04-C02-P038 -->
Alyosha went up to say goodbye and kissed him on the shoulder.

<!-- B04-C02-P039 -->
“What's that for?” the old man asked, a little surprised. “We'll see each other again. Or do you think we won't?”

<!-- B04-C02-P040 -->
“Not at all. I just did it without thinking.”

<!-- B04-C02-P041 -->
“I didn't mean anything either, just saying...” The old man looked at him. “Listen, listen!” he called after him. “Come again sometime, soon, and have some fish soup. I'll make you fish soup, something special, not like today's. Be sure to come! Tomorrow, do you hear? Come tomorrow!”

<!-- B04-C02-P042 -->
As soon as Alyosha had gone out the door, he returned to the cupboard and tossed back another half a small glass.

<!-- B04-C02-P043 -->
“No more!” he muttered with a grunt, locked the cupboard again, put the key back in his pocket again, then went into the bedroom, lay down on the bed, exhausted, and fell asleep in an instant.
