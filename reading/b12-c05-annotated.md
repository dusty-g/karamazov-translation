# Book XII: A Judicial Error
## V. A Sudden Catastrophe

<!-- B12-C05-P001 -->
I should mention that he had already been called before Alyosha. But the bailiff had then reported to the presiding judge that, owing to a sudden indisposition or some kind of attack, the witness could not appear just then, though as soon as he recovered he would be ready to give his evidence whenever required. Somehow no one heard this at the time; it became known only afterward. His appearance went almost unnoticed at first: the principal witnesses, particularly the two rivals, had already been examined, and curiosity was satisfied for the moment. A certain weariness could even be felt in the audience. Several witnesses remained to be heard, but in view of all that had already been disclosed, they probably had nothing remarkable to contribute. Meanwhile, time was passing. Ivan Fyodorovich approached with an oddly slow step, looking at no one, his head even lowered as if he were frowning over some thought. He was impeccably dressed, but his face made a painful impression, on me at least: there was something almost earth-tinged about it, something like the face of a dying man. His eyes were clouded; he raised them and slowly looked around the hall. Alyosha suddenly started up from his chair and groaned, “Ah!” I remember this. But few people noticed that either.

<!-- B12-C05-P002 -->
The presiding judge began by saying that he was testifying without an oath, that he could give evidence or remain silent, but that, of course, whatever he said must be said in good conscience, and so on and so forth. Ivan Fyodorovich listened and gazed at him with clouded eyes; then suddenly a smile slowly began to spread across his face, and the moment the presiding judge, who was looking at him in surprise, finished speaking, he burst out laughing.

<!-- B12-C05-P003 -->
“Well, what else?” he asked loudly.

<!-- B12-C05-P004 -->
The whole courtroom fell silent, as though something had been sensed. The presiding judge grew uneasy.

<!-- B12-C05-P005 -->
“Perhaps you… are not yet quite well?” he began, looking around for the bailiff.

<!-- B12-C05-P006 -->
“Don’t worry, Your Excellency. I’m well enough, and I can tell you something interesting,” Ivan Fyodorovich replied, suddenly quite calm and respectful.

<!-- B12-C05-P007 -->
“You have some special information to present?” the presiding judge continued, still distrustful.

<!-- B12-C05-P008 -->
Ivan Fyodorovich lowered his eyes, paused for several seconds, then raised his head again and answered as though stammering:

<!-- B12-C05-P009 -->
“No… I haven’t. I have nothing special.”

<!-- B12-C05-P010 -->
They began putting questions to him. He answered with marked reluctance, as though making an effort to be brief, and even with a kind of disgust that steadily increased, though his answers remained sensible. On many points he pleaded ignorance. He knew nothing of his father’s accounts with Dmitri Fyodorovich. “And I took no interest in them,” he said. He had heard the defendant threaten to kill their father. He had heard about the money in the packet from Smerdyakov…

<!-- B12-C05-P011 -->
“It’s all the same thing,” he suddenly broke off, looking exhausted. “I have nothing special to tell the court.”

<!-- B12-C05-P012 -->
“I see that you are unwell, and I understand your feelings…” the presiding judge began.

<!-- B12-C05-P013 -->
He was turning to the two parties, the prosecutor and defense counsel, inviting them to put any questions they thought necessary, when Ivan Fyodorovich suddenly asked in an exhausted voice:

<!-- B12-C05-P014 -->
“Let me go, Your Excellency. I feel very ill.”

<!-- B12-C05-P015 -->
With these words, without waiting for permission, he suddenly turned of his own accord and started out of the hall. But after four steps he stopped, as though suddenly thinking something over, gave a quiet laugh, and returned to his former place.

<!-- B12-C05-P016 -->
“I’m like that peasant girl, Your Excellency… You know how it goes: ‘If I wants, I’ll jump in; if I wants, I won’t.’ They follow her about with a sarafan or a skirt of some kind, wanting her to jump into it so they can tie it on and take her off to be married, and she says: ‘If I wants, I’ll jump in; if I wants, I won’t’… It’s among one of our peoples…”

<!-- B12-C05-P017 -->
“What do you mean by this?” the presiding judge asked sternly.

<!-- B12-C05-P018 -->
“This.” Ivan Fyodorovich suddenly took out a bundle of money. “Here’s the money… the very money that was in that packet”—he nodded toward the table of material evidence—“and that my father was killed for. Where shall I put it? Mr. Bailiff, pass it over.”

<!-- B12-C05-P019 -->
The bailiff took the whole bundle and handed it to the presiding judge.

<!-- B12-C05-P020 -->
“How did this money come into your possession… if it is indeed the same money?” the presiding judge asked in astonishment.

<!-- B12-C05-P021 -->
“I got it from Smerdyakov, the murderer, yesterday. I went to see him before he hanged himself. He killed my father, not my brother. He killed him, and I taught him to kill him… Who doesn’t wish his father dead?…”

<!-- B12-C05-P022 -->
“Are you in your right mind?” escaped involuntarily from the presiding judge.

<!-- B12-C05-P023 -->
“That’s just it, I am… in my vile right mind, just like you, just like all these… m-mugs!” He suddenly turned toward the audience. “They’ve killed their father and pretend to be horrified,” he ground out with furious contempt. “Making faces at one another. Liars! They all want their fathers dead. One reptile devours another… If there hadn’t been a parricide, they’d all have been furious and gone away angry… Spectacles! ‘Bread and spectacles!’ Though I’m a fine one myself! Have you any water or not? Give me a drink, for Christ’s sake!” He suddenly clutched his head.

<!-- B12-C05-P024 -->
The bailiff immediately came toward him. Alyosha suddenly jumped up and cried, “He’s ill, don’t believe him, he has delirium tremens!” Katerina Ivanovna sprang up from her chair and stood motionless with horror, staring at Ivan Fyodorovich. Mitya rose and, with a strange, wild, twisted smile, watched and listened avidly to his brother.

<!-- B12-C05-P025 -->
“Calm down, I’m not mad, only a murderer!” Ivan began again. “You can’t demand eloquence from a murderer…” he suddenly added for some reason, and laughed with a twisted mouth.

<!-- B12-C05-P026 -->
The prosecutor, visibly agitated, leaned toward the presiding judge. The members of the court whispered hurriedly to one another. Fetyukovich was all ears. The hall held its breath in expectation. The presiding judge suddenly seemed to collect himself.

<!-- B12-C05-P027 -->
“Witness, your words are incomprehensible and inadmissible here. Calm yourself, if you can, and explain… if you really have anything to say. How can you substantiate such a confession… if you are not simply raving?”

<!-- B12-C05-P028 -->
“That’s just it, I have no witnesses. That dog Smerdyakov won’t send you a deposition from the next world… in a packet. You’re always wanting packets; one is enough. I have no witnesses… Except perhaps one.” He gave a thoughtful little laugh.

<!-- B12-C05-P029 -->
“Who is your witness?”

<!-- B12-C05-P030 -->
“He has a tail, Your Excellency; it won’t be in proper form! *Le diable n’existe point!*[^b12-c05-n001] Don’t pay any attention, he’s a shabby, petty little devil,” he added, abruptly ceasing to laugh and speaking almost confidentially. “He’s surely somewhere here, under that table with the material evidence. Where else would he sit? Look, listen to me: I told him I wouldn’t keep silent, and he starts about the geological upheaval… nonsense! Well, set the monster free… He’s started singing a hymn because he feels so lighthearted! Just like a drunken scoundrel bawling out ‘Vanka went to Petersburg,’ while I would give a quadrillion quadrillions for two seconds of joy. You don’t know me! Oh, how stupid it all is with you! Well, take me instead of him! I must have come for something… Why, why is everything there is so stupid!…”

<!-- B12-C05-P031 -->
Again he began slowly looking around the hall, as though deep in thought. But everyone was already in turmoil. Alyosha started toward him from his seat, but the bailiff had already taken Ivan Fyodorovich by the arm.

<!-- B12-C05-P032 -->
“What’s this now?” he cried, staring straight into the bailiff’s face, and suddenly seized him by the shoulders and furiously hurled him to the floor. But the guards were already there; they seized him, and he let out a frenzied scream. All the while they were carrying him away, he screamed and shouted something incoherent.

<!-- B12-C05-P033 -->
An uproar followed. I cannot remember everything in order; I was agitated myself and could not keep track. I know only that afterward, when everything had settled down and everyone understood what had happened, the bailiff came in for a severe reprimand, although he gave his superiors a sound explanation: the witness had been well all along; a doctor had seen him when he had suffered a slight faintness an hour earlier, but until entering the hall he had spoken coherently, so that nothing could have been foreseen; on the contrary, he himself had insisted and been determined to testify. But before anyone had begun to settle down or recover, another scene erupted immediately after this one: Katerina Ivanovna went into hysterics. She began sobbing with loud shrieks, but refused to leave, struggled, begged not to be taken away, and suddenly cried out to the presiding judge:

<!-- B12-C05-P034 -->
“I must give one more piece of evidence, immediately… immediately!… Here’s a paper, a letter… Take it, read it quickly, quickly! It’s this monster’s letter, this one, this one!” She pointed to Mitya. “He killed his father; you’ll see at once. He writes to me how he’ll kill his father! The other one is ill, ill, he has delirium tremens! I’ve seen for three days that he has a fever!”

<!-- B12-C05-P035 -->
She screamed this beside herself. The bailiff took the paper she was holding out to the presiding judge, while she fell onto her chair, covered her face, and began sobbing convulsively and soundlessly, shaking all over and suppressing the faintest groan for fear they would send her out of the hall. The paper she had handed over was that very letter Mitya had written at the Capital City tavern, which Ivan Fyodorovich had called a document of “mathematical” significance. Alas! That mathematical quality was precisely what they found in it, and without the letter Mitya might not have been destroyed, or at least not so terribly! I repeat, it was difficult to follow the details. Even now it all appears to me in such confusion. The presiding judge must have immediately communicated the new document to the court, the prosecutor, defense counsel, and the jury. I remember only how they began questioning the witness. When the presiding judge gently asked whether she had calmed down, Katerina Ivanovna cried eagerly:

<!-- B12-C05-P036 -->
“I’m ready, ready! I’m perfectly able to answer you,” she added, evidently still terribly afraid that for some reason they would not hear her. They asked her to explain more fully what this letter was and in what circumstances she had received it.

<!-- B12-C05-P037 -->
“I received it the day before the crime itself, and he wrote it from the tavern the day before that, so two days before his crime—look, it’s written on some sort of bill!” she cried breathlessly. “He hated me then because he himself had behaved vilely and gone after that creature… and also because he owed me those three thousand… Oh, those three thousand hurt his pride because of his own baseness! This is how those three thousand came about—I beg you, I implore you to hear me out: three weeks before he killed his father, he came to me in the morning. I knew he needed money, and I knew what for—yes, precisely to seduce that creature and take her away with him. I knew then that he had already betrayed me and wanted to leave me, and I, I myself held out the money to him, offered it myself, supposedly to send to my sister in Moscow. And as I gave it to him, I looked him in the face and said he could send it whenever he liked, ‘even in a month’s time.’ How, how could he fail to understand that I was saying straight to his face: ‘You need money to betray me with your creature; well, here’s the money, I’m giving it to you myself. Take it, if you’re dishonorable enough to take it!’ I wanted to expose him, and what happened? He took it, he took it and carried it away and spent it with that creature there, in a single night… But he understood, he understood that I knew everything. I assure you he understood then that by giving him the money I was merely testing him: would he be dishonorable enough to take it from me or not? I looked him in the eyes, and he looked me in the eyes and understood everything, everything, and took it, took it and carried off my money!”

<!-- B12-C05-P038 -->
“It’s true, Katya!” Mitya suddenly howled. “I looked you in the eyes and understood that you were dishonoring me, and still I took your money! Despise the scoundrel, all of you despise me! I deserve it!”

<!-- B12-C05-P039 -->
“Defendant!” the presiding judge cried. “One more word and I shall have you removed.”

<!-- B12-C05-P040 -->
“That money tormented him,” Katya continued in a convulsive rush. “He wanted to give it back to me, he did, that’s true, but he needed money for that creature too. So he killed his father, and still didn’t return my money, but went away with her to that village where he was arrested. There he squandered again the money he had stolen from the father he murdered. And the day before he killed his father he wrote me this letter, wrote it drunk—I saw that at once—wrote it out of spite and knowing, knowing for certain that I wouldn’t show the letter to anyone, even if he did kill him. Otherwise he wouldn’t have written it. He knew I wouldn’t want to take revenge and destroy him! But read it, read it carefully, please, more carefully, and you’ll see that in the letter he described everything, everything beforehand: how he would kill his father and where his money lay. Look, please don’t overlook it, there’s one phrase: ‘I’ll kill him, if only Ivan leaves.’ So he had already planned how he would kill him,” Katerina Ivanovna prompted the court with spiteful, gloating malice. Oh, one could see that she had read this fatal letter to its depths and studied every stroke in it. “If he hadn’t been drunk, he wouldn’t have written to me, but look, everything is described in advance, exactly as he later killed him, the whole program!”

<!-- B12-C05-P041 -->
Thus she cried out beside herself, certainly scorning all the consequences for herself, although of course she had foreseen them perhaps a month earlier, for even then, perhaps trembling with hatred, she had dreamed: “Should I read this to the court?” Now she seemed to be hurtling down a mountainside. I remember, I think it was at this very point, the clerk read the letter aloud, and it made a shattering impression. Mitya was asked whether he acknowledged the letter as his.

<!-- B12-C05-P042 -->
“Mine, mine!” Mitya exclaimed. “If I hadn’t been drunk, I wouldn’t have written it!… We hated each other for many things, Katya, but I swear, I swear, even while I hated you I loved you, and you never loved me!”

<!-- B12-C05-P043 -->
He collapsed into his seat, wringing his hands in despair. The prosecutor and defense counsel began questioning her in turn, chiefly to this effect: “What prompted you to conceal such a document earlier and to testify in an entirely different spirit and tone?”

<!-- B12-C05-P044 -->
“Yes, yes, I lied earlier, it was all lies, against my honor and conscience, but I wanted to save him then because he hated and despised me so much,” Katya cried like a madwoman. “Oh, he despised me terribly, always despised me, and do you know, do you know—he despised me from the very moment I bowed down at his feet for that money. I saw it… I felt it at once, but for a long time I wouldn’t believe myself. How often I read in his eyes: ‘Still, you came to me of your own accord that time.’ Oh, he didn’t understand, he understood nothing of why I ran to him then; he’s only capable of suspecting baseness! He judged others by himself, he thought everyone was like him,” Katya ground out furiously, now utterly frenzied. “And he only wanted to marry me because I’d received an inheritance, that’s why, that’s why! I always suspected that was why! Oh, he’s a beast! He was certain for all his life that I would tremble before him all my life with shame because I had come to him then, and that he could forever despise me for it and so hold the upper hand—that’s why he wanted to marry me! It’s true, all of it’s true! I tried to conquer him with my love, with endless love, I was even prepared to endure his betrayal, but he understood nothing, nothing. How could he understand anything! He’s a monster! I didn’t receive that letter until the following evening; they brought it to me from the tavern, and that very morning, that very morning, I had been ready to forgive him everything, everything, even his betrayal!”

<!-- B12-C05-P045 -->
Of course, the presiding judge and the prosecutor tried to calm her. I am sure that perhaps they all felt ashamed themselves to take advantage of her frenzy and listen to such confessions. I remember hearing them say, “We understand how hard this is for you; believe us, we are capable of feeling,” and so on and so forth—but they extracted all the evidence from the crazed, hysterical woman nonetheless. Finally she described, with that extraordinary clarity which so often flashes out, if only for a moment, even in such states of extreme tension, how throughout these two months Ivan Fyodorovich had almost driven himself mad trying to save “the monster and murderer,” his brother.

<!-- B12-C05-P046 -->
“He tormented himself,” she cried. “He kept trying to lessen his brother’s guilt, confessing to me that he too had disliked his father and perhaps had wished him dead himself. Oh, what a deep, deep conscience! His conscience tortured him! He told me everything, everything; he came and talked to me every day as his only friend. I have the honor of being his only friend!” she suddenly exclaimed, as if in challenge, her eyes flashing. “He went to Smerdyakov twice. Once he came to me and said: if it wasn’t my brother who killed him, but Smerdyakov—because everyone here was spreading that fable, that Smerdyakov had killed him—then perhaps I’m guilty too, because Smerdyakov knew I disliked my father and perhaps thought I wanted him dead. Then I took out this letter and showed him, and he became absolutely convinced his brother had killed him, and it utterly crushed him. He couldn’t bear his own brother being a parricide! A week ago I could already see it was making him ill. In the last few days he would sit with me and rave. I saw he was losing his mind. He walked about raving; people saw him like that in the streets. At my request the visiting doctor examined him the day before yesterday and told me he was on the verge of a fever—all because of him, all because of the monster! And yesterday he learned that Smerdyakov was dead; the shock was so great that he went mad… and all because of the monster, all in trying to save the monster!”

<!-- B12-C05-P047 -->
Oh, of course, one can speak like this and confess like this only once in a lifetime—at the moment before death, for instance, mounting the scaffold. But Katya was entirely herself, and this was her moment. She was the same impetuous Katya who had rushed to the young libertine to save her father; the same Katya who, proud and chaste, had earlier sacrificed herself and her maidenly shame before this whole audience, telling of “Mitya’s noble deed” merely to soften, however slightly, the fate awaiting him. And now she had sacrificed herself in just the same way, but for someone else, perhaps only now, only at this moment, feeling and fully realizing for the first time how dear that other man was to her! She sacrificed herself in terror for him, suddenly imagining that he had ruined himself with his testimony that he, and not his brother, had killed their father; she sacrificed herself to save him, his good name, his reputation! Yet something terrible had flashed through it all: had she lied about Mitya in describing their former relationship? That was the question. No, no, she had not deliberately slandered him when she cried that Mitya despised her for her bow to the ground! She believed it herself; she had been profoundly convinced, perhaps from the very moment of that bow, that the simplehearted Mitya, who still adored her then, was laughing at her and despising her. It was only out of pride that she had then bound herself to him in a hysterical, strained love, out of wounded pride, and that love resembled revenge rather than love. Oh, perhaps that strained love might have turned into a real love; perhaps Katya wanted nothing else. But Mitya had wounded her to the depths of her soul by betraying her, and her soul had not forgiven him. The moment for revenge had descended unexpectedly, and all that had accumulated so long and painfully in the wronged woman’s breast burst out at once, again unexpectedly. She had betrayed Mitya, but she had betrayed herself too! And of course, scarcely had she finished speaking when the tension snapped and shame overwhelmed her. The hysterics began again; she fell down, sobbing and crying out. They carried her away. At the moment they were taking her out, Grushenka rushed toward Mitya from her seat with a scream, so suddenly that they had no time to stop her.

<!-- B12-C05-P048 -->
“Mitya!” she howled. “Your snake has destroyed you! There, she’s shown you what she is!” she shouted at the court, shaking with fury. At a sign from the presiding judge they seized her and began leading her from the hall. She resisted, struggling and straining back toward Mitya. Mitya shouted and lunged toward her too. They restrained him.

<!-- B12-C05-P049 -->
Yes, I suppose our lady spectators were satisfied: the spectacle had been lavish. Next I remember the visiting Moscow doctor appearing. I think the presiding judge had already sent the bailiff to arrange help for Ivan Fyodorovich. The doctor reported to the court that the patient was suffering a most dangerous attack of fever and ought to be taken away immediately. In answer to the prosecutor’s and defense counsel’s questions, he confirmed that the patient had come to him of his own accord the day before yesterday, and that he had then predicted the imminent onset of fever, but the patient had refused treatment. “He was certainly not in a sound state of mind; he himself admitted that he saw visions while awake, met various people in the street who were already dead, and that Satan came to visit him every evening,” the doctor concluded. After giving his evidence, the famous physician withdrew. The letter produced by Katerina Ivanovna was added to the material evidence. After conferring, the court resolved to continue the examination and enter both unexpected testimonies, Katerina Ivanovna’s and Ivan Fyodorovich’s, in the record.

<!-- B12-C05-P050 -->
But I shall not describe the further examination of the evidence. The remaining witnesses’ testimony merely repeated and confirmed what had gone before, though each had its distinctive features. But, I repeat, everything will be brought into a single focus in the prosecutor’s speech, to which I shall now turn. Everyone was excited, everyone electrified by the latest catastrophe and burning with impatience for the conclusion, the speeches of the two sides, and the verdict. Fetyukovich was visibly shaken by Katerina Ivanovna’s testimony. The prosecutor, however, was triumphant. When the examination ended, a recess was announced which lasted nearly an hour. At last the presiding judge opened the arguments. I think it was exactly eight in the evening when our prosecutor, Ippolit Kirillovich, began his speech for the prosecution.
