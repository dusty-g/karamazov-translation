# Book III: The Lustful
## Chapter II · Lizaveta the Stinking

<!-- B03-C02-P001 -->
There was one particular circumstance here that shook Grigory deeply, finally confirming an old, ugly, revolting suspicion of his. This Lizaveta the Stinking was a very short girl, “a little over two arshins,” as many of the devout old women in our town fondly recalled after her death. Her healthy, broad, rosy face, the face of a twenty-year-old, was wholly that of an idiot; her eyes had a fixed and unpleasant gaze, though a meek one. All her life, summer and winter, she went barefoot, dressed in nothing but a coarse hemp shift. Her almost black hair, extraordinarily thick and curled like a sheep’s fleece, sat on her head like some enormous cap. It was always caked with earth and dirt, too, with little leaves, splinters, and wood shavings stuck in it, because she always slept on the ground, in the dirt. Her father, Ilya, was a homeless, ruined, sickly townsman, a heavy drinker who had lived for years as a sort of workman in the household of a well-to-do family, also townspeople of ours. Lizaveta’s mother had long been dead. Perpetually ill and ill-tempered, Ilya beat Lizaveta mercilessly when she came home. But she seldom did, for she found shelter all over town as a holy fool, one of God’s people.

Ilya’s employers, Ilya himself, and even many compassionate people in town, chiefly merchants and their wives, had tried more than once to dress Lizaveta more decently than in a shift alone. Every winter they put her in a sheepskin coat and boots; but as a rule, after letting them dress her without a word of protest, she would walk away and somewhere—usually on the cathedral porch—unfailingly take off everything she had been given, whether a kerchief, a skirt, a sheepskin coat, or boots, leave it all on the spot, and go off barefoot in her shift as before. Once the new governor of our province paid our town an inspection visit and had his finer feelings deeply offended by the sight of Lizaveta. Although he understood that she was a “holy fool,” as he had been informed, he nevertheless pointed out that a young girl roaming about in nothing but a shift was an offense against decency and ordered that it should not happen again. But the governor left, and Lizaveta was left as she was.

At last her father died, and being an orphan made her all the dearer to every devout person in town. Indeed, everyone seemed positively to love her. Even the boys neither teased nor hurt her, and our boys, especially the schoolboys, are a rowdy lot. She went into houses where she knew no one, and nobody drove her out; on the contrary, everyone had a kind word for her and gave her a small coin. If they gave her a coin, she took it and immediately carried it off to drop into a collection box at a church or the jail. If they gave her a bread ring or a little roll at the market, she invariably went and gave the bread ring or the roll to the first child she met; or else she would stop one of our very richest ladies and give it to her, and the ladies were even glad to accept. She herself ate nothing but black bread and water. She would go into a rich merchant’s shop and sit down, with costly goods lying about and money too, and the owners were never wary of her: they knew they could lay out thousands in cash in front of her and forget it, and she would not take a single kopeck.

She seldom went inside a church, but slept either on church porches or in someone’s vegetable garden, having climbed over the wattle fence—we still have a great many wattle fences here instead of board fences, even today. She came home, that is, to the household where her late father had lived, about once a week; in winter she came every day, but only for the night, sleeping either in the entryway or in the cowshed. People marveled that she could endure such a life, but she had grown used to it; short though she was, she had an exceptionally sturdy constitution. Some of our gentlemen insisted that she did all this merely out of pride, but somehow that did not fit: she could not speak a single word, and only occasionally moved her tongue about and made a lowing sound—what pride could there be in that?

Now it happened once—this was a long time ago—that on a clear, warm September night, under a full moon, at an hour already very late by our standards, a drunken band of our gentlemen, five or six fellows who had been out enjoying themselves, were coming home from the club “by the back way.” Wattle fences lined both sides of the lane, with the neighboring houses’ vegetable gardens beyond them; the lane led to a little footbridge over our long, stinking puddle, which we sometimes choose to call a river. Beside the fence, among the nettles and burdocks, our party spotted Lizaveta asleep. The tipsy gentlemen stopped over her, laughing loudly, and began making the filthiest jokes they could. One young gentleman suddenly thought of a thoroughly eccentric question on an unthinkable subject: “Could anyone at all possibly consider a beast like that a woman—right now, for instance,” and so on. With lofty disgust, they all decided no one could. But Fyodor Pavlovich happened to be in the group, and he immediately sprang forward and declared that one could consider her a woman, very much so, and that there was even something peculiarly piquant about it, and so on and so forth.

It is true that in those days he went to quite elaborate lengths to thrust himself forward as our local buffoon. He liked to jump in and amuse the gentlemen, ostensibly as their equal, of course, but in reality as an utterly servile lout before them. This was precisely the time when he had received the news from Petersburg of the death of his first wife, Adelaida Ivanovna, and, with mourning crape on his hat, was drinking and behaving so outrageously that some people in town, even among the most dissolute, winced at the sight of him. The party naturally roared with laughter at this unexpected opinion; one of them even began egging Fyodor Pavlovich on, while the others spat in disgust more vigorously than ever, though still hugely amused, and at last they all went on their way. Later Fyodor Pavlovich swore that he too had gone off with the others. Perhaps he had done just that; no one knows for certain, and no one ever did. But five or six months later everyone in town was talking, with sincere and tremendous indignation, about the fact that Lizaveta was pregnant. People questioned one another and tried to find out: whose sin was it, who had wronged her?

It was then that a strange rumor suddenly spread through the whole town: the man who had wronged her was none other than this same Fyodor Pavlovich. Where had the rumor come from? By then only one member of that party of gentlemen had happened to remain in town, and he was an elderly and respected state councilor, with a family and grown daughters, who certainly would not have spread any stories even if something had happened; the other members of the party, five or so, had gone away by then. But the rumor pointed straight at Fyodor Pavlovich and went on pointing. Of course, he did not exactly lay claim to it: he would not even have bothered answering a few petty merchants or townspeople. At that time he was proud, and would speak only to his own set of officials and noblemen, whom he entertained so well.

It was now that Grigory stood up for his master with all his energy and strength, not merely defending him against all these slanders but quarreling and arguing on his behalf, and he changed many people’s minds. “It’s her own fault, the low creature,” he declared, and the man who had wronged her was none other than “Karp with the Screw”—the nickname of a fearsome convict, well known in town at the time, who had recently escaped from the provincial jail and was living secretly among us. This conjecture seemed plausible. People remembered Karp; in particular, they remembered that on those very nights, toward autumn, he had been prowling around town and had robbed three people. But the whole affair and all the talk did nothing to diminish the general sympathy for the poor holy fool; everyone took still greater care to protect and watch over her. A merchant’s widow named Kondratyeva, a well-to-do woman, even arranged to take Lizaveta into her house as early as the end of April, intending to keep her there until the birth. They watched her tirelessly, but despite all their vigilance, on the very last day, in the evening, Lizaveta suddenly slipped out of Kondratyeva’s house and found herself in Fyodor Pavlovich’s garden.

How she had climbed over the garden’s high, solid fence in her condition remained something of a mystery. Some insisted that “someone had carried her over”; others, that “something had carried her over.” Most likely it had all happened in a quite natural way, however difficult: Lizaveta, who knew how to climb over wattle fences into other people’s vegetable gardens to sleep, had somehow scrambled up Fyodor Pavlovich’s fence as well and jumped down into the garden, though she hurt herself in doing so, despite her condition. Grigory rushed to Marfa Ignatyevna and sent her to help Lizaveta, while he ran for an old midwife, a townswoman who fortunately lived nearby. They saved the baby, but by dawn Lizaveta had died.

Grigory picked up the infant, brought him into the house, sat his wife down, and laid him in her lap, against her breast. “An orphan is God’s child and kin to everyone, and all the more to you and me. Our little departed one has sent us this child, though he was born of a son of the devil and a righteous woman. Feed him, and weep no more.”

And so Marfa Ignatyevna raised the baby. He was baptized and named Pavel, and everyone, of their own accord, without being told, began giving him the patronymic Fyodorovich.[^b03-c02-n001] Fyodor Pavlovich objected to none of this and even found it all amusing, though he went on denying everything as vigorously as he could. People in town liked the fact that he had taken in the foundling. Later Fyodor Pavlovich invented a surname for the foundling too: he called him Smerdyakov, after his mother’s nickname, Lizaveta the Stinking. This was the very Smerdyakov who became Fyodor Pavlovich’s second servant and who, at the beginning of our story, lived in the outbuilding with old Grigory and old Marfa. He was employed as the cook. There is a good deal I ought to add about him in particular, but I am ashamed to keep my reader’s attention diverted for so long by such ordinary lackeys, and so I shall return to my story, trusting that something about Smerdyakov will find its way in of its own accord as the narrative goes on.

[^b03-c02-n001]: A Russian patronymic comes from a father’s first name. Calling Pavel “Fyodorovich” links him to Fyodor Pavlovich in the townspeople’s eyes; the chapter leaves his paternity uncertain.
