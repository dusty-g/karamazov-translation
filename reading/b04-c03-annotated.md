# Book IV: Strains
## III. Getting Mixed Up with Schoolboys

<!-- B04-C03-P001 -->
“Thank God he didn't ask me about Grushenka,” Alyosha thought in his turn, leaving his father's house and heading for Madame Khokhlakov's. “I might have had to tell him about meeting Grushenka yesterday.” He felt with pain that the combatants had gathered fresh strength overnight, and with the coming of day their hearts had hardened again. “Father is angry and bitter; he's thought up something and set his mind on it. And Dmitri? He's rallied overnight too, he must be angry and bitter too, and of course he's thought up something as well… Oh, I must find time to track him down today, whatever happens…”

<!-- B04-C03-P002 -->
But Alyosha had little time for reflection: something happened on his way that, though seemingly of no great importance, made a deep impression on him. He had just crossed the square and turned into a lane leading to Mikhailovskaya Street, which ran parallel to the main street and was separated from it only by a ditch—our whole town is crisscrossed with ditches—when he saw a small cluster of schoolboys down below, in front of the little bridge. They were all young children, from nine to twelve, no older. They were on their way home from lessons, some with satchels on their backs, others with leather bags slung from straps over their shoulders; some wore jackets, others overcoats, and a few had high boots with gathered tops, the sort small children spoiled by well-to-do fathers especially like to show off in. The whole group was talking animatedly about something, apparently holding a council. Alyosha could never pass children without taking an interest; he had been the same in Moscow. Although he loved children of about three best of all, he was very fond of schoolboys of ten or eleven too. And so, worried as he was just now, he suddenly felt like going over and talking to them. As he approached, he studied their rosy, lively faces and suddenly saw that every boy had a stone in his hand, and some had two. Across the ditch, about thirty paces from the group, another boy stood by a fence, also a schoolboy, also with a bag at his side. Judging by his height he was no more than ten, perhaps younger: a pale, sickly child with flashing black eyes. He was watching the group of six schoolboys intently and searchingly. They were plainly his schoolmates, who had just come out of school with him, but he was evidently at war with them. Alyosha came up to a rosy-cheeked boy with curly fair hair and a black jacket, looked him over, and remarked:

<!-- B04-C03-P003 -->
“When I had a bag like yours, we used to wear them on the left, so we could get things out straightaway with our right hand. Yours is on the right. It must be awkward getting things out.”

<!-- B04-C03-P004 -->
Alyosha began with this practical observation without any calculated cunning. Yet there is no other way for a grown-up to begin if he wants to win a child's confidence at once, especially the confidence of a whole group of children. He must start seriously and practically, as an absolute equal. Alyosha understood this by instinct.

<!-- B04-C03-P005 -->
“But he's left-handed,” another boy answered at once, a sturdy, spirited lad of about eleven. All five of the other boys stared at Alyosha.

<!-- B04-C03-P006 -->
“He throws stones left-handed too,” a third boy observed. Just then a stone flew into the group, grazed the left-handed boy, and went on past, though it had been thrown skillfully and hard. The boy across the ditch had thrown it.

<!-- B04-C03-P007 -->
“Get him, Smurov! Let him have it!” they all shouted. But Smurov, the left-handed boy, had already acted without waiting to be told. He paid him back at once, throwing a stone at the boy across the ditch, but missed: the stone struck the ground. The boy across the ditch immediately sent another stone into the group, this time straight at Alyosha, hitting him quite painfully on the shoulder. The boy had a whole pocketful of stones ready. Even thirty paces away you could see it from the bulging pockets of his little overcoat.

<!-- B04-C03-P008 -->
“He meant that for you, for you! He was aiming at you on purpose. You're Karamazov, aren't you? Karamazov?” the boys shouted, laughing. “Right, all at once! Fire!”

<!-- B04-C03-P009 -->
Six stones flew out of the group at once. One hit the boy on the head, and he fell, but he leaped up instantly and began hurling stones back at the group in a fury. An unbroken exchange of fire began, for many of the boys in the group turned out to have stones ready in their pockets too.

<!-- B04-C03-P010 -->
“What are you doing? Aren't you ashamed, gentlemen? Six against one—you'll kill him!” Alyosha cried.

<!-- B04-C03-P011 -->
He sprang forward into the path of the flying stones to shield the boy across the ditch with his own body. Three or four of them stopped for a moment.

<!-- B04-C03-P012 -->
“He started it!” shouted a boy in a red shirt, his childish voice angry. “He's a little brute. He stabbed Krasotkin with a penknife in class just now, and made him bleed. Only Krasotkin wouldn't tell on him. He ought to get a beating…”

<!-- B04-C03-P013 -->
“But what for? You've been teasing him yourselves, haven't you?”

<!-- B04-C03-P014 -->
“There, he's thrown another stone at your back. He knows you!” the children shouted. “He's throwing at you now, not us. Come on, everyone, at him again! Don't miss, Smurov!”

<!-- B04-C03-P015 -->
The exchange began again, viciously this time. A stone hit the boy across the ditch in the chest. He cried out, burst into tears, and ran up the hill toward Mikhailovskaya Street. The group raised a clamor: “Aha, scared! Running away! Scrubbing rag!”

<!-- B04-C03-P016 -->
“You don't know what a little brute he is, Karamazov. Killing him wouldn't be enough,” repeated the boy in the jacket, his eyes blazing. He seemed to be the oldest of them all.

<!-- B04-C03-P017 -->
“What's so bad about him?” asked Alyosha. “Is he a telltale?” The boys exchanged glances, as though with a smirk.

<!-- B04-C03-P018 -->
“Are you going that way too, to Mikhailovskaya Street?” the same boy went on. “Then catch up with him… Look, see, he's stopped again. He's waiting and watching you.”

<!-- B04-C03-P019 -->
“Watching you, watching you!” the boys took up.

<!-- B04-C03-P020 -->
“Go on, ask him if he likes a bathhouse scrubbing rag, all frayed. You hear? Ask him just that.”

<!-- B04-C03-P021 -->
They all burst out laughing. Alyosha looked at them, and they looked at him.

<!-- B04-C03-P022 -->
“Don't go, he'll hurt you!” Smurov called out in warning.

<!-- B04-C03-P023 -->
“Gentlemen, I won't ask him about the scrubbing rag, because that's probably some way you have of teasing him. But I'll find out from him why you hate him so much…”

<!-- B04-C03-P024 -->
“You find out, you find out!” the boys laughed.

<!-- B04-C03-P025 -->
Alyosha crossed the little bridge and went uphill along the fence, straight toward the boy in disgrace.

<!-- B04-C03-P026 -->
“Look out!” they called after him in warning. “He won't be scared of you. He'll stab you before you know it, when you're not looking… Like Krasotkin.”

<!-- B04-C03-P027 -->
The boy waited for him without moving. Close up, Alyosha saw a child of no more than nine, frail and undersized, with a thin, pale, rather long face and large dark eyes that glared at him. He wore a shabby old overcoat he had outgrown so badly that it looked grotesque. His bare arms stuck out of the sleeves. There was a large patch on the right knee of his trousers, and a large hole in the toe of his right boot, over the big toe, plainly thickly smeared with ink. Both bulging pockets of his coat were crammed with stones. Alyosha stopped two paces away, looking at him inquiringly. The boy immediately guessed from Alyosha's eyes that he did not mean to hit him, and lowered his defiant guard, even speaking first.

<!-- B04-C03-P028 -->
“There's one of me and six of them… I'll beat the whole lot of them on my own,” he said suddenly, his eyes flashing.

<!-- B04-C03-P029 -->
“One of those stones must have hurt you badly,” Alyosha remarked.

<!-- B04-C03-P030 -->
“Well, I hit Smurov on the head!” the boy cried.

<!-- B04-C03-P031 -->
“They told me back there that you know me, and that you threw a stone at me for some reason?” Alyosha asked.

<!-- B04-C03-P032 -->
The boy looked at him darkly.

<!-- B04-C03-P033 -->
“I don't know you. Do you know me?” Alyosha pressed him.

<!-- B04-C03-P034 -->
“Leave me alone!” the boy suddenly shouted angrily. Yet he did not move, as though he were still waiting for something, and his eyes flashed with malice again.

<!-- B04-C03-P035 -->
“All right, I'll go,” said Alyosha. “Only I don't know you, and I'm not teasing you. They told me what they call you to tease you, but I don't want to tease you. Goodbye!”

<!-- B04-C03-P036 -->
“Monk in silk trousers!” the boy shouted, following Alyosha with the same angry, challenging stare and taking up a fighting stance, expecting Alyosha to come at him now for certain. But Alyosha turned, looked at him, and walked away. He had not gone three steps when the largest cobblestone the boy had in his pocket struck him painfully in the back.

<!-- B04-C03-P037 -->
“So you do it from behind? Then what they say about you is true—you attack people when they're not looking?” Alyosha turned around again, but this time the boy furiously flung another stone straight at his face. Alyosha managed to put up his arm in time, and the stone hit his elbow.

<!-- B04-C03-P038 -->
“Aren't you ashamed? What have I done to you?” he cried.

<!-- B04-C03-P039 -->
Silent and defiant, the boy waited for just one thing: surely now Alyosha would come at him. When he saw that even now Alyosha did not, he flew into a rage like a small wild animal. He sprang forward and hurled himself at Alyosha, and before Alyosha could move, the furious boy bent his head, seized Alyosha's left hand in both of his, and bit his middle finger hard. He sank his teeth into it and held on for about ten seconds. Alyosha cried out in pain, tugging at his finger with all his strength. At last the boy let go and sprang back to where he had been. The finger had been badly bitten, deep, right to the bone, beside the nail; blood streamed from it. Alyosha took out a handkerchief and wrapped his injured hand tightly in it. It took him almost a full minute. The boy stood waiting all that time. At last Alyosha raised his quiet eyes to him.

<!-- B04-C03-P040 -->
“All right,” he said. “You see how badly you've bitten me. That's enough now, isn't it? Now tell me, what have I done to you?”

<!-- B04-C03-P041 -->
The boy looked at him in surprise.

<!-- B04-C03-P042 -->
“Although I don't know you at all and I've never seen you before,” Alyosha went on just as calmly, “I must have done something to you. You wouldn't hurt me like this for nothing. So tell me, what have I done? How have I wronged you?”

<!-- B04-C03-P043 -->
Instead of answering, the boy suddenly burst into loud, unrestrained sobs and suddenly ran away from Alyosha. Alyosha followed slowly into Mikhailovskaya Street, and for a long time he could still see the boy running in the distance, never slowing down or looking back, and surely still sobbing just as loudly. He resolved to find him as soon as he had time, without fail, and get to the bottom of this mystery, which had struck him so deeply. Just now he had no time.
