# Book II, Chapter V
## So Be It, So Be It!

*Part I · Book II: An Inappropriate Gathering*

<!-- B02-C05-P001 -->
The elder had been away from his cell for about twenty-five minutes. It was already after half past twelve, and Dmitri Fyodorovich, for whose sake everyone had gathered, still had not appeared. But they seemed almost to have forgotten him, and when the elder came back into the cell he found his guests deep in a lively general discussion. Ivan Fyodorovich and the two hieromonks were doing most of the talking. Miusov was joining in too, and apparently with considerable heat, but once again things were going badly for him; he had plainly been relegated to the background, and they scarcely even answered him, so that this new circumstance only added to his mounting irritation. The fact was that he and Ivan Fyodorovich had already sparred a little over how much each of them knew, and he could not calmly endure Ivan's somewhat offhand treatment of him. “Until now, at least, I've kept abreast of everything progressive in Europe, and this new generation simply ignores us,” he thought. Fyodor Pavlovich, who had himself promised to sit down in his chair and keep quiet, did in fact keep quiet for a while, but he watched his neighbor Pyotr Alexandrovich with a mocking little smile, plainly enjoying his irritation. He had long meant to pay him back for a thing or two and had no intention of missing his chance now. At last he could bear it no longer, leaned toward his neighbor's shoulder, and teased him once more in an undertone:

<!-- B02-C05-P002 -->
“Why didn't you leave earlier, after ‘lovingly did kiss it’? Why did you agree to stay in such disgraceful company? Because you felt humiliated and insulted, and stayed to get your own back by showing off your intelligence. You won't leave now until you've shown them how clever you are.”

<!-- B02-C05-P003 -->
“You again? On the contrary, I'm leaving this instant.”

<!-- B02-C05-P004 -->
“Later, later than anyone!” Fyodor Pavlovich gave him another jab. This was almost at the very moment the elder returned.

<!-- B02-C05-P005 -->
The argument fell silent for a moment, but the elder, sitting down in his old place, looked around at them all as though kindly inviting them to go on. Alyosha, who had studied almost every expression on his face, could see clearly that he was terribly tired and forcing himself to carry on. Lately he had been having fainting spells from exhaustion. Almost the same pallor that preceded those spells was spreading over his face now; his lips had turned white. But he plainly did not want to dismiss the gathering; it seemed he had some purpose of his own in this—but what? Alyosha watched him closely.

<!-- B02-C05-P006 -->
“We're discussing his most interesting article,” said the librarian, Hieromonk Joseph, addressing the elder and indicating Ivan Fyodorovich. “He draws a great many new conclusions, though the idea seems to cut both ways. On the question of church courts exercising public jurisdiction and the extent of their authority, he has replied in a journal article to a clergyman who wrote a whole book on the subject…”

<!-- B02-C05-P007 -->
“Unfortunately I haven't read your article, though I've heard of it,” the elder replied, studying Ivan Fyodorovich with a keen, searching gaze.

<!-- B02-C05-P008 -->
“He takes a most interesting position,” Father Librarian continued. “On the question of church courts exercising public jurisdiction, he apparently rejects the separation of church and state altogether.”

<!-- B02-C05-P009 -->
“That's interesting, but in what sense?” the elder asked Ivan Fyodorovich.

<!-- B02-C05-P010 -->
At last Ivan answered him, but without the condescending politeness Alyosha had still been afraid of the day before; he spoke modestly and with restraint, with evident courtesy and apparently without the slightest ulterior motive.

<!-- B02-C05-P011 -->
“I start from the proposition that this mingling of elements—that is, of the essential natures of church and state, taken separately—will of course go on forever, even though it is impossible and can never be made normal, or even brought into any workable accord, because a falsehood lies at the very foundation of the matter. A compromise between state and church in such questions as the administration of justice is, I believe, impossible in its complete and pure form. The clergyman I was answering maintains that the church occupies a precise and definite place within the state. I objected that, on the contrary, the church must contain the entire state within itself, rather than merely occupy some corner of it, and that if for some reason this is impossible now, the very nature of things means that it must unquestionably be made the direct and principal aim of all further development of Christian society.”

<!-- B02-C05-P012 -->
“Absolutely right!” Father Paisius, the silent, learned hieromonk, said firmly, with nervous intensity.

<!-- B02-C05-P013 -->
“Ultramontanism, pure and simple!”[^b02-c05-n001] Miusov cried, impatiently crossing one leg over the other.

<!-- B02-C05-P014 -->
“Ah, but we haven't even got any mountains!” Father Joseph exclaimed, then continued, turning to the elder: “Among other things, he answers the following ‘fundamental and essential’ propositions of his opponent—a clergyman, mind you. First: that ‘no social association can or should arrogate to itself the power to dispose of the civil and political rights of its members.’ Second: that ‘jurisdiction in criminal and civil cases must not belong to the church and is incompatible with its nature both as a divine institution and as an association of people for religious purposes.’ And third and last: that ‘the church is a kingdom not of this world’…”

<!-- B02-C05-P015 -->
“A most unworthy play on words for a clergyman!” Father Paisius could no longer hold back and interrupted again. “I read the book you were answering,” he said to Ivan Fyodorovich, “and was astonished at a clergyman's saying that ‘the church is a kingdom not of this world.’ If it is not of this world, then it cannot exist on earth at all. The words ‘not of this world’ are not used in that sense in the Holy Gospel. Such words are not to be played with. Our Lord Jesus Christ came precisely to establish the church on earth. The kingdom of heaven is, of course, not of this world, but in heaven, yet it can only be entered through the church, which was founded and established on earth. Secular wordplay of this kind is therefore inadmissible and unworthy. The church is truly a kingdom, destined to reign, and in the end it must assuredly appear as a kingdom over all the earth—for this we have the promise…”

<!-- B02-C05-P016 -->
He suddenly fell silent, as though checking himself. Having listened to him respectfully and attentively, Ivan Fyodorovich continued with extraordinary calm, but as willingly and unaffectedly as before, addressing the elder:

<!-- B02-C05-P017 -->
“The whole idea of my article is that in ancient times, during its first three centuries, Christianity appeared on earth only as a church and was only a church. But when the pagan Roman state desired to become Christian, what inevitably happened was that, in becoming Christian, it merely incorporated the church into itself, while in a great many of its functions it continued to be a pagan state just as before. In essence, there is no doubt that this was bound to happen. But Rome, as a state, retained far too much of pagan civilization and wisdom—for example, the very aims and foundations of the state itself. The church of Christ, meanwhile, on entering the state, could of course surrender nothing of its own foundations, of the rock on which it stood, and could only pursue its own aims, once firmly established and laid down for it by the Lord himself: among them, to turn the whole world, and therefore the whole ancient pagan state as well, into the church. Thus—that is, with the future in view—it is not the church that must seek a definite place for itself within the state, like ‘any social association’ or ‘an association of people for religious purposes,’ as the author I am answering calls the church; on the contrary, every earthly state should in time be transformed entirely into the church and become nothing other than the church alone, having abandoned any of its aims that differ from those of the church. None of this will demean it or rob it of its honor or its glory as a great state, or rob its rulers of their glory; it will merely take it off a false road, still pagan and mistaken, and set it on the right and true road, the only one that leads to eternal ends. That is why the author of the book on ‘The Foundations of Ecclesiastical Courts’ would be thinking correctly if, in seeking and proposing these foundations, he regarded them as a temporary compromise, still necessary in our sinful and unfinished age, but nothing more. Yet the moment the author of these foundations ventures to declare that the principles he now proposes, some of which Father Joseph has just listed, are immutable, inherent, and everlasting, he sets himself directly against the church and its holy, everlasting, and immutable destiny. That is my whole article, a complete outline of it.”

<!-- B02-C05-P018 -->
“In a few words, then,” Father Paisius spoke again, pressing home each word, “according to certain theories that have become all too clear in our nineteenth century, the church must transform itself into the state, as though passing from a lower to a higher form, in order then to disappear within it, yielding to science, the spirit of the age, and civilization. If it refuses and resists, it is allotted a mere corner of the state in consequence, and even there it is kept under supervision—and this is the case everywhere in the modern European lands of our time. But according to the Russian understanding and hope, the church must not transform itself into the state, as though passing from a lower to a higher type; on the contrary, the state must in the end be found worthy to become the church alone, and nothing else whatever. So be it, so be it!”

<!-- B02-C05-P019 -->
“Well, I confess you've reassured me a little now,” Miusov said with a mocking smile, crossing his legs again. “As far as I understand, then, this means the realization of some infinitely distant ideal, at the Second Coming. As you please. A beautiful utopian dream of the disappearance of wars, diplomats, banks, and so forth. Something rather like socialism, even. And here I was thinking you meant it seriously, that the church would now, for example, try criminal cases and sentence people to flogging and penal servitude, or perhaps even death.”

<!-- B02-C05-P020 -->
“But even if church courts were the only courts for the whole community even now, even now the church would not sentence people to penal servitude or death. Crime and the way it is regarded would undoubtedly have to change then—gradually, of course, not all at once or this very moment, but still, fairly soon…” Ivan Fyodorovich said calmly, without blinking.

<!-- B02-C05-P021 -->
“Are you serious?” Miusov stared at him.

<!-- B02-C05-P022 -->
“If everything became the church, the church would excommunicate the criminal and the disobedient, rather than cut off their heads,” Ivan Fyodorovich continued. “I ask you, where would the excommunicated man go? He would then have to leave not only other people, as he does now, but Christ as well. By his crime he would have rebelled not only against people but against the church of Christ. Strictly speaking, that is of course true now as well, but it has still not been declared, and the conscience of today's criminal very, very often makes bargains with itself: ‘I stole, yes, but I'm not attacking the church, I'm no enemy of Christ’—that is what today's criminal tells himself at every turn. But then, when the church took the place of the state, it would be hard for him to say that, unless he repudiated the whole church throughout the earth: ‘They are all mistaken, they've all gone astray, they are all a false church, and I alone, murderer and thief, am the true Christian church.’ That is very hard to tell yourself; it would take extraordinary circumstances, circumstances that rarely arise. Now, on the other hand, consider the church's own view of crime: wouldn't it have to change from its present, almost pagan view, and be transformed from the mechanical cutting off of an infected member, as is done now to protect society, into the idea—fully and truly—of a person's rebirth, resurrection, and salvation?…”

<!-- B02-C05-P023 -->
“What exactly is all this supposed to mean? I'm losing you again,” Miusov interrupted. “Another dream of some kind. Something shapeless, impossible to understand. How can there be excommunication—what sort of excommunication? I suspect you're simply amusing yourself, Ivan Fyodorovich.”

<!-- B02-C05-P024 -->
“But in truth it is just the same even now,” the elder suddenly began, and everyone turned to him at once. “For if there were no church of Christ now, there would be nothing to restrain a criminal in his evil deeds, nor any punishment for them afterward—I mean real punishment, not mechanical punishment, as they were just calling it, which in most cases only embitters the heart, but real punishment, the only effective punishment, the only punishment that brings fear and peace, and that lies in the awareness of one's own conscience.”

<!-- B02-C05-P025 -->
“How so, may I ask?” Miusov said with the liveliest curiosity.

<!-- B02-C05-P026 -->
“In this way,” the elder began. “All these sentences of exile and forced labor, and formerly with beatings too, reform no one, and above all they frighten scarcely a single criminal, and the number of crimes, far from falling, keeps on growing. Surely you must agree with that. And so society is not protected at all in this way, for though the harmful member is mechanically cut off and sent far away, out of sight, another criminal immediately appears in his place, or perhaps two more. If anything protects society even in our time, and even reforms the criminal himself and makes him a different person, it is again solely the law of Christ, making itself felt in the awareness of one's own conscience. Only by acknowledging his guilt as a son of Christ's community, that is, of the church, does he also acknowledge his guilt before that community itself, that is, before the church. Thus it is only before the church that the modern criminal is capable of acknowledging his guilt, and certainly not before the state. Now, if judgment belonged to society as the church, it would know whom to bring back from excommunication and admit once more into its communion. At present, though, the church has no power to enforce its judgments, only the possibility of moral condemnation, and so it too withdraws from actively punishing the criminal. It does not excommunicate him, but continues its fatherly admonition. More than that, it even tries to preserve its entire Christian communion with the criminal: it admits him to church services and the Eucharistic gifts, gives him alms, and treats him more as a captive than as a guilty man. And what would become of the criminal, O Lord, if Christian society too—that is, the church—rejected him as the civil law rejects him and cuts him off? What would happen if the church too punished him with excommunication immediately and every time the law of the state punished him? There could be no greater despair, at least for the Russian criminal, for Russian criminals still believe. And yet, who knows: perhaps something terrible would happen then—perhaps faith would be lost in the criminal's despairing heart, and what then? But the church, like a tender and loving mother, withdraws of its own accord from active punishment, because even without its punishment the guilty man has been punished all too painfully by the state court, and surely someone must take pity on him. Above all, it withdraws because the judgment of the church is the only judgment that contains the truth, and consequently cannot, in its essence or morally, be combined with any other judgment, even in a temporary compromise. Here no bargains can be made. A foreign criminal, they say, rarely repents, for even modern doctrines confirm him in the idea that his crime is not a crime at all, but only a rebellion against an unjustly oppressive power. Society cuts him off from itself quite mechanically, by the force that triumphs over him, and accompanies this exclusion with hatred—at least that is what they themselves tell us about themselves in Europe—with hatred and utter indifference to his further fate as a brother, forgetting him altogether. Thus everything happens without the slightest compassion from the church, for in many cases there are no churches there at all anymore, only churchmen and magnificent church buildings, while the churches themselves have long been striving to pass from a lower form, as the church, to a higher form, as the state, in order to disappear into it completely. So it seems, at least in the Lutheran lands. In Rome, the state has been proclaimed in place of the church for a thousand years now. And so the criminal himself no longer feels that he is a member of the church, and, cut off, remains in despair. If he returns to society, it is often with such hatred that he himself seems to excommunicate society from himself. You can judge for yourselves how that will end. In many cases it would seem to be the same with us; but the point is that, besides the established courts, we also have the church, which never loses communion with the criminal as its beloved and still precious son, and beyond that the judgment of the church exists and is preserved, if only in people's minds: though it is not enforced now, it still lives for the future, if only in a dream, and the criminal himself undoubtedly acknowledges it by the instinct of his soul. What was said here just now is also true: if the judgment of the church really came to pass in all its power—that is, if the whole of society became the church alone—then not only would the judgment of the church act to reform the criminal as it never does today, but perhaps crime itself really would fall to an unbelievably small fraction of what it is. And the church would undoubtedly understand the future criminal and future crime quite differently in many cases than it does now, and would know how to bring back the excommunicated, forestall the man plotting a crime, and restore the fallen to life. It is true,” the elder said with a smile, “that Christian society is itself not ready yet and rests on only seven righteous people; but since they never fail, it still stands unshaken, awaiting its complete transfiguration from a society that is still an almost pagan association into the one universal and sovereign church. So be it, so be it, though it come only at the end of the ages, for this alone is destined to be fulfilled! And there is no need to trouble ourselves over times and seasons, for the mystery of times and seasons lies in God's wisdom, in his foreknowledge, and in his love. And what by human reckoning may still be very far away may, by God's decree, already be on the eve of its appearance, at the doors. And may that last possibility come to pass—so be it, so be it.”

<!-- B02-C05-P027 -->
“So be it! So be it!” Father Paisius affirmed, reverently and sternly.

<!-- B02-C05-P028 -->
“Strange, exceedingly strange!” Miusov said, not so much heatedly as with a kind of suppressed indignation.

<!-- B02-C05-P029 -->
“What seems so strange to you?” Father Joseph inquired cautiously.

<!-- B02-C05-P030 -->
“What on earth is all this?” Miusov cried, as though suddenly bursting free. “The state is abolished on earth, and the church is raised to the rank of the state! This isn't merely ultramontanism, it's arch-ultramontanism! Pope Gregory the Seventh never dreamed of such a thing!”

<!-- B02-C05-P031 -->
“You insist on understanding it exactly backward!” Father Paisius said sternly. “The church does not turn into the state—understand that. That is Rome and its dream. That is the devil's third temptation![^b02-c05-n002] On the contrary, the state turns into the church, rises to the church, and becomes the church throughout the earth, which is the complete opposite of ultramontanism, of Rome, and of your interpretation, and is nothing other than the great destiny of Orthodoxy on earth. From the East this star shall shine.”

<!-- B02-C05-P032 -->
Miusov maintained an imposing silence. His whole figure expressed extraordinary self-respect. A lofty, condescending smile appeared on his lips. Alyosha followed everything with a pounding heart. The whole conversation had stirred him to his depths. He happened to glance at Rakitin, who was standing motionless in his old place by the door, listening and watching intently, although his eyes were lowered. But from the bright flush on his cheeks Alyosha guessed that Rakitin too seemed no less stirred than he was; Alyosha knew what had stirred him.

<!-- B02-C05-P033 -->
“Allow me to tell you a little anecdote, gentlemen,” Miusov suddenly said in an imposing tone, with a particularly stately air. “In Paris, several years ago now, soon after the December coup, I happened one day, while paying a social visit to a very, very important person who was then in authority, to meet a most interesting gentleman at his house. This individual was not exactly a detective, but something like the head of a whole squad of political detectives—a fairly influential position in its way. Seizing the opportunity, I struck up a conversation with him out of extreme curiosity; and since he had been received not as an acquaintance but as a subordinate official who had come to make a report of a certain kind, he, for his part, seeing how his superior received me, favored me with a measure of frankness—within certain limits, of course; that is, he was polite rather than frank, precisely as the French know how to be polite, all the more so since he saw that I was a foreigner. But I understood him very well. We were speaking of revolutionary socialists, who were among those being persecuted at the time. I'll leave out the main substance of our conversation and give you just one most interesting remark that suddenly escaped this gentleman: ‘We,’ he said, ‘aren't really so very afraid of all these socialists—anarchists, atheists, and revolutionaries; we watch them, and we know their moves. But among them there are a few unusual people, though not many: they believe in God and are Christians, and at the same time they're socialists. Those are the ones we fear most of all; they're dangerous people! A Christian socialist is more dangerous than an atheist socialist.’ Those words struck me even then, but here with you, gentlemen, they've somehow suddenly come back to me…”

<!-- B02-C05-P034 -->
“In other words, you apply those words to us and see us as socialists?” Father Paisius asked directly, without beating around the bush. But before Pyotr Alexandrovich could think of an answer, the door opened and Dmitri Fyodorovich, so very late, came in. They really did seem to have stopped expecting him, and his sudden appearance even caused a certain surprise at first.

[^b02-c05-n001]: *Ultramontanism* defended papal authority over church affairs and its reach into secular government. The word literally suggests “beyond the mountains”; Father Joseph turns Miusov’s charge into a joke about the absence of mountains here.

[^b02-c05-n002]: In Matthew 4:8–10, the devil offers Jesus the kingdoms of the world in return for worship. Father Paisius invokes this temptation when he accuses Rome of seeking worldly rule.
