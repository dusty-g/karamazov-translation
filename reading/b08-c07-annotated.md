# Book VIII: Mitya
## VII. The Former and Undisputed One

<!-- B08-C07-P001 -->
Mitya came up to the table in quick, long strides.

<!-- B08-C07-P002 -->
“Gentlemen,” he began loudly, almost shouting, but stumbling over every word, “I... I mean no harm! Don't be afraid,” he exclaimed. “I mean no harm, no harm,” he suddenly turned to Grushenka, who had leaned sideways in her armchair toward Kalganov and gripped his arm. “I... I'm traveling too. I'm here till morning. Gentlemen, may a traveler passing through... stay with you till morning? Only till morning, one last time, in this very room?”

<!-- B08-C07-P003 -->
He finished this by addressing the plump little man sitting on the sofa with a pipe. The man solemnly removed the pipe from his lips and said sternly:

<!-- B08-C07-P004 -->
“Pan, this is a private gathering. There are other rooms.”

<!-- B08-C07-P005 -->
“Why, it's you, Dmitri Fyodorovich! What's the matter with you?” Kalganov suddenly spoke up. “Do sit down with us. How do you do!”

<!-- B08-C07-P006 -->
“How do you do, my dear fellow... my priceless fellow! I've always respected you...” Mitya answered joyfully and impetuously, at once holding out his hand across the table.

<!-- B08-C07-P007 -->
“Ow, what a grip! You've nearly broken my fingers,” Kalganov laughed.

<!-- B08-C07-P008 -->
“That's how he always shakes hands, always!” Grushenka answered cheerfully, still smiling rather timidly. Mitya's appearance seemed suddenly to have convinced her that he would make no trouble, and she studied him with intense curiosity and lingering uneasiness. Something about him had struck her very forcibly; certainly she had never expected him to come in and speak like this at such a moment.

<!-- B08-C07-P009 -->
“How do you do, sir,” the landowner Maximov chimed in sweetly on the left. Mitya rushed to him too.

<!-- B08-C07-P010 -->
“How do you do! You're here too—how glad I am you're here! Gentlemen, gentlemen, I...” Again he turned to the gentleman with the pipe, evidently taking him for the most important person there. “I flew here... I wanted to spend my last day and my last hour in this room, this very room... where I too worshipped... my queen! Forgive me, pan!” he cried frantically. “I flew here and swore an oath... Oh, don't be afraid, it's my last night! Let's drink to peace, pan! They'll bring wine in a moment... I brought this...” For some reason he suddenly pulled out his bundle of banknotes. “Permit me, pan! I want music, thunder, uproar, everything we had before... But a worm, a needless worm will crawl over the earth and be gone! I'll commemorate my day of joy on my last night!”

<!-- B08-C07-P011 -->
He almost choked. He wanted to say so much, so very much, but only these strange exclamations came out. The gentleman stared at him, at his bundle of banknotes, looked at Grushenka, and was visibly bewildered.

<!-- B08-C07-P012 -->
“If my królowa permits...” he began.

<!-- B08-C07-P013 -->
“What's this królowa—is that a queen?” Grushenka suddenly interrupted. “The way you all talk makes me laugh. Sit down, Mitya. What are you saying? Please don't frighten me. You won't frighten me, will you? If you won't, then I'm glad to see you...”

<!-- B08-C07-P014 -->
“Me, frighten you?” Mitya suddenly cried, throwing up his arms. “Oh, go past me, go on by, I won't stand in your way!” And suddenly, quite unexpectedly for everyone, and certainly for himself, he flung himself onto a chair and burst into tears, turning his head toward the opposite wall and clutching the back of the chair in both arms, as though embracing it.

<!-- B08-C07-P015 -->
“There, there, just look at you!” Grushenka exclaimed reproachfully. “That's just how he used to come to see me: he'd suddenly start talking, and I couldn't understand a thing. Once he cried just like this, and now here's a second time. For shame! What are you crying for? If only you had something to cry about!” she suddenly added enigmatically, stressing those words with a kind of irritation.

<!-- B08-C07-P016 -->
“I... I'm not crying... Well, how do you do!” In an instant he swung around on his chair and suddenly laughed, but not his abrupt, wooden laugh: it was a kind of soundless, prolonged, nervous, shaking laugh.

<!-- B08-C07-P017 -->
“There you go again... Now, cheer up, cheer up!” Grushenka coaxed him. “I'm very glad you've come, very glad, Mitya. Do you hear? I'm very glad! I want him to sit here with us,” she announced imperiously, seemingly to everyone, although her words plainly concerned the man on the sofa. “I want him to, I do! And if he goes, I'll go too, so there!” she added, her eyes suddenly blazing.

<!-- B08-C07-P018 -->
“My queen's wish is law!” the gentleman pronounced, gallantly kissing Grushenka's hand. “I invite the pan to join our company!” he said courteously to Mitya. Mitya half sprang up again, plainly intending to launch into another speech, but something else came out.

<!-- B08-C07-P019 -->
“Let's drink, pan!” he cut himself short instead. Everyone laughed.

<!-- B08-C07-P020 -->
“Good Lord! I thought he was going to make another speech,” Grushenka exclaimed nervously. “Listen, Mitya,” she added insistently, “don't jump up again. It's splendid that you've brought champagne. I'll drink some myself. I can't stand fruit liqueur. Best of all, you've come yourself—it was so dull... Have you come for another spree, then? Put your money away! Where did you get so much?”

<!-- B08-C07-P021 -->
Mitya, who was still clutching the banknotes crumpled in his hand, much observed by everyone, especially the Poles, hurriedly and awkwardly stuffed them into his pocket. He blushed. At that very moment the innkeeper brought an opened bottle of champagne and glasses on a tray. Mitya seized the bottle, but was so flustered he forgot what to do with it. Kalganov took it from him and poured the wine for him.

<!-- B08-C07-P022 -->
“Another bottle, another!” Mitya shouted to the innkeeper, and, forgetting to clink glasses with the gentleman whom he had so solemnly invited to drink to peace with him, suddenly emptied his whole glass alone, without waiting for anyone. His entire face changed at once. In place of the solemn, tragic expression with which he had entered, something almost infantile appeared. He suddenly seemed utterly meek and humble. He looked at everyone timidly and joyfully, giggling often and nervously, with the grateful look of a guilty little dog that has been petted and let back inside. He seemed to have forgotten everything and looked around at them all in delight, smiling like a child. He kept looking at Grushenka and laughing, and moved his chair right up against her armchair. Little by little he made out the two Polish gentlemen too, although he had as yet scarcely grasped what they were. The one on the sofa impressed him with his bearing, his Polish accent, and above all his pipe. “Well, what of it? It's a good thing he smokes a pipe,” Mitya reflected as he gazed at him. The gentleman's somewhat bloated face, nearly forty years old, with its very small nose and the two extremely thin, pointed, waxed and impudent mustaches beneath it, likewise raised not the slightest question in Mitya for the moment. Even the gentleman's very shabby little Siberian-made wig, with the hair at the temples brushed forward in a most absurd fashion, did not particularly strike Mitya. “Well, that must be how a wig is worn,” he continued his blissful contemplation. The other gentleman, seated by the wall, younger than the gentleman on the sofa, who eyed the whole company brazenly and defiantly and listened to the general conversation with silent contempt, impressed Mitya only by his great height, frightfully out of proportion to the man on the sofa. “He must be six foot three when he stands up,” flashed through Mitya's head. It also struck him that this tall gentleman was probably the sofa gentleman's friend and henchman, a sort of “bodyguard,” and that the little gentleman with the pipe certainly commanded the tall one. But all this too seemed splendid and indisputable to Mitya. All rivalry had died in the little dog. He still understood nothing about Grushenka or the enigmatic tone of several things she had said; he understood only, with his whole heart trembling, that she was kind to him, that she had “forgiven” him and seated him beside her. He was beside himself with delight when he saw her take a sip of wine from her glass. The company's silence suddenly seemed to strike him, however, and he began looking around at them all with expectant eyes. “Why are we just sitting here? Why don't you start something, gentlemen?” his grinning gaze seemed to say.

<!-- B08-C07-P023 -->
“He keeps telling lies, you see, and we've been laughing at him,” Kalganov suddenly began, pointing to Maximov, as if he had guessed Mitya's thought.

<!-- B08-C07-P024 -->
Mitya stared eagerly at Kalganov, then at once at Maximov.

<!-- B08-C07-P025 -->
“Lies?” He gave his short, wooden laugh, immediately delighted by something. “Ha, ha!”

<!-- B08-C07-P026 -->
“Yes. Imagine, he claims that all our cavalry married Polish women in the twenties. But that's utter nonsense, isn't it?”

<!-- B08-C07-P027 -->
“Polish women?” Mitya echoed again, now positively enchanted.

<!-- B08-C07-P028 -->
Kalganov understood Mitya's relationship with Grushenka very well, and had his suspicions about the Polish gentleman too; but none of that interested him very much, perhaps did not interest him at all. What interested him most was Maximov. He had come here with Maximov by chance and met the two Poles at the inn for the first time in his life. He had known Grushenka before and had even visited her once with someone; she had not liked him then. But here she looked at him very affectionately; before Mitya's arrival she had even caressed him, but somehow he remained unmoved. He was a young man of no more than twenty, elegantly dressed, with a very pretty, fair little face and beautiful thick light-brown hair. But in that fair little face were lovely pale-blue eyes with an intelligent, at times even a deep expression, beyond his years, although the young man sometimes spoke and looked exactly like a child, without the slightest embarrassment, even when he knew he was doing it. In general he was highly individual, even capricious, although always gentle. At times something fixed and stubborn flashed into his face: he would look at you and listen, while seeming to dream obstinately of something of his own. Sometimes he became listless and lazy; then suddenly he would grow excited, apparently, at times, for the most trifling reason.

<!-- B08-C07-P029 -->
“Imagine, I've been taking him around with me for four days now,” he continued, drawing out his words a little lazily, as it were, but without the least affectation, quite naturally. “You remember when your brother pushed him out of the carriage and he went flying? Since then. He interested me very much by that, and I took him to the country, but now he keeps telling lies, so it's embarrassing to be with him. I'm taking him back...”

<!-- B08-C07-P030 -->
“The pan has never seen a Polish lady and says it couldn't be,” the gentleman with the pipe remarked to Maximov.

<!-- B08-C07-P031 -->
The gentleman with the pipe spoke Russian tolerably well, at least much better than he pretended. When he used Russian words, he twisted them into a Polish shape.

<!-- B08-C07-P032 -->
“But I was married to a Polish lady myself, sir,” Maximov giggled back.

<!-- B08-C07-P033 -->
“Did you serve in the cavalry, then? You were talking about the cavalry. Were you a cavalryman?” Kalganov immediately joined in.

<!-- B08-C07-P034 -->
“Yes, of course, was he a cavalryman? Ha, ha!” cried Mitya, listening avidly and turning his questioning gaze swiftly toward each new speaker, as though expecting heaven knew what revelations from every one of them.

<!-- B08-C07-P035 -->
“No, sir, you see, sir,” Maximov turned to him. “I mean, those young ladies there... pretty little things, sir... when one dances a mazurka with one of our uhlans... once she's danced the mazurka with him, she jumps straight onto his lap like a little cat, sir... a white one, sir... and pan papa and pani mama see and allow it... allow it, sir... and next morning the uhlan goes and offers his hand... there you are, sir... offers his hand, hee, hee!” Maximov finished with a giggle.

<!-- B08-C07-P036 -->
“The pan is a scoundrel!” the tall gentleman on the chair suddenly growled and crossed one leg over the other. All Mitya noticed was his huge greased boot with its thick, dirty sole. Indeed, both gentlemen were rather shabbily and greasily dressed.

<!-- B08-C07-P037 -->
“There, now, a scoundrel! Why is he swearing at him?” Grushenka suddenly flared up.

<!-- B08-C07-P038 -->
“Pani Agrippina, the pan saw peasant girls in Poland, not ladies of noble birth,” the gentleman with the pipe remarked to Grushenka.

<!-- B08-C07-P039 -->
“You may count on that!” the tall gentleman on the chair snapped contemptuously.

<!-- B08-C07-P040 -->
“Oh, really! Let him speak! People are talking—why interrupt them? They're amusing,” Grushenka retorted.

<!-- B08-C07-P041 -->
“I am not interrupting, pani,” the gentleman in the wig remarked pointedly, looking at Grushenka for a long time. Then, falling into a dignified silence, he began sucking his pipe again.

<!-- B08-C07-P042 -->
“No, no, the gentleman was right just now,” Kalganov grew excited again, as if something momentous were at stake. “He's never been to Poland, so how can he talk about Poland? You weren't married in Poland, were you? Were you?”

<!-- B08-C07-P043 -->
“No, sir, in Smolensk province, sir. Only an uhlan had brought her away beforehand, sir—my wife, sir, my future wife—with pani mama and her auntie and another relative with a grown-up son, all the way from Poland itself, from Poland itself... and he passed her on to me. He was a lieutenant of ours, a very fine young man. At first he meant to marry her himself, but he didn't, because she turned out to be lame...”

<!-- B08-C07-P044 -->
“So you married a lame woman?” Kalganov exclaimed.

<!-- B08-C07-P045 -->
“A lame one, sir. The two of them deceived me a little then and kept it quiet. I thought she was hopping about... she kept hopping about, and I thought it was from high spirits...”

<!-- B08-C07-P046 -->
“For joy at marrying you?” Kalganov shouted in a ringing, almost childish voice.

<!-- B08-C07-P047 -->
“Yes, sir, for joy. But it turned out to be for quite another reason, sir. Later, when we were married, that same evening after the wedding she confessed and apologized very feelingly. She'd jumped over a puddle once when she was young, she said, and injured her little leg that way, hee, hee!”

<!-- B08-C07-P048 -->
Kalganov burst into the most childish laughter and almost fell over on the sofa. Grushenka laughed too. Mitya was at the very summit of happiness.

<!-- B08-C07-P049 -->
“You know, you know, this time he's really telling the truth, this time he isn't lying!” Kalganov exclaimed to Mitya. “And you know, he's been married twice—he's talking about his first wife—and his second wife, you know, ran away and is still alive. Did you know that?”

<!-- B08-C07-P050 -->
“Really?” Mitya turned quickly to Maximov with an expression of extraordinary astonishment.

<!-- B08-C07-P051 -->
“Yes, sir, she ran away. I had that misfortune,” Maximov modestly confirmed. “With a certain monsieur, sir. And the main thing is, first of all she had my whole little estate signed over to her alone. You're an educated man, she said, you can find your own crust of bread. That's how she left me. A respected bishop once remarked to me: one of your wives was lame, but the other was altogether too fleet of foot, hee, hee!”

<!-- B08-C07-P052 -->
“Listen, listen!” Kalganov was fairly bubbling over. “Even if he lies—and he often does—he lies solely to give everyone pleasure. That isn't base, is it, isn't base? You know, sometimes I love him. He's very base, but he's naturally base, eh? What do you think? Other people behave basely for something, for some advantage, but with him it's just natural... Imagine, for example, he claims—he argued all the way yesterday—that Gogol wrote about him in *Dead Souls*. Remember the landowner Maximov whom Nozdryov flogged and was put on trial for it: ‘for inflicting personal injury upon the landowner Maximov with birch rods while in a drunken state’—you remember? Well, imagine, he claims he was the one, that it was him they flogged! Could that be possible? Chichikov was traveling in the twenties at the latest, the early twenties, so the dates don't fit at all. They couldn't have flogged him then. Could they? Could they?”

<!-- B08-C07-P053 -->
It was hard to imagine why Kalganov was so worked up, but his excitement was sincere. Mitya threw himself wholeheartedly into his concerns.

<!-- B08-C07-P054 -->
“But what if they did flog him!” he cried, roaring with laughter.

<!-- B08-C07-P055 -->
“It wasn't exactly a flogging, sir, just a little something,” Maximov suddenly put in.

<!-- B08-C07-P056 -->
“What do you mean, a little something? Either they flogged you or they didn't.”

<!-- B08-C07-P057 -->
“Która godzina, panie?—what time is it?” the gentleman with the pipe asked the tall gentleman on the chair, looking bored. The other shrugged: neither of them had a watch.

<!-- B08-C07-P058 -->
“Why shouldn't we talk? Let other people talk too. Just because you're bored, nobody else is to say a word,” Grushenka flared up again, plainly picking a quarrel on purpose. Something seemed to flash into Mitya's mind for the first time. This time the gentleman answered with visible irritation:

<!-- B08-C07-P059 -->
“Pani, I say nothing against it, I have said nothing.”

<!-- B08-C07-P060 -->
“Well, all right, and you go on telling us,” Grushenka called to Maximov. “Why have you all gone quiet?”

<!-- B08-C07-P061 -->
“There's really nothing to tell, sir, because it's all nonsense,” Maximov at once took up the tale with evident pleasure and a touch of coyness. “And in Gogol it's all allegorical, because all the names he put in were allegorical. Nozdryov wasn't Nozdryov at all, but Nosov, and Kuvshinnikov—that's nothing like it, because he was Shkvornev. Fenardi really was Fenardi, only he wasn't Italian but Russian, Petrov, sir; and Mademoiselle Fenardi was pretty, sir, with pretty little legs in tights, sir, a short little skirt all covered in spangles, and she spun around, only not for four hours, just four little minutes, sir... and she enchanted everyone...”

<!-- B08-C07-P062 -->
“But what did they flog you for? What did they flog you for?” Kalganov shouted.

<!-- B08-C07-P063 -->
“For Piron, sir,” Maximov answered.

<!-- B08-C07-P064 -->
“What Piron?” Mitya cried.

<!-- B08-C07-P065 -->
“The famous French writer, Piron, sir. We were all drinking wine in a large gathering at a tavern, at that very fair. They'd invited me along, and first of all I began reciting epigrams: ‘Is that you, Boileau? What a funny outfit!’ And Boileau answers that he's going to a masquerade—that is, the bathhouse, sir, hee, hee! They took it personally. So I quickly recited another, well known to all educated people, a biting one, sir:

> You're Sappho, I'm Phaon; I won't dispute that claim,  
> But sadly, all the same,  
> You do not know the way to the sea.

“They were more offended still and began abusing me quite indecently for it, and just then, unfortunately for me, to put things right, I told a very learned anecdote about Piron: how they wouldn't admit him to the French Academy, and to get his revenge he wrote his own epitaph for his tombstone:

> Ci-gît Piron qui ne fut rien  
> Pas même académicien.[^b08-c07-n001]

“And they went and flogged me.”

<!-- B08-C07-P066 -->
“But what for, what for?”

<!-- B08-C07-P067 -->
“For my education. There are any number of things people might flog a man for,” Maximov concluded mildly and instructively.

<!-- B08-C07-P068 -->
“Oh, enough, it's all nasty, I don't want to listen. I thought it would be amusing,” Grushenka suddenly interrupted. Mitya started and immediately stopped laughing. The tall gentleman rose from his seat and, with the haughty air of a man bored by company beneath him, began pacing back and forth across the room, his hands behind his back.

<!-- B08-C07-P069 -->
“Look at him striding about!” Grushenka eyed him contemptuously. Mitya grew uneasy; besides, he noticed that the gentleman on the sofa was looking at him irritably.

<!-- B08-C07-P070 -->
“Pan,” Mitya cried, “let's drink, pan! And the other pan too: let's drink, panowie!” In a flash he brought three glasses together and filled them with champagne.

<!-- B08-C07-P071 -->
“To Poland, panowie! I drink to your Poland, to the Polish land!” Mitya exclaimed.

<!-- B08-C07-P072 -->
“Bardzo mi to miło, panie, wypijem—that pleases me very much, pan, let's drink,” said the gentleman on the sofa with solemn graciousness, and took his glass.

<!-- B08-C07-P073 -->
“And the other gentleman, what's his name—hey, your illustrious lordship, take a glass!” Mitya bustled about.

<!-- B08-C07-P074 -->
“Pan Wróblewski,” supplied the gentleman on the sofa.

<!-- B08-C07-P075 -->
Pan Wróblewski swayed over to the table and took his glass standing up.

<!-- B08-C07-P076 -->
“To Poland, panowie, hurrah!” Mitya shouted, raising his glass.

<!-- B08-C07-P077 -->
All three drank. Mitya seized the bottle and at once refilled the three glasses.

<!-- B08-C07-P078 -->
“Now to Russia, panowie, and let's be brothers!”

<!-- B08-C07-P079 -->
“Pour us some too,” Grushenka said. “I want to drink to Russia.”

<!-- B08-C07-P080 -->
“So do I,” said Kalganov.

<!-- B08-C07-P081 -->
“And I'd like some too, sir... to dear little Russia, our dear old granny,” Maximov tittered.

<!-- B08-C07-P082 -->
“Everyone, everyone!” Mitya exclaimed. “Landlord, more bottles!”

<!-- B08-C07-P083 -->
They brought all three bottles remaining of those Mitya had brought. Mitya poured.

<!-- B08-C07-P084 -->
“To Russia, hurrah!” he proclaimed again. Everyone except the Poles drank, and Grushenka emptied her whole glass at once. The Polish gentlemen did not even touch theirs.

<!-- B08-C07-P085 -->
“What about you, panowie?” Mitya exclaimed. “So that's how you are?”

<!-- B08-C07-P086 -->
Pan Wróblewski took his glass, raised it, and said in a booming voice:

<!-- B08-C07-P087 -->
“To Russia within her borders of before the year seventeen seventy-two!”[^b08-c07-n002]

<!-- B08-C07-P088 -->
“Oto bardzo pięknie!—now that's good!” the other gentleman cried, and both drained their glasses together.

<!-- B08-C07-P089 -->
“You're a pair of fools, panowie!” suddenly escaped Mitya.

<!-- B08-C07-P090 -->
“Pa-an!!” the two gentlemen shouted threateningly, squaring up to Mitya like roosters. Pan Wróblewski was especially incensed.

<!-- B08-C07-P091 -->
“How can a man help loving his own country?” he cried.

<!-- B08-C07-P092 -->
“Silence! No quarreling! There will be no quarrels!” Grushenka cried imperiously, stamping her foot. Her face flamed, her eyes flashed. The glass she had just drunk was taking effect. Mitya was terribly frightened.

<!-- B08-C07-P093 -->
“Forgive me, panowie! It's my fault, I won't do it again. Wróblewski, Pan Wróblewski, I won't do it again!”

<!-- B08-C07-P094 -->
“You at least keep quiet! Sit down, you fool!” Grushenka snapped at him in angry exasperation.

<!-- B08-C07-P095 -->
Everyone sat down, everyone fell silent, everyone looked at one another.

<!-- B08-C07-P096 -->
“Gentlemen, it's all my fault!” Mitya began again, having understood nothing of Grushenka's exclamation. “Why are we just sitting here? What shall we do... to have some fun, to have fun again?”

<!-- B08-C07-P097 -->
“Oh, this really is terribly dull,” Kalganov drawled lazily.

<!-- B08-C07-P098 -->
“We might play a little bank, sir, like earlier...” Maximov suddenly giggled.

<!-- B08-C07-P099 -->
“Bank? Splendid!” Mitya took it up. “If only the panowie...”

<!-- B08-C07-P100 -->
“Późno, panie!” the gentleman on the sofa answered, seemingly unwillingly.

<!-- B08-C07-P101 -->
“That's true,” Pan Wróblewski agreed.

<!-- B08-C07-P102 -->
“Późno? What's this późno?” Grushenka asked.

<!-- B08-C07-P103 -->
“It means late, pani, late, the hour is late,” the gentleman on the sofa explained.

<!-- B08-C07-P104 -->
“Everything's too late for them, everything's impossible!” Grushenka almost shrieked in annoyance. “They're dull themselves, so everyone else must be dull too. Before you came, Mitya, they sat in silence just like this, putting on airs with me...”

<!-- B08-C07-P105 -->
“My goddess!” cried the gentleman on the sofa. “What you say shall be done. I see your displeasure, and that makes me sad. I am ready, pan,” he concluded, turning to Mitya.

<!-- B08-C07-P106 -->
“Begin, pan!” Mitya exclaimed, snatching his banknotes from his pocket and laying two hundred-ruble notes on the table.

<!-- B08-C07-P107 -->
“I want to lose a lot to you, pan. Take the cards, set up the bank!”

<!-- B08-C07-P108 -->
“The cards must come from the landlord, pan,” the little gentleman said seriously and insistently.

<!-- B08-C07-P109 -->
“That's the best way,” Pan Wróblewski agreed.

<!-- B08-C07-P110 -->
“From the landlord? Very well, I understand. Let them come from the landlord. That's good of you, panowie! Cards!” Mitya ordered the innkeeper.

<!-- B08-C07-P111 -->
The innkeeper brought an unopened pack of cards and informed Mitya that the girls were gathering, the little Jews with their dulcimers would probably be there soon too, but the troika with the provisions had not yet arrived. Mitya leaped up from the table and ran into the next room to make arrangements at once. But only three girls had arrived, and Marya was not there yet either. He himself did not know how to arrange things or why he had run out: he merely ordered treats, hard candies and toffees, to be taken from the box and distributed to the girls. “And vodka for Andrei, vodka for Andrei!” he commanded hastily. “I've hurt Andrei!” Just then Maximov, who had hurried after him, touched his shoulder.

<!-- B08-C07-P112 -->
“Give me five rubles,” he whispered to Mitya. “I'd risk a little at faro too, hee, hee!”

<!-- B08-C07-P113 -->
“Excellent, splendid! Take ten, here!” Once more he pulled all his banknotes from his pocket and found ten rubles. “And if you lose, come back for more, come back for more...”

<!-- B08-C07-P114 -->
“Very good, sir,” Maximov whispered joyfully and ran into the main room. Mitya followed at once, apologizing for keeping them waiting. The Poles had already sat down and opened the pack. They looked much friendlier, almost affectionate. The gentleman on the sofa had lit a fresh pipe and was preparing to deal; his face had even taken on a certain solemnity.

<!-- B08-C07-P115 -->
“To your places, panowie!” Pan Wróblewski proclaimed.

<!-- B08-C07-P116 -->
“No, I'm not playing any more,” Kalganov said. “I already lost fifty rubles to them earlier.”

<!-- B08-C07-P117 -->
“The pan was unlucky; the pan may be lucky again,” the gentleman on the sofa remarked in his direction.

<!-- B08-C07-P118 -->
“How much in the bank? Will you cover whatever I stake?” Mitya asked excitedly.

<!-- B08-C07-P119 -->
“At your service, pan, perhaps a hundred, perhaps two hundred—whatever you stake.”

<!-- B08-C07-P120 -->
“A million!” Mitya roared with laughter.

<!-- B08-C07-P121 -->
“Has the pan captain perhaps heard of Pan Podvysotsky?”

<!-- B08-C07-P122 -->
“What Podvysotsky?”
