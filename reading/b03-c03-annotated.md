# Book III: The Lustful
## III. Confession of a Passionate Heart: In Verse

<!-- B03-C03-P001 -->

After hearing the order his father had shouted at him from the carriage as he left the monastery, Alyosha stayed where he was for a while, deeply perplexed. Not that he stood rooted to the spot; that was never his way. On the contrary, anxious as he was, he immediately found time to go to the abbot's kitchen and learn what his papa had been up to upstairs. Then, however, he set off, hoping he would somehow solve the problem tormenting him on his way into town. Let me say at once: his father's shouting and his order to move back home, “with your pillows and mattress,” did not frighten him in the least. He understood all too well that this loudly proclaimed order to move, shouted with such a show of anger, had been given “in the heat of the moment”—for artistic effect, one might even say. Much as a townsman here, who had recently gone on a spree at his own name-day party, had suddenly begun smashing his own crockery in front of his guests, tearing up his own and his wife's clothes, wrecking his furniture and finally breaking the windows of his house, all because he was angry at being refused more vodka, and all, once again, for effect. Something of just this sort had happened to papa now, of course. Naturally, the next day, once sober, the townsman had regretted the cups and plates he had smashed. Alyosha knew the old man would certainly let him go back to the monastery the next day, perhaps even that very day. And he was entirely certain that whatever his father might do to anyone else, he would never want to hurt him. Alyosha was certain that no one anywhere would ever want to hurt him; more than that, no one could. This was an axiom for him, something given once and for all, without argument, and with that conviction he went forward without the slightest hesitation.

<!-- B03-C03-P002 -->

But at that moment another fear was stirring inside him, a fear of an entirely different kind, all the more painful because he could not have defined it himself: fear of a woman, specifically Katerina Ivanovna, who had so urgently begged him to come and see her about something in the note Mrs. Khokhlakov had given him earlier. That summons, and the necessity of going, had immediately planted a painful feeling in his heart. All morning, as time went on, it had ached more and more, despite all the subsequent scenes and adventures at the monastery, including the one just now at the abbot's, and so on and so forth. What frightened him was not that he did not know what she would say to him or how he would answer. Nor did he fear her as a woman in general: he knew little of women, of course, but all his life, from infancy until he entered the monastery, he had lived only among them. It was this woman he feared, Katerina Ivanovna herself. He had been afraid of her ever since he first saw her. He had seen her only once or twice, perhaps three times, and had even happened to exchange a few words with her once. He remembered her as a beautiful, proud, imperious young woman. But it was not her beauty that troubled him; it was something else. Precisely because he could not explain his fear, it grew stronger now. Her intentions were of the noblest kind; he knew that. She wanted to save his brother Dmitri, who had already wronged her, and wanted it out of sheer generosity. Yet despite knowing this, despite the justice he could not help doing to all those fine, generous feelings, a chill ran down his spine as he drew closer to her house.

<!-- B03-C03-P003 -->

He realized that he would not find his brother Ivan Fyodorovich, who was so close to her, at her house: Ivan must be with their father now. Still less likely was he to find Dmitri there, and he had an inkling why. Their conversation, then, would take place alone. He badly wanted to see Dmitri before that fateful conversation, to call on him first. Without showing him the letter, he could have a word with him about something. But Dmitri lived a long way off and was certainly not at home now either. After standing still for a minute, Alyosha finally made up his mind. He crossed himself with a quick, habitual gesture and, immediately smiling at something, set off resolutely toward his dreaded lady.

<!-- B03-C03-P004 -->

He knew where her house was. But if he went along Main Street, then across the square and so on, it would be quite a long way. Our little town sprawls a great deal, and the distances can be considerable. Besides, his father was waiting for him, might not yet have forgotten his order, and could turn peevish, so Alyosha had to hurry if he was to manage both visits. With all this in mind, he decided to take a shortcut through the back ways, every one of which he knew like the back of his hand. Taking the back ways meant going almost without paths, along deserted fences, sometimes even climbing over other people's wattle fences and crossing their yards, though everyone there knew him and greeted him. This way he could reach Main Street in half the distance. At one point his route took him very close to his father's house, past the neighboring garden, which belonged to a dilapidated little house, leaning to one side, with four windows. Alyosha knew its owner was a legless old townswoman. She lived with her daughter, a former “civilized” maidservant in the capital, where until quite recently she had always held positions in generals' households. A year before, she had come home because of the old woman's illness, and now she paraded about in elegant dresses. The old woman and her daughter had fallen into terrible poverty, however, and even went next door to Fyodor Pavlovich's kitchen every day for soup and bread. Marfa Ignatyevna willingly ladled out soup for them. But the daughter, who came to collect it, had not sold a single one of her dresses, one of which even had an enormously long train. Alyosha had learned this last detail, quite by chance, of course, from his friend Rakitin, who knew absolutely everything that went on in their little town, and naturally had forgotten it at once. Yet now, as he came abreast of the neighbor's garden, he suddenly remembered that very train, quickly raised his bowed, thoughtful head, and... found himself facing the most unexpected encounter.

<!-- B03-C03-P005 -->

Behind the wattle fence in the neighbor's garden stood his brother Dmitri Fyodorovich. He had climbed onto something and was visible from the chest up, waving his arms at Alyosha as hard as he could, calling and beckoning to him, apparently afraid not merely to shout but even to say a word aloud, for fear of being overheard. Alyosha ran straight to the fence.

<!-- B03-C03-P006 -->

“Good thing you looked up yourself, or I'd have shouted to you in another moment,” Dmitri Fyodorovich whispered joyfully and hurriedly. “Climb over here! Quick! Oh, how wonderful that you've come. I was just thinking about you...”

<!-- B03-C03-P007 -->

Alyosha was glad too; his only puzzle was how to get over the fence. But “Mitya” caught him under the elbow with his powerful arm and helped him over. Gathering up his cassock, Alyosha vaulted across as nimbly as a barefoot town boy.

<!-- B03-C03-P008 -->

“Now we're off! Come on!” burst from Mitya in an ecstatic whisper.

<!-- B03-C03-P009 -->

“Where to?” Alyosha whispered too, looking all around and finding himself in an entirely empty garden, with no one there but the two of them. The garden was small, but the owner's little house was still at least fifty paces away. “There's nobody here. Why are you whispering?”

<!-- B03-C03-P010 -->

“Why am I whispering? Oh, damn it!” Dmitri Fyodorovich suddenly cried at the top of his voice. “Why am I whispering? There, you see for yourself what a muddle nature can suddenly get into. I'm here in secret, keeping watch over a secret. Explanations later. But knowing it was a secret, I suddenly started talking in secret too, whispering like an idiot when there's no need. Come on! Over there! Keep quiet till we get there. I want to kiss you!

> Glory to the Highest in the world,  
> Glory to the Highest within me!..

“I was sitting here repeating that just before you came...”

<!-- B03-C03-P011 -->

The garden covered about a desyatina, or a little more, but trees grew only around its edges, along all four fences: apple trees, maples, lindens, birches. The center was open, a grassy patch that yielded several poods of hay each summer. The owner let the garden out each spring for a few rubles. There were beds of raspberries, gooseberries, and currants too, all along the fences, and vegetable beds close to the house, though these had only recently been planted. Dmitri Fyodorovich led his guest to a corner of the garden farthest from the house. There, among dense lindens and old bushes of currant and elder, guelder rose and lilac, something suddenly came into view that looked like the ruins of an ancient green summerhouse, blackened and crooked, with lattice walls but a covered roof, where one could still shelter from the rain. God knew when it had been built—fifty years before, according to tradition—by a former owner of the little house, Alexander Karlovich von Schmidt, a retired lieutenant colonel. But it had all rotted now, the floor was decayed, every floorboard wobbled, and the wood smelled of damp. Inside stood a green wooden table sunk into the ground, with benches around it, also green, still fit to sit on. Alyosha had noticed his brother's ecstatic state at once, but on entering the summerhouse he saw half a bottle of cognac and a small glass on the table.

<!-- B03-C03-P012 -->

“It's cognac!” Mitya roared with laughter. “And you're already giving me that look: ‘Drinking again?’ Don't believe the phantom.

> Don't believe the empty, lying crowd,  
> Forget your doubts...

“I'm not drinking, I'm only ‘treating myself,’ as that swine of yours, Rakitin, says. He'll be a state councilor and still go on saying ‘treating myself.’ Sit down. Alyoshka, I'd like to take you and crush you against my chest, because in the whole world... truly... tru-ly... (take that in! Take it in!) you're the only one I love!”

<!-- B03-C03-P013 -->

He spoke the last line in something close to a frenzy.

<!-- B03-C03-P014 -->

“Only you, and one ‘vile woman’ I've fallen in love with, and that was the end of me. But falling in love doesn't mean loving. You can fall in love and hate at the same time. Remember that! I'm telling you now, while I'm still cheerful! Sit here at the table, and I'll sit next to you, at the side, and look at you and keep talking. You'll keep quiet, and I'll keep talking, because the time has come. Though, you know, I've decided we really ought to speak softly, because here... here... the most unexpected ears might be listening. I'll explain everything. As I said: to be continued. Why was I desperate to see you, longing for you just now, all these days, and now? (I dropped anchor here five days ago.) All these days? Because you're the only one I'll tell everything to, because I must, because I need you, because tomorrow I come hurtling down from the clouds, because tomorrow life will end and begin. Have you ever felt it, ever dreamed you were falling off a mountain into a pit? Well, I'm falling now, and it isn't a dream. And I'm not afraid, and you mustn't be either. That is, I am afraid, but it's sweet. That is, it isn't sweet, it's ecstasy... Oh, damn it, it doesn't matter, whatever it is. A strong spirit, a weak spirit, a woman's spirit—whatever it is! Let's praise nature: look at all that sunshine, how clear the sky is, the leaves are all green, it's still summer, between three and four in the afternoon, everything's quiet! Where were you going?”

<!-- B03-C03-P015 -->

“To Father's, but first I meant to call on Katerina Ivanovna.”

<!-- B03-C03-P016 -->

“To her and to Father! Oh! What a coincidence! Why do you think I was calling you, wanting you, hungering and thirsting for you with every fold of my soul and even my ribs? Precisely so I could send you to Father on my behalf, and then to her, to Katerina Ivanovna, and have done with both her and Father. To send an angel. I could have sent anyone, but I needed to send an angel. And here you are, going to her and Father of your own accord.”

<!-- B03-C03-P017 -->

“You really meant to send me?” Alyosha burst out, a look of pain on his face.

<!-- B03-C03-P018 -->

“Wait, you knew that. And I can see you've understood everything at once. But keep quiet, keep quiet for now. Don't pity me and don't cry!”

<!-- B03-C03-P019 -->

Dmitri Fyodorovich stood up, thought for a moment, and put a finger to his forehead.

<!-- B03-C03-P020 -->

“She sent for you herself, she wrote you a letter or something, and that's why you were going to her. Otherwise, would you have gone?”

<!-- B03-C03-P021 -->

“Here's the note.” Alyosha took it from his pocket. Mitya quickly read it through.

<!-- B03-C03-P022 -->

“And you took the back ways! Oh, gods! Thank you for sending him through the back ways, so that I caught him like the foolish old fisherman catching the goldfish in the fairy tale. Listen, Alyosha, listen, brother. Now I'm going to tell you everything. For I have to tell someone. I've already told an angel in heaven, but I have to tell an angel on earth too. You're an angel on earth. You'll listen, you'll judge, and you'll forgive... And that's what I need: to be forgiven by someone higher than myself. Listen: if two beings suddenly tear themselves away from everything earthly and fly into the extraordinary, or at least one of them does, and before that, as he flies away or perishes, he comes to the other and says: do such and such for me, something no one ever asks of anyone, something you can only ask on your deathbed—surely the other will do it... if he's a friend, if he's a brother?”

<!-- B03-C03-P023 -->

“I'll do it, but tell me what it is, and tell me quickly,” Alyosha said.

<!-- B03-C03-P024 -->

“Quickly... Hmm. Don't hurry, Alyosha: you're hurrying and worrying. There's no need to hurry now. The world has turned into a new street now. Ah, Alyosha, what a pity you've never thought your way to ecstasy! But what am I saying to him? As if you hadn't thought your way there! What am I saying, blockhead that I am:

> Be noble, man!

“Whose line is that?”

<!-- B03-C03-P025 -->

Alyosha decided to wait. He realized that perhaps all his business really was here now, and nowhere else. Mitya thought for a minute, his elbow on the table, his head resting in his hand. Both were silent for a while.

<!-- B03-C03-P026 -->

“Lyosha,” Mitya said, “you're the only one who won't laugh! I'd like to begin... my confession... with Schiller's hymn to joy. *An die Freude!* But I don't know German, I only know it's *an die Freude*. And don't think this is drunken babbling. I'm not drunk at all. Cognac is cognac, but it takes two bottles to make me drunk,

> And red-faced Silenus  
> On his stumbling donkey—

“and I haven't even drunk a quarter of a bottle, and I'm not Silenus. Not Silenus but strong—*silyon*[^b03-c03-n001]—because I've made my decision for all eternity. Forgive the pun; you'll have to forgive me a great deal today, more than a pun. Don't worry, I'm not spinning this out, I'm talking about what matters and I'll come to the point in a moment. I won't drag this out—won't wrench the kike from my soul.[^b03-c03-n002] Wait, how does it go...”

<!-- B03-C03-P027 -->

He lifted his head, thought for a moment, and suddenly began in ecstasy:[^b03-c03-n003]

> Timid, naked, wild, the troglodyte  
> Hid in caves among the rocks;  
> The nomad wandered through the fields  
> And laid those fields to waste.  
> The hunter, with his spear and arrows,  
> Ranged fearsomely through the woods...  
> Woe to those the waves cast up  
> On inhospitable shores!
>
> Down from the Olympian heights  
> Comes mother Ceres, following  
> Proserpina, stolen from her:  
> A savage world lies before her.  
> No shelter, no refreshment  
> Can the goddess find there anywhere;  
> Nowhere does a temple  
> Bear witness to the worship of the gods.
>
> Fruits of the fields and sweet grape clusters  
> Do not shine at feasts;  
> Only the remains of bodies smoke  
> Upon the bloody altars.  
> And wherever Ceres turns  
> Her sorrowing gaze,  
> Everywhere she sees mankind  
> In deepest degradation!

<!-- B03-C03-P028 -->

Sobs suddenly burst from Mitya's chest. He seized Alyosha's hand.

<!-- B03-C03-P029 -->

“My friend, my friend, in degradation, in degradation even now. Man has so terribly much to endure on earth, so terribly many sorrows! Don't think I'm merely a lout with an officer's rank who drinks cognac and debauches himself. Brother, that's almost all I ever think about, this degraded man—unless I'm lying. God grant I'm not lying now, praising myself. I think about this man because I am that man myself.

> So that man may rise in spirit  
> Out of his baseness,  
> Let him join in everlasting union  
> With ancient Mother Earth.

“But here's the trouble: how am I to join in everlasting union with the earth? I don't kiss the earth, I don't cut open her breast. What am I to do, become a peasant or a little shepherd? I go on my way and I don't know whether I've landed in stench and shame or in light and joy. That's the trouble, because everything in the world is a riddle! And whenever I sank into the deepest, the very deepest shame of debauchery (and that was all that ever happened to me), I always recited that poem about Ceres and man. Did it reform me? Never! Because I'm a Karamazov. Because if I go flying into the abyss, I'll go straight in, headfirst and heels in the air, and I'll even be pleased to be falling in that very position of humiliation, and I'll find beauty in it for myself. And there, in the midst of that shame, I suddenly begin a hymn. Let me be cursed, let me be low and vile, but let me too kiss the hem of the robe in which my God is clothed; even if at the very same time I follow the devil, I am still your son too, Lord, and I love you, and I feel the joy without which the world could neither stand nor be.

> Eternal joy gives drink  
> To the soul of God's creation,  
> With the secret force of fermentation  
> Sets the cup of life aflame;  
> Lured the grass out into light,  
> Unfolded chaos into suns  
> And poured them through the reaches  
> Beyond the astronomer's command.
>
> At kindly nature's breast  
> All that breathes drinks joy;  
> All creatures, all peoples  
> She draws along behind her;  
> Gave us friends in our distress,  
> The grape's juice, the Graces' wreaths,  
> To insects—lust...  
> The angel stands before God.

“But enough poetry! I've shed tears, and you must let me weep. Let it be foolishness that everyone will laugh at, but you won't. Look, your eyes are shining too. Enough poetry. Now I want to tell you about the ‘insects,’ the ones God endowed with lust:

> To insects—lust!

“Brother, I'm that very insect, and those words were written specifically about me. And all of us Karamazovs are the same, and that insect lives in you too, angel, and breeds storms in your blood. Storms they are, because lust is a storm, more than a storm! Beauty is a terrible, a dreadful thing! Terrible because it can't be defined, and it can't be defined because God has set us nothing but riddles. Here the shores meet, here all contradictions live together. Brother, I'm very uneducated, but I've thought a great deal about this. So terribly many mysteries! Too many riddles weigh on man here on earth. Solve them however you can and come through the water dry. Beauty! And I can't bear it when a man, even one of the loftiest heart and mind, starts with the ideal of the Madonna and ends with the ideal of Sodom. More terrible still is the man who already has the ideal of Sodom in his soul, yet does not deny the ideal of the Madonna either, and his heart burns for it, truly, truly burns as it did in the unblemished days of his youth. No, man is broad, much too broad. I'd narrow him. The devil only knows what it all is, that's what! What the mind sees as shame is all beauty to the heart. Is there beauty in Sodom? Believe me, that's precisely where it lies for the vast majority of people—did you know that secret or not? The dreadful thing is that beauty is not only terrible but mysterious. Here the devil battles with God, and the battlefield is human hearts. But then, we all talk about whatever hurts us. Listen, now to the business itself.”

[^b03-c03-n001]: Silenus, a companion of the wine god, appears on a donkey in the quoted poem. In Russian, his name sounds like *silyon*, “strong”—Dmitri’s joke about his resolve.

[^b03-c03-n002]: Dmitri twists Russian expressions for wearing someone down.

[^b03-c03-n003]: Though Dmitri names Schiller’s “Ode to Joy,” these opening stanzas are from Schiller’s “The Eleusinian Festival,” in a Russian version. Ceres is searching for her abducted daughter Proserpina and finds a world before agriculture and settled worship.
