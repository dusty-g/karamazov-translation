# Book IV: Strains
## VI. Strain in the Cottage

<!-- B04-C06-P001 -->
He really was in distress, a kind he had seldom experienced before. He had rushed in and “made a fool of himself”—and over what? Matters of love! “What do I know about that? How can I judge such things?” he repeated to himself for the hundredth time, blushing. “Oh, the shame wouldn't matter; shame is only the punishment I deserve. The trouble is that now I'll certainly be the cause of fresh unhappiness... And the elder sent me to reconcile them, to bring them together. Is that how you bring people together?” Here he suddenly remembered once more how he had “joined their hands,” and again he was terribly ashamed. “Though I did it all sincerely, I must be wiser in future,” he suddenly concluded, without so much as a smile at his conclusion.

<!-- B04-C06-P002 -->
Katerina Ivanovna's errand took him to Lake Street, and his brother Dmitri lived directly on the way, in a lane not far from it. Alyosha decided that in any case he would call on him before visiting the staff captain, though he had a feeling his brother would not be there. He suspected that Dmitri might now deliberately hide from him somehow, but he had to find him at all costs. Meanwhile time was passing: the thought of the dying elder had not left him for a minute, not for a second, since he had come out of the monastery.

<!-- B04-C06-P003 -->
One detail in Katerina Ivanovna's errand had also greatly interested him. When she mentioned the little schoolboy, the staff captain's son, who had run beside his father crying aloud, the thought had already flashed through Alyosha's mind that this boy was surely the schoolboy who had bitten his finger earlier, when he, Alyosha, was asking how he had offended him. Now Alyosha was almost certain of it, though he did not yet know why. These other considerations drew his thoughts away, and he decided not to “think” about the “trouble” he had just caused, not to torment himself with remorse, but to get on with what he had to do, and then whatever happened would happen. This thought finally cheered him. As he turned into the lane to his brother Dmitri's, feeling hungry, he took the roll he had brought from his father's out of his pocket and ate it as he walked. This restored his strength.

<!-- B04-C06-P004 -->
Dmitri was not at home. The owners of the little house—an old carpenter, his son and his elderly wife—even looked at Alyosha suspiciously. “This is the third day he hasn't spent the night here. Maybe he's gone away somewhere,” the old man answered Alyosha's insistent questions. Alyosha realized that he was speaking according to instructions. When he asked, “Isn't he at Grushenka's, or hiding at Foma's again?”—Alyosha deliberately tried these frank questions—all three householders actually looked frightened. “So they love him and stand by him,” Alyosha thought. “That's good.”

<!-- B04-C06-P005 -->
At last he found Kalmykova's house on Lake Street. She belonged to the townspeople's estate; her house was a dilapidated, lopsided little place, with only three windows facing the street and a dirty yard in the middle of which a cow stood alone. The entrance led from the yard into a passage; to the left lived the old landlady and her elderly daughter, both apparently deaf. After he had repeated his question about the staff captain several times, one of them finally understood that he was asking for the lodgers and pointed across the passage to the door of the main room. The staff captain's lodging did in fact prove to be nothing but an ordinary cottage room. Alyosha had taken hold of the iron door handle to open it when the extraordinary silence behind the door suddenly struck him. Yet he knew from Katerina Ivanovna that the retired staff captain had a family. “Either they're all asleep, or perhaps they heard me come and are waiting for me to open the door. I'd better knock again.” And he knocked. An answer came, but not immediately—perhaps ten seconds later.

<!-- B04-C06-P006 -->
“Who is it?” someone shouted in a loud, strenuously angry voice.

<!-- B04-C06-P007 -->
Then Alyosha opened the door and stepped over the threshold. He found himself in a room that, though fairly large, was extremely crowded with people and all sorts of household belongings. On the left was a large Russian stove. A rope had been stretched right across the room, from the stove to the left-hand window, and various rags hung on it. Against each side wall, left and right, stood a bed covered with knitted blankets. On the one to the left a mound of four cotton-covered pillows had been built up, each smaller than the one below. On the other bed, to the right, only one very small pillow could be seen. Farther on, in the front corner, a small space had been partitioned off by a curtain or sheet, likewise thrown over a rope stretched across the corner. From the side, behind this curtain, one could also make out a bed arranged on a bench and a chair placed against it. A plain, square wooden peasant table had been moved from the front corner to the middle window. All three windows, each made up of four small, greenish, moldy panes, were very dim and tightly closed, so that the room was rather stuffy and not very light. On the table stood a frying pan containing the remains of some fried eggs, a partly eaten slice of bread, and also a half-shtof bottle with a scant remnant of worldly blessings at the very bottom. On a chair beside the left-hand bed sat a woman who looked like a lady, wearing a printed cotton dress. Her face was very thin and yellow; her deeply sunken cheeks revealed her ill health at first glance. But what struck Alyosha most was the poor lady's expression—extremely questioning and at the same time terribly haughty. Until she herself spoke, while Alyosha was talking to the man of the house, she kept moving her large brown eyes from one speaker to the other with the same questioning haughtiness. Beside this lady, by the left-hand window, stood a young girl with a rather unattractive face and thin reddish hair, poorly but very neatly dressed. She surveyed Alyosha with distaste as he came in. On the right, also beside a bed, sat another female figure. She was a very pitiable creature, also a young girl, about twenty, but hunchbacked and unable to walk: her legs had withered, as Alyosha was told later. Her crutches stood beside her in the corner between the bed and the wall. The poor girl's remarkably beautiful, kind eyes looked at Alyosha with a sort of quiet gentleness. At the table, finishing his eggs, sat a gentleman of about forty-five, short, lean, weakly built and reddish-haired, with a sparse reddish beard very like a frayed scrubber of bast fiber. For some reason this comparison, and especially the word “scrubber,” flashed through Alyosha's mind at his very first glance; he remembered it afterward. Evidently this was the gentleman who had shouted “Who is it?” from behind the door, since there was no other man in the room. But as Alyosha entered he all but sprang off the bench on which he had been sitting at the table and, hastily wiping himself with a threadbare napkin, flew toward him.

<!-- B04-C06-P008 -->
“A monk begging for the monastery! He knew where to come!” the girl standing in the left-hand corner said loudly. But the gentleman hurrying toward Alyosha wheeled round to her on his heels and answered in an agitated, breaking voice:

<!-- B04-C06-P009 -->
“No, Miss Varvara Nikolavna, that isn't it, you've guessed wrong! Allow me to ask in my turn,” he suddenly turned back to Alyosha, “what has prompted you, sir, to visit... these depths, sir?”

<!-- B04-C06-P010 -->
Alyosha looked at him attentively; he had never seen this man before. There was something awkward, hurried and irritable about him. Though he had evidently just been drinking, he was not drunk. His face expressed an extreme insolence and at the same time—strange as that was—an evident cowardice. He resembled a man who had spent a long time submitting and enduring, but had suddenly sprung up and wanted to assert himself. Or, better still, a man who terribly wanted to hit you, but was terribly afraid that you would hit him. In his words and in the intonation of his rather shrill voice there was a sort of clownish humor, now spiteful, now timid, unable to sustain its tone and continually breaking down. He had asked his question about “these depths” almost trembling all over, his eyes bulging, and had darted so close to Alyosha that the latter mechanically took a step backward. This gentleman was wearing a dark, very shabby coat of some nankeen-like fabric, darned and stained. His trousers were extraordinarily light-colored, of a kind nobody had worn for a long time, checked and made of some very thin material; they were crumpled at the bottom and had ridden up, as though he had outgrown them like a little boy.

<!-- B04-C06-P011 -->
“I'm... Alexei Karamazov...” Alyosha began in reply.

<!-- B04-C06-P012 -->
“Perfectly capable of comprehending, sir,” the gentleman cut in at once, giving him to understand that he already knew who he was. “And I, sir, am Staff Captain Snegiryov, in my turn. But I should still like to know what precisely prompted...”

<!-- B04-C06-P013 -->
“I simply called in. Really, I wanted to say something to you on my own behalf... If you'll allow me...”

<!-- B04-C06-P014 -->
“In that case, here is a chair, sir; kindly take your place, sir. That's what they used to say in the old comedies: ‘Kindly take your place’...” With a quick gesture the staff captain seized an empty chair—a plain peasant chair, all wood, with no upholstery—and set it almost in the middle of the room. Then, taking another such chair for himself, he sat down opposite Alyosha, as close to him as before, so that their knees almost touched.

<!-- B04-C06-P015 -->
“Nikolai Ilyich Snegiryov, sir, formerly a staff captain in the Russian infantry; disgraced by his vices, but a staff captain all the same. I ought more properly to be called Staff Captain Yes-Sir than Snegiryov, since I only began adding ‘sir’ to everything in the second half of my life. That ‘sir’ is acquired through humiliation.”

<!-- B04-C06-P016 -->
“That's quite true,” Alyosha said with a faint smile. “Only, is it acquired involuntarily or on purpose?”

<!-- B04-C06-P017 -->
“God knows, involuntarily. Never used it, went my whole life without saying ‘sir’ at every turn, and then suddenly I fell and got up saying it. It's the work of a higher power. I see you take an interest in contemporary questions. But how have I managed to excite such curiosity, since the conditions in which I live make hospitality impossible?”

<!-- B04-C06-P018 -->
“I've come... about that very matter...”

<!-- B04-C06-P019 -->
“That very matter?” the staff captain interrupted impatiently.

<!-- B04-C06-P020 -->
“About that encounter you had with my brother Dmitri Fyodorovich,” Alyosha said abruptly and awkwardly.

<!-- B04-C06-P021 -->
“What encounter would that be, sir? Could it be that very one, sir? So this is about the scrubber, the bath scrubber?” He suddenly leaned forward so far that this time his knees positively knocked against Alyosha's. His lips pressed together into a peculiarly thin line.

<!-- B04-C06-P022 -->
“What scrubber?” Alyosha murmured.

<!-- B04-C06-P023 -->
“He's come to complain to you about me, Papa!” cried the now familiar voice of the boy from earlier, behind the curtain in the corner. “I'm the one who bit his finger!”

<!-- B04-C06-P024 -->
The curtain was pulled aside, and Alyosha saw his earlier enemy in the corner beneath the icons, on the bed contrived from a bench and a chair. The boy lay covered with his little coat and an old quilt. He was evidently ill and, judging by his burning eyes, feverish. He looked at Alyosha fearlessly now, unlike earlier, as though to say, “I'm at home; you can't get at me now.”

<!-- B04-C06-P025 -->
“What finger did you bite?” The staff captain half rose from his chair. “He bit your finger, sir?”

<!-- B04-C06-P026 -->
“Yes, mine. Earlier, in the street, he was throwing stones at some boys and they were throwing them at him—six of them against him alone. I went up to him, and he threw a stone at me too, then another at my head. I asked what I'd done to him. He suddenly rushed at me and bit my finger badly; I don't know why.”

<!-- B04-C06-P027 -->
“I'll flog him at once, sir! This very minute, sir!” The staff captain sprang right out of his chair.

<!-- B04-C06-P028 -->
“But I'm not complaining at all, I was only telling you. I don't want you to flog him at all. Besides, he seems to be ill now...”

<!-- B04-C06-P029 -->
“And you thought I would flog him, sir? That I'd take my Ilyushechka and flog him here before you for your complete satisfaction? Do you require it promptly, sir?” the staff captain said, suddenly turning toward Alyosha with a movement as though he meant to attack him. “I'm sorry about your finger, sir, but before I flog Ilyushechka, would you like me to chop off four fingers of my own, this very moment, before your eyes, with this very knife, to give you the satisfaction you deserve? Four fingers should be enough, I think, to quench your thirst for vengeance, sir; you wouldn't demand a fifth?..” He suddenly stopped, as though choking. Every feature of his face was moving and twitching, while his eyes looked fiercely defiant. He was almost beside himself.

<!-- B04-C06-P030 -->
“I think I understand everything now,” Alyosha answered quietly and sadly, remaining seated. “So your boy is a good boy, who loves his father, and he attacked me because I'm the brother of the man who insulted you... I understand that now,” he repeated thoughtfully. “But my brother Dmitri Fyodorovich regrets what he did, I know that, and if he can possibly come to you, or better still meet you again in that same place, he will ask your forgiveness in front of everyone... if you wish it.”

<!-- B04-C06-P031 -->
“You mean, tore out a man's wretched beard and then asked his pardon... And that finishes the business and gives satisfaction, does it, sir?”

<!-- B04-C06-P032 -->
“Oh no, on the contrary, he'll do anything you wish, in any way you wish!”

<!-- B04-C06-P033 -->
“So if I asked his lordship to go down on his knees before me in that very tavern, sir—‘The Capital City’ is its name—or out in the square, sir, he would?”

<!-- B04-C06-P034 -->
“Yes, he would go down on his knees.”

<!-- B04-C06-P035 -->
“You've pierced me, sir. You've brought tears to my eyes and pierced me. I'm too susceptible to feeling. Then allow me to introduce myself fully: my family, my two daughters and my son—my litter, sir. When I die, who will love them, sir? And while I'm alive, who will love a shabby creature like me, except them? This is a great thing the Lord has arranged for every man of my sort, sir. Because there must be at least someone who can love even a man like me...”

<!-- B04-C06-P036 -->
“Oh, that's absolutely true!” Alyosha exclaimed.

<!-- B04-C06-P037 -->
“Oh, stop your clowning at last! Some fool comes along and you disgrace us!” the girl by the window suddenly cried out, addressing her father with a look of disgust and contempt.

<!-- B04-C06-P038 -->
“Wait a little, Varvara Nikolavna, allow me to pursue my course,” her father shouted, in a commanding tone, though he looked at her very approvingly. “That's the kind of character we have here, sir.” He turned back to Alyosha.

“Though that ought to be in the feminine: would *she* consent to bless, sir. But allow me to introduce you to my wife as well. Here is Arina Petrovna, sir, a lady without legs, aged forty-three. Her legs do walk, but only a little, sir. Of humble birth, sir. Arina Petrovna, smooth your features: this is Alexei Fyodorovich Karamazov. Stand up, Alexei Fyodorovich.” He took him by the hand and suddenly pulled him to his feet with a strength one would never have expected. “You're being introduced to a lady; you must stand, sir. Not the Karamazov, Mama, who... hm, and so forth, but his brother, shining with the virtues of humility. Allow me, Arina Petrovna, allow me, Mama, allow me to kiss your hand first.”

<!-- B04-C06-P039 -->
And he kissed his wife's hand respectfully, even tenderly. The girl by the window turned her back on the scene in indignation; the wife's haughty, questioning face suddenly expressed extraordinary affection.

<!-- B04-C06-P040 -->
“How do you do? Sit down, Mr. Chernomazov,” she said.

<!-- B04-C06-P041 -->
“Karamazov, Mama, Karamazov—we're of humble birth, sir,” he whispered again.

<!-- B04-C06-P042 -->
“Well, Karamazov or whatever it is, I always say Chernomazov... Do sit down; why did he make you stand? A lady without legs, he says. I have legs, only they're swollen up like buckets, while the rest of me has wasted away. I used to be so fat, and now look at me, as if I'd swallowed a needle...”

<!-- B04-C06-P043 -->
“We're of humble birth, sir, of humble birth,” the captain prompted once more.

<!-- B04-C06-P044 -->
“Papa, oh, Papa!” suddenly said the hunchbacked girl, who had so far sat silently in her chair, and she suddenly covered her eyes with a handkerchief.

<!-- B04-C06-P045 -->
“Clown!” the girl at the window blurted out.

<!-- B04-C06-P046 -->
“You see the news we have here,” the mother said, spreading her hands toward her daughters. “Like clouds passing; the clouds pass and our music starts again. Before, when we were in the army, we used to have lots of visitors like that. I don't bring that into the matter, my dear. Let whoever loves someone go on loving them. The deacon's wife came then and said, ‘Alexander Alexandrovich is a man with the finest soul, but Nastasya Petrovna,’ she says, ‘is a spawn of hell.’ ‘Well,’ I answer, ‘that depends on who adores whom, but you're a little heap with a big stink.’ ‘And you,’ she says, ‘need to be kept in obedience.’ ‘You black sword, you,’ I tell her, ‘who have you come to teach?’ ‘I let clean air in,’ she says, ‘and you let in foul air.’ ‘Well, ask all the gentlemen officers,’ I answer her, ‘whether my air is foul or some other kind.’ And it's been weighing on my soul ever since, so that the other day I was sitting here just as I am now, and I saw that same general come in who visited us at Easter. ‘Tell me, Your Excellency,’ I say, ‘is a lady of good birth allowed to let air in freely?’ ‘Yes,’ he answers, ‘you ought to open the little windowpane or the door, because the air in here isn't fresh.’ And they're all like that! What is it about my air that troubles them? The dead smell worse. ‘I'm not spoiling your air,’ I say. ‘I'll have some shoes made and go away.’ My dears, my darlings, don't reproach your own mother! Nikolai Ilyich, my dear, have I displeased you? All I have is my Ilyushechka coming home from school and loving me. Yesterday he brought an apple. Forgive your own mother, my dears, forgive her, my darlings, forgive me, all alone as I am—why has my air become so unpleasant to you?”

<!-- B04-C06-P047 -->
The poor woman suddenly broke into sobs, tears streaming down her face. The staff captain rushed over to her.

<!-- B04-C06-P048 -->
“Mama, Mama, my darling, enough, enough! You're not alone. Everyone loves you, everyone adores you!” He began kissing both her hands again and gently stroking her face with his palms; then, seizing the napkin, he suddenly began wiping the tears from her face. Alyosha even thought he saw tears glittering in the captain's own eyes. “Well, sir, do you see? Do you hear, sir?” he suddenly turned to him almost furiously, pointing at the poor, feeble-minded woman.

<!-- B04-C06-P049 -->
“I see and hear,” Alyosha murmured.

<!-- B04-C06-P050 -->
“Papa, Papa! You're not going to... with him... Leave him alone, Papa!” the boy suddenly cried, half rising in his bed and looking at his father with burning eyes.

<!-- B04-C06-P051 -->
“Will you stop your clowning at last, stop putting on those stupid antics that never lead to anything!” Varvara Nikolaevna shouted from the same corner, now thoroughly angry; she even stamped her foot.

<!-- B04-C06-P052 -->
“You are entirely right to lose your temper this time, Varvara Nikolavna, and I shall satisfy you with all speed. Put on your little hat, Alexei Fyodorovich; I'll take my cap here, and let us go, sir. I must say a serious word to you, but outside these walls. This girl sitting here is my daughter, sir, Nina Nikolaevna—I forgot to introduce her to you—a heavenly angel in the flesh... flown down to us mortals... if only you can understand that...”

<!-- B04-C06-P053 -->
“Why, he's shaking all over, as though he were in convulsions,” Varvara Nikolaevna continued indignantly.

<!-- B04-C06-P054 -->
“And this one, who is now stamping her little foot at me and denounced me as a clown just now—she too is a heavenly angel in the flesh, sir, and she was right to call me that, sir. Let us go then, Alexei Fyodorovich; this must be settled, sir...”

<!-- B04-C06-P055 -->
And taking Alyosha by the arm, he led him out of the room and straight into the street.
