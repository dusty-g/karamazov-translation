# Book XI — Brother Ivan Fyodorovich
## IX. The Devil. Ivan Fyodorovich's Nightmare

<!-- B11-C09-P001 -->
I am no doctor, yet I feel the moment has come when I absolutely must explain something, at least, about the nature of Ivan Fyodorovich's illness to the reader. Looking ahead, I shall say only this: on that very evening he stood on the verge of a delirious fever that would at last take full possession of his constitution, long disordered but stubbornly resisting illness. Knowing nothing of medicine, I venture the supposition that perhaps, by a tremendous effort of will, he really had managed to postpone the illness for a time, dreaming, of course, of overcoming it altogether. He knew he was unwell, but recoiled from the prospect of being ill now, at these approaching, fateful moments of his life, when he had to be present, speak his word boldly and decisively, and himself “justify himself before himself.” He had, however, once gone to the new doctor from Moscow, whom Katerina Ivanovna had sent for because of a fancy of hers I have already mentioned. After hearing and examining him, the doctor concluded that he had something even resembling a disorder of the brain, and was not at all surprised by a certain admission Ivan made to him, though with repugnance. “Hallucinations are quite possible in your condition,” the doctor decided, “though we should investigate them... In any case, you must begin serious treatment without losing a minute, or things will go badly.” But after leaving him, Ivan Fyodorovich had not followed the sensible advice, and had neglected to take to his bed for treatment. “I can still walk, I still have my strength. If I collapse, that's another matter; then let whoever likes treat me,” he decided, dismissing it with a wave. And so he sat there now, almost conscious himself that he was delirious, and, as I have said, gazed persistently at something on the sofa against the opposite wall. Someone had suddenly appeared sitting there, God knows how he had entered, for he had not been in the room when Ivan Fyodorovich returned from Smerdyakov. He was a gentleman, or rather a Russian gentleman of a certain sort, no longer young, “qui frisait la cinquantaine,” nearing fifty, as the French say, with only a little gray in his dark, fairly long and still thick hair and his short, pointed beard. He wore a brown jacket, evidently made by the best tailor but already worn, sewn perhaps the year before last and now utterly out of fashion, so that no well-to-do man of society had worn one like it for two years. His linen, his long scarf-like tie, everything was as with any stylish gentleman, but on closer inspection the linen was rather dirty and the broad scarf much worn. The visitor's checked trousers fitted beautifully, but again they were too light and somehow too tight, as people no longer wore them; equally out of place was the soft white felt hat he had brought, quite wrong for the season. In short, an appearance of respectability with very slender means. The gentleman seemed to belong to the class of former landowners who had never soiled their hands and had flourished under serfdom; a man who had plainly seen the world and good society, once had connections and perhaps still retained them, but, growing gradually impoverished after a merry youth and the recent abolition of serfdom, had turned into a sort of well-mannered dependent, wandering among kindly old acquaintances. They received him for his agreeable, accommodating nature and also because he was, after all, a respectable man who could be seated at their table in anyone's company, though in a modest place, of course. Such dependents, gentlemen of agreeable character who can tell a story and make up a card party, and who decidedly dislike having errands thrust upon them, are usually alone—bachelors or widowers. They may have children, but the children are always being brought up somewhere far away by some aunts whom the gentleman hardly ever mentions in good society, as though a little ashamed of the connection. Little by little he grows quite estranged from his children, receiving an occasional letter of congratulations on his name day or at Christmas, and sometimes even answering it. The unexpected visitor's face was not exactly good-natured; rather, it too was accommodating, ready to assume any obliging expression the circumstances required. He wore no watch, but had a tortoiseshell lorgnette on a black ribbon. A massive gold ring with an inexpensive opal adorned the middle finger of his right hand. Ivan Fyodorovich maintained an angry silence and would not begin. The visitor waited, sitting precisely like a dependent who had just come downstairs from the room allotted to him to keep his host company over tea, but remained meekly silent because the host was occupied and thinking gloomily about something; ready, however, for any pleasant conversation the moment the host began. Suddenly his face took on a look of unexpected concern.

“Listen,” he began to Ivan Fyodorovich, “forgive me, I only mean to remind you: you went to Smerdyakov to find out about Katerina Ivanovna, and left without finding out anything about her. You must have forgotten...”

<!-- B11-C09-P003 -->
“Ah, yes!” burst suddenly from Ivan, and his face darkened with concern. “Yes, I forgot... But it doesn't matter now; everything tomorrow,” he muttered to himself. “And you,” he turned irritably to the visitor, “I was about to remember that myself, because it was precisely what was weighing on me! You pop up, and I'm supposed to believe you prompted me, instead of my remembering it myself?”

<!-- B11-C09-P004 -->
“Then don't believe it,” the gentleman smiled gently. “What sort of belief comes by force? Besides, no proofs help with belief, especially material ones. Thomas believed not because he saw the risen Christ, but because he already wished to believe. Take the spiritualists, for instance... I'm very fond of them... Imagine, they think they're useful to faith because devils from the other world show them their little horns. ‘That's material proof, so to speak, that the other world exists.’ The other world and material proof—tra-la-la! And finally, if the devil is proved, is it certain God is proved too? I want to join an idealist society; I'll form the opposition: ‘A realist, you understand, not a materialist, heh-heh!’”

<!-- B11-C09-P005 -->
“Listen,” Ivan Fyodorovich suddenly rose from the table. “I'm certainly delirious now... of course I'm delirious... Tell whatever lies you like, I don't care! You won't drive me into a frenzy as you did last time. Only I feel ashamed of something... I want to walk about the room... Sometimes I can't see you or even hear your voice, as last time, but I always guess what nonsense you're spouting, because it's me, I'm speaking myself, not you! Only I don't know whether last time I was asleep or saw you awake. I'll wet a towel with cold water and put it on my head, and perhaps you'll evaporate.”

<!-- B11-C09-P006 -->
Ivan Fyodorovich went to the corner, took a towel, did as he had said, and began walking up and down the room with the wet towel on his head.

<!-- B11-C09-P007 -->
“I like how we've gone straight to the familiar ‘you,’” the visitor began.

<!-- B11-C09-P008 -->
“Fool,” Ivan laughed. “Would you expect me to address you formally? I'm cheerful now, only my temple hurts... and the top of my head... Only please don't philosophize as you did last time. If you can't clear off, tell some amusing lies. Gossip—you're a hanger-on, so gossip. What a nightmare to get stuck with! But I'm not afraid of you. I'll overcome you. They won't cart me off to the madhouse!”

<!-- B11-C09-P009 -->
“C'est charmant—how charming—a hanger-on. Yes, that's exactly my proper form. What am I on earth but a hanger-on? Incidentally, I listen to you with a little surprise: by God, you seem to be gradually beginning to take me for something real, instead of merely your fantasy, as you insisted last time...”

<!-- B11-C09-P010 -->
“Not for a single moment do I take you for something real,” Ivan cried, even with a kind of fury. “You're a lie, you're my illness, you're a phantom. I simply don't know how to destroy you, and I see I must suffer for a while. You're my hallucination. You're an embodiment of myself, though only one side of me... my thoughts and feelings, only the nastiest and stupidest. From that point of view you might even interest me, if only I had time to bother with you...”

<!-- B11-C09-P011 -->
“Wait, wait, I'll catch you out: earlier, by the streetlamp, when you turned on Alyosha and shouted, ‘You found out from him! How did you find out he visits me?’—you were thinking of me then. So for one tiny moment you did believe, you believed I really existed,” the gentleman laughed softly.

<!-- B11-C09-P012 -->
“Yes, that was a weakness of nature... but I couldn't believe in you. I don't know whether I was asleep or walking about last time. Perhaps I only saw you in a dream then, not awake at all...”

<!-- B11-C09-P013 -->
“And why were you so harsh with him earlier, with Alyosha? He's sweet; I owe him amends for Elder Zosima.”

<!-- B11-C09-P014 -->
“Be quiet about Alyosha! How dare you, lackey!” Ivan laughed again.

<!-- B11-C09-P015 -->
“You swear, but you laugh—a good sign. You're much more pleasant to me today than last time, though, and I understand why: that great decision...”

<!-- B11-C09-P016 -->
“Be quiet about the decision!” Ivan cried savagely.

<!-- B11-C09-P017 -->
“I understand, I understand, c'est noble, c'est charmant; you're going to defend your brother tomorrow and sacrifice yourself... c'est chevaleresque.[^b11-c09-n001]”

<!-- B11-C09-P018 -->
“Shut up, or I'll kick you!”

<!-- B11-C09-P019 -->
“In a way I'll be glad, for then my purpose is achieved: if there are kicks, you believe in my reality, because you don't kick a phantom. Joking aside, it's all the same to me; swear if you like, but still it's better to be a little more polite, even to me. ‘Fool’ and ‘lackey’—what sort of language is that!”

<!-- B11-C09-P020 -->
“When I abuse you, I abuse myself!” Ivan laughed again. “You're me, myself, only with a different mug. You say precisely what I'm already thinking... and you can't tell me anything new!”

<!-- B11-C09-P021 -->
“If our thoughts agree, that only does me credit,” the gentleman said with delicacy and dignity.

<!-- B11-C09-P022 -->
“Only you take all my vile thoughts, and above all the stupid ones. You're stupid and vulgar. Terribly stupid. No, I can't bear you! What am I to do, what am I to do!” Ivan ground out.

<!-- B11-C09-P023 -->
“My friend, I still want to be a gentleman and to be received as one,” the visitor began, in a fit of the particular wounded pride of a dependent, already yielding and good-natured in advance. “I'm poor, but... I won't say very honest, but... In society it's generally taken as an axiom that I'm a fallen angel. By God, I can't imagine how I could ever have been an angel. If I ever was, it was so long ago there's no sin in forgetting. Now I value only my reputation as a respectable man and live as best I can, trying to be agreeable. I sincerely love people—oh, I've been slandered in so many ways! Here, when I move in among you from time to time, my life takes on something like actual reality, and that's what I like most. For I too, like you, suffer from the fantastic, and so I love your earthly realism. Here everything has an outline, here are formulas, here is geometry, while with us it's all some sort of indeterminate equations! Here I walk about and dream. I love dreaming. Besides, on earth I grow superstitious—please don't laugh: that's precisely what I like, becoming superstitious. Here I adopt all your habits. I've taken to going to the public bathhouse—can you imagine?—and I love taking steam baths with merchants and priests. My dream is to be incarnated, but finally, irrevocably, as some fat merchant's wife weighing seven poods, and to believe everything she believes. My ideal is to go into church and light a candle with a pure heart—by God, it is. Then my sufferings would be at an end. I've also taken to being treated by your doctors: in spring smallpox broke out, and I went to the foundling hospital to have myself vaccinated. If you knew how pleased I was that day: I donated ten rubles to our brother Slavs!.. But you're not listening. You seem very much out of sorts today, you know.” The gentleman paused a little. “I know you went to that doctor yesterday... Well, how's your health? What did the doctor say?”

<!-- B11-C09-P024 -->
“Fool!” Ivan snapped.

<!-- B11-C09-P025 -->
“But how clever you are. Swearing again? It's not that I'm asking out of sympathy, just so. Don't answer, if you like. Now rheumatism has started up again...”

<!-- B11-C09-P026 -->
“Fool,” Ivan repeated once more.

<!-- B11-C09-P027 -->
“You keep on with that, but I caught such a dose of rheumatism last year that I still remember it.”

<!-- B11-C09-P028 -->
“The devil with rheumatism?”

<!-- B11-C09-P029 -->
“Why not, if I sometimes take on flesh? If I take on flesh, I accept the consequences. Satana sum et nihil humanum a me alienum puto—I am Satan, and nothing human is alien to me.”

<!-- B11-C09-P030 -->
“What, what? Satana sum et nihil humanum... That's not stupid, for a devil!”

<!-- B11-C09-P031 -->
“I'm glad to have pleased you at last.”

<!-- B11-C09-P032 -->
“But you didn't get that from me,” Ivan suddenly stopped as though struck. “That never occurred to me. It's strange...”

<!-- B11-C09-P033 -->
“C'est du nouveau, n'est-ce pas? Something new, isn't it? This time I'll be honest and explain. Listen: in dreams, and especially nightmares—from indigestion or something—a person sometimes sees such artistic dreams, such a complex and real actuality, such events or even a whole world of events, bound up in such a plot, with such unexpected details, from your highest manifestations down to the last button on a shirtfront, that I swear to you Leo Tolstoy couldn't invent it. Yet sometimes it's not writers at all who have these dreams, but the most ordinary people, clerks, newspaper columnists, priests... There's a whole problem in that: one minister actually admitted to me himself that all his best ideas come to him when he's asleep. Well, it's the same now. Though I'm your hallucination, as in a nightmare I say original things that have never occurred to you before, so that I'm not merely repeating your thoughts at all; and yet I'm only your nightmare, nothing more.”

<!-- B11-C09-P034 -->
“You're lying. Your very purpose is to convince me you exist independently, and aren't my nightmare, yet now you confirm yourself that you're a dream.”

<!-- B11-C09-P035 -->
“My friend, today I've adopted a special method; I'll explain it later. Wait, where was I? Yes, I caught cold then, only not here with you, but still over there...”

<!-- B11-C09-P036 -->
“Where is ‘there’? Tell me, how long are you going to stay? Can't you go away?” Ivan exclaimed, almost in despair. He stopped walking, sat on the sofa, leaned his elbows on the table again, and gripped his head with both hands. He tore off the wet towel and threw it aside in vexation: evidently it did no good.

<!-- B11-C09-P037 -->
“Your nerves are upset,” the gentleman observed with an easy, casual, yet perfectly friendly air. “You're angry with me even for being capable of catching cold, yet it happened in the most natural way. I was hurrying to a diplomatic evening at the house of a great Petersburg lady who had her sights on a ministerial post. Well, tailcoat, white tie, gloves—yet I was still God knows where, and to reach your earth I still had to fly across space... Of course it's only an instant, but even a ray of sunlight takes a full eight minutes to travel, and imagine me in a tailcoat and open waistcoat. Spirits don't freeze, but once you've taken on flesh... In short, I was being flighty and set off, yet in those spaces, in the ether, in those waters which were above the firmament, the cold is so... But cold isn't the word, you can't even call it cold. Can you imagine: a hundred and fifty degrees below zero! You know the village girls' prank: in thirty-degree frost they challenge some novice to lick an ax. His tongue freezes to it at once, and the blockhead tears the skin off till it bleeds. Yet that's only at thirty degrees; at a hundred and fifty, I should think you'd only have to touch your finger to the ax and it would be gone, if... if there could happen to be an ax there...”

<!-- B11-C09-P038 -->
“Could there happen to be an ax there?” Ivan Fyodorovich suddenly interrupted absently and with disgust. He was struggling with all his strength not to believe his delirium and lapse completely into madness.

<!-- B11-C09-P039 -->
“An ax?” the visitor repeated in surprise.

<!-- B11-C09-P040 -->
“Yes, what would happen to an ax there?” Ivan Fyodorovich suddenly cried with a kind of savage, insistent obstinacy.

<!-- B11-C09-P041 -->
“What would happen to an ax in space? Quelle idée! What an idea! If it got far enough away, I suppose it would begin circling the earth as a satellite, without knowing why. Astronomers would calculate the rising and setting of the ax, Gatsuk would enter it in his almanac, and that would be that.”

<!-- B11-C09-P042 -->
“You're stupid, terribly stupid!” Ivan said obstinately. “Tell cleverer lies or I won't listen. You want to overpower me with realism, convince me you exist, but I don't want to believe you exist! I won't believe it!”

<!-- B11-C09-P043 -->
“But I'm not lying; it's all true. Unfortunately, the truth is almost always unwitty. I see you're determined to expect something grand from me, perhaps even something beautiful. That's a great pity, because I give only what I can.”

<!-- B11-C09-P044 -->
“Don't philosophize, ass!”

<!-- B11-C09-P045 -->
“What philosophy can there be when my whole right side's paralyzed and I'm groaning and grunting? I've been to the entire medical profession. They're excellent at diagnosis, explain the whole illness to you on their fingers, but they can't cure it. There was one enthusiastic student: even if you die, he said, at least you'll know exactly what illness you died of! And then there's their habit of sending you to specialists: we only diagnose, they say, but go to such-and-such a specialist and he'll cure you. The old doctor who treated every illness has completely, completely vanished, I tell you; now there are only specialists, all advertising in the papers. If your nose hurts, they send you to Paris: there's a European specialist there who treats noses. You arrive in Paris, he examines your nose: I can only cure your right nostril, he'll say, because I don't treat left nostrils, that's not my specialty. After me, go to Vienna; a special specialist there will finish curing the left nostril. What can you do? I resorted to folk remedies. One German doctor advised rubbing myself with honey and salt on the bathhouse bench. I went solely for another excuse to go to the bathhouse: made myself filthy all over, and no good came of it. In despair I wrote to Count Mattei in Milan; he sent a book and some drops—God be with him. And imagine: Hoff's malt extract helped! I bought it by chance, drank a bottle and a half, and I could have danced; it was all gone as if by magic. I decided I simply must publish a ‘thank you’ to him in the papers—gratitude spoke up in me. And imagine, here another story began: not one editorial office would accept it! ‘It would be too reactionary,’ they said. ‘Nobody will believe it, le diable n'existe point—the devil no longer exists. Publish it anonymously,’ they advised. But what sort of ‘thank you’ is it anonymously? I joked with the office clerks: ‘It's believing in God that's reactionary nowadays,’ I said, ‘but I'm the devil; you can believe in me.’ ‘We understand,’ they said. ‘Who doesn't believe in the devil? But still, it won't do. It might harm our editorial line. Unless as a joke?’ But as a joke, I thought, it wouldn't be witty. So they never published it. And would you believe it, I even took it to heart. My finest feelings, gratitude for example, are formally forbidden me solely because of my social position.”

“Off into philosophy again!” Ivan ground out hatefully.

By some appointment made before time began, which I've never been able to understand, I'm assigned to ‘negate,’ whereas I'm sincerely kind and quite incapable of negation. No, off you go and negate, they say; without negation there'll be no criticism, and what sort of journal has no ‘criticism department’? Without criticism there'll be nothing but ‘hosanna.’ But ‘hosanna’ alone isn't enough for life; that ‘hosanna’ must pass through the crucible of doubt, and so on, in that vein. I don't involve myself in any of it, though. I didn't create it, and I'm not responsible. So they chose a scapegoat, made him write for the criticism department, and life came into being. We understand this comedy: I, for instance, simply and plainly demand annihilation for myself. No, live, they say, because without you nothing will happen. If everything on earth were sensible, nothing would ever happen. Without you there'll be no events, and there must be events. I'll drop only a tiny seed of faith into you, and an oak will grow from it—such an oak that, sitting in it, you'll want to join the ‘hermit fathers and chaste women,’ for secretly you very, very much want that. You'll eat locusts and trudge off to the desert to save your soul!”

<!-- B11-C09-P076 -->
“So you're working for my soul's salvation, you scoundrel?”

<!-- B11-C09-P077 -->
“One must do a good deed sometime. How angry you are, how angry, I see!”

<!-- B11-C09-P078 -->
“Buffoon! Have you ever tempted those people who eat locusts and pray for seventeen years in the bare desert, covered in moss?”

<!-- B11-C09-P079 -->
“My dear fellow, I've done nothing else. You forget the whole world and all the worlds and fasten on one such man, because the diamond is so very precious; one such soul sometimes equals a whole constellation—we have our own arithmetic. What a precious victory! Yet some of them, by God, are no less developed than you, though you won't believe it. They can contemplate such abysses of belief and unbelief at the very same moment that sometimes it truly seems there's only a hair's breadth left before the man goes ‘head over heels,’ as the actor Gorbunov says.”

<!-- B11-C09-P080 -->
“Well, and did they leave you with your nose?[^b11-c09-n002]”

<!-- B11-C09-P081 -->
“My friend,” the visitor observed sententiously, “it's still better to come away with a nose than sometimes with no nose at all, as a certain ailing marquis recently remarked—he must have been treated by a specialist—in confession to his Jesuit spiritual father. I was there: sheer delight. ‘Give me back my nose!’ he said, beating his breast. ‘My son,’ the father evaded, ‘by the inscrutable decrees of Providence everything is compensated, and visible misfortune sometimes brings extraordinary, though invisible, benefit. If stern fate has deprived you of your nose, your advantage is that no one will ever dare tell you you've been left with your nose.’ ‘Holy Father, that's no comfort!’ the despairing man exclaimed. ‘On the contrary, I'd be delighted to be left with my nose every day of my life, if only it were in its proper place!’ ‘My son,’ the father sighed, ‘you cannot demand every blessing at once, and this is already a complaint against Providence, which has not forgotten you even here; for if you cry out, as you just did, that you'd gladly be left with your nose all your life, then even here your wish has indirectly been granted: for in losing your nose, you have, all the same, been left with your nose, as it were...’”

<!-- B11-C09-P082 -->
“Ugh, how stupid!” Ivan cried.

<!-- B11-C09-P083 -->
“My friend, I only wanted to make you laugh, but I swear it's genuine Jesuit casuistry, and I swear it happened word for word as I've told you. It's a recent affair and caused me a great deal of trouble. The unfortunate young man went home and shot himself that very night; I never left his side until the last moment... As for those Jesuit confession booths, they're truly my favorite amusement in life's sad moments. Here's another incident, only the other day. A little blonde, a Norman girl of about twenty, comes to an old father. Beauty, flesh, nature—makes your mouth water. She bends down and whispers her sin through the little opening. ‘What, my daughter, have you fallen again already?’ the father exclaims. ‘O Sancta Maria, what do I hear—with a different man now! How long is this to go on, and aren't you ashamed!’ ‘Ah, mon père,’ the sinner replies, all penitential tears, ‘ça lui fait tant de plaisir et à moi si peu de peine![^b11-c09-n003]’ Now imagine such an answer! Even I gave way then: it's the cry of nature itself; if you like, it's better than innocence! I absolved her sin on the spot and turned to leave, but immediately had to return: I heard the father arranging to meet her that evening through the little opening. And the old man was flint, yet there he'd fallen in an instant! Nature, nature's truth, had its way! What, turning up your nose again, angry again? I don't know how to please you...”

<!-- B11-C09-P084 -->
“Leave me alone. You pound in my brain like an inescapable nightmare,” Ivan groaned painfully, powerless before his vision. “You bore me unbearably, agonizingly! I'd give a great deal if I could drive you away!”

<!-- B11-C09-P085 -->
“I repeat, lower your demands; don't demand ‘everything grand and beautiful’ from me, and you'll see how amicably we'll get along,” the gentleman said earnestly. “Truly, you're angry because I didn't appear to you in a red radiance, ‘thundering and flashing,’ with scorched wings, but presented myself in such a modest form. First your aesthetic feelings are offended, and second your pride: how could such a vulgar devil come to so great a man? No, you do have that romantic streak Belinsky mocked so long ago. What can one do? A young man. Before coming, I thought of appearing for a joke as a retired actual state councilor who'd served in the Caucasus, with the star of the Lion and Sun on my tailcoat, but I was positively afraid, because you'd have beaten me simply for daring to fasten the Lion and Sun to my tailcoat instead of at least the Pole Star or Sirius. And you keep saying I'm stupid. But my God, I don't claim to match your intelligence. Mephistopheles, appearing to Faust, testified that he wills evil and does only good. Well, that's as he pleases; with me it's precisely the reverse. Perhaps I'm the only person in all nature who loves truth and sincerely wishes for good. I was there when the Word, who had died on the cross, ascended to heaven bearing on his breast the soul of the thief crucified on his right. I heard the joyous shrieks of the cherubim singing and crying, ‘Hosanna,’ and the thunderous cry of delight from the seraphim that shook heaven and the whole universe. And then, I swear by everything holy, I wanted to join the choir and shout with them all: ‘Hosanna!’ It was already on my lips, already bursting from my breast... You know how sensitive I am, how receptive to art. But common sense—oh, the most unfortunate quality of my nature—kept me within proper bounds even then, and I let the moment pass! For what, I thought at that very instant, what would happen after my ‘hosanna’? Everything in the world would immediately go out, and no events would happen anymore. And so, solely from professional duty and my social position, I had to stifle that good moment in myself and stick to dirty tricks. Someone else takes all the credit for good, and only dirty tricks are allotted to me. But I don't envy the honor of sponging off someone else; I'm not ambitious. Why, of all beings in the world, am I alone doomed to curses from every respectable person, and even kicks from their boots, since in taking flesh I must sometimes accept those consequences too? I know there's a secret here, but they absolutely refuse to reveal it to me, because perhaps, once I understood what it was all about, I'd roar ‘hosanna,’ the necessary minus would immediately disappear, and good sense would begin throughout the world—and with it, naturally, the end of everything, even newspapers and magazines, because who would subscribe to them then? I know I'll be reconciled in the end, I'll finish my quadrillion too and learn the secret. But until that happens, I sulk and reluctantly fulfill my assignment: to destroy thousands so that one may be saved. How many souls, for instance, had to be destroyed, how many honorable reputations disgraced, to obtain just one righteous Job, over whom they caught me out so cruelly in those days! No, until the secret is revealed, two truths exist for me: one from over there, theirs, still quite unknown to me, and the other mine. And it's not yet known which will prove the better... Have you fallen asleep?”

<!-- B11-C09-P086 -->
“No wonder,” Ivan groaned angrily. “Everything stupid in my nature, long since lived through, ground over in my mind, thrown away like carrion—you serve it back to me as some sort of novelty!”

<!-- B11-C09-P087 -->
“Missed the mark again! And I thought I'd charm you with the literary presentation, even. That ‘hosanna’ in heaven—I really didn't do badly there, did I? Then immediately the sarcastic tone à la Heine, eh, don't you agree?”

<!-- B11-C09-P088 -->
“No, I was never such a lackey! How could my soul have produced a lackey like you?”

<!-- B11-C09-P089 -->
“My friend, I know a most charming and delightful young Russian gentleman: a young thinker and a great lover of literature and beautiful things, the author of a promising poem entitled ‘The Grand Inquisitor’... He was the only person I had in mind!”

<!-- B11-C09-P090 -->
“I forbid you to speak of ‘The Grand Inquisitor,’” Ivan exclaimed, blushing all over with shame.

<!-- B11-C09-P091 -->
“Well, what about ‘The Geological Upheaval’? Remember? Now there's a little poem!”

<!-- B11-C09-P092 -->
“Be quiet or I'll kill you!”

<!-- B11-C09-P093 -->
“Kill me? No, excuse me, I'll say it. I came precisely to treat myself to this pleasure. Oh, I love the dreams of my ardent young friends, trembling with thirst for life! ‘There are new people there,’ you decided last spring, as you prepared to come here. ‘They intend to destroy everything and begin with anthropophagy. Fools, they didn't ask me! In my opinion there's no need to destroy anything; all that's necessary is to destroy the idea of God in humanity—that's where to begin! There, there is where we must start—oh, blind men who understand nothing! Once humanity renounces God to a man—and I believe that this period, a parallel to geological periods, will come about—then of itself, without anthropophagy, the whole former worldview, and above all the whole former morality, will collapse, and everything new will begin. People will unite to take from life all it can give, but solely for happiness and joy in this world here. Man will exalt himself with the spirit of divine, titanic pride, and the man-god will appear. Hour by hour, conquering nature without limit through his will and science, man will thereby experience each hour a delight so lofty that it will replace all his former hopes of heavenly delights. Everyone will know that he is wholly mortal, without resurrection, and will accept death proudly and calmly, like a god. From pride he will understand that he has no reason to complain because life is but a moment, and will love his brother without any reward. Love will satisfy only life's moment, but the mere awareness of its brevity will intensify its fire as much as it once dissipated itself in hopes of an infinite love beyond the grave’... And so on, and so forth, in the same vein. Delightful!” Ivan sat with his hands pressed over his ears and his eyes on the floor, but his whole body began trembling. The voice continued:

“The question now, my young thinker thought, is whether such a period can ever arrive or not. If it arrives, everything is settled, and humanity will establish itself once and for all. But since, given humanity's inveterate stupidity, this may not come about for another thousand years, anyone who already recognizes the truth is permitted to arrange his life entirely as he pleases, on the new principles. In this sense ‘everything is permitted’ to him. More than that: even if this period never arrives, since there is no God or immortality all the same, the new man is permitted to become a man-god, even if he is the only one in the whole world, and certainly, in his new rank, to step lightheartedly over every former moral barrier of the former slave-man, should that prove necessary. For God there is no law! Wherever God stands, there is already God's place! Wherever I stand will immediately be the foremost place... ‘everything is permitted,’ and that's that! But that's our contemporary little Russian man: he won't even dare cheat without sanction, so dearly has he come to love truth...”

<!-- B11-C09-P095 -->
The visitor spoke, evidently carried away by his own eloquence, raising his voice more and more and glancing mockingly at his host; but he was not allowed to finish. Ivan suddenly seized a glass from the table and flung it at the orator.

“Ah, mais c'est bête enfin! Oh, but this is absurd!” the other exclaimed, jumping up from the sofa and flicking drops of tea off himself with his fingers. “Remembered Luther's inkwell![^b11-c09-n004] He considers me a dream himself and throws glasses at a dream! Suddenly a firm, insistent knocking sounded on the window frame from the yard. Ivan Fyodorovich leaped up from the sofa.

<!-- B11-C09-P099 -->
