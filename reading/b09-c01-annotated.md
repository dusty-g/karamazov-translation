# Book IX: The Preliminary Investigation
## I. The Beginning of the Official Perkhotin’s Career

<!-- B09-C01-P001 -->
Pyotr Ilyich Perkhotin, whom we left hammering with all his strength at the merchant woman Morozova’s stout, locked gates, did, of course, finally make himself heard. At this furious pounding, Fenya, who had been so frightened two hours earlier and was still too agitated and full of “thoughts” to go to bed, was once again frightened almost into hysterics: she imagined that Dmitri Fyodorovich had come knocking again (although she herself had seen him drive away), because no one else could knock so “brazenly.” She rushed to the yard porter, who had woken and was already going to answer the gate, and began begging him not to let the visitor in. But the porter questioned the man outside and, on learning who he was and that he wanted to see Fedosya Markovna on a matter of great importance, at last decided to unlock the gate. Pyotr Ilyich found Fedosya Markovna in the same kitchen; “for reassurance,” she begged him to let the porter come in too. He began questioning her and immediately hit upon the essential point: when Dmitri Fyodorovich ran off to look for Grushenka, he had taken the pestle from the mortar, and had come back without the pestle but with blood on his hands. “And the blood was still dripping, dripping and dripping off them!” Fenya exclaimed, plainly having invented this terrible detail in her own overwrought imagination. But Pyotr Ilyich himself had seen the bloody hands, although they were not dripping, and had helped wash them; nor was the question how quickly they had dried, but precisely where Dmitri Fyodorovich had run with the pestle—whether it was definitely to Fyodor Pavlovich’s, and what grounds there were for being so certain. Pyotr Ilyich pressed this point at length, and although he learned nothing definite, he nevertheless came away almost convinced that Dmitri Fyodorovich could have gone nowhere but his father’s house, and that something must therefore have happened there. “And when he came back,” Fenya added in agitation, “and I’d told him everything, I started asking him: ‘Why are both your hands bloody, dear Dmitri Fyodorovich?’” And he had supposedly answered her outright that it was human blood and that he had just killed a man. “He admitted it outright, he confessed everything to me there and then, and suddenly ran out like a madman. I sat down and began wondering: where’s he run off to now, like a madman? He’ll go to Mokroye, I thought, and kill the mistress there. So I ran out to beg him not to kill the mistress, went to his lodgings, but when I looked by Plotnikov’s shop I saw he was just driving off and there wasn’t any blood on his hands anymore.” (Fenya had noticed this and remembered it.) The old woman, Fenya’s grandmother, confirmed her granddaughter’s account as far as she could. After a few more questions Pyotr Ilyich left the house still more agitated and anxious than when he had entered it.

The most direct and simplest course now, it would seem, was to go to Fyodor Pavlovich’s house and find out whether anything had happened there, and if so, what; only then, once he knew beyond dispute, should he go to the district police chief, as he had firmly resolved to do. But the night was dark, Fyodor Pavlovich’s gates were stout, there would be more knocking, and he knew Fyodor Pavlovich only slightly. Suppose he finally made himself heard, they opened the gate, and nothing had happened at all? Tomorrow the mocking Fyodor Pavlovich would go about town telling the story of how an official unknown to him, Perkhotin, had come battering at his gate at midnight to find out whether someone had killed him. A scandal! And Pyotr Ilyich feared a scandal more than anything in the world. Yet the feeling driving him was so strong that, stamping angrily and cursing himself again, he at once set off in a new direction, this time not toward Fyodor Pavlovich’s but toward Madame Khokhlakov’s. He would ask her, he thought, whether she had given Dmitri Fyodorovich three thousand earlier that evening, at such-and-such a time: if she said no, he would go straight to the district police chief without calling at Fyodor Pavlovich’s; if she said yes, he would put everything off until tomorrow and go home. Of course, it immediately occurs to one that this young man’s decision to call at nearly eleven at night on a lady in society whom he did not know at all, perhaps get her out of bed, and ask her a question astonishing in such circumstances, might offer considerably more scope for a scandal than a visit to Fyodor Pavlovich. But such things sometimes happen, particularly in situations like this, with the decisions of even the most punctilious and phlegmatic people. And at that moment Pyotr Ilyich was anything but phlegmatic! All his life afterward he remembered how the irresistible anxiety that had gradually taken hold of him finally became torment, driving him on even against his will. Of course, he kept abusing himself all the way for going to see this lady, but “I’ll see it through, I’ll see it through to the end!” he repeated for the tenth time through clenched teeth, and he carried out his intention: he saw it through. It was exactly eleven when he entered Madame Khokhlakov’s house. They admitted him to the courtyard quite quickly, but when he asked whether the mistress was asleep or had not yet gone to bed, the porter could give no definite answer beyond saying that this was her usual bedtime. “Have yourself announced upstairs. If she wants to see you, she’ll see you; if she doesn’t, she won’t.” Pyotr Ilyich went upstairs, but there things became more difficult. The footman refused to announce him and at last called a maid. Politely but insistently, Pyotr Ilyich asked her to tell her mistress that a local official, Perkhotin, had come on a special matter, and that if the matter were not so important he would never have ventured to call. “Tell her in precisely, precisely those words,” he asked the girl. She went away. He remained waiting in the entrance hall. Madame Khokhlakov was not yet asleep, but she was already in her bedroom. She had been upset ever since Mitya’s earlier visit and already foresaw that she would not escape the migraine that usually came over her at night on such occasions. Surprised by the maid’s announcement, she nevertheless irritably told her to send him away, although this unexpected visit at such an hour from an unknown “local official” had intensely aroused her feminine curiosity. But this time Pyotr Ilyich was as stubborn as a mule: when told of her refusal he insisted most urgently that the maid announce him again, saying “in these very words” that he had come “on a matter of extraordinary importance, and she might herself regret it afterward if she would not receive him now.” “It was as though I were hurtling downhill,” he said later. The maid looked him over in surprise and went to announce him a second time. Madame Khokhlakov was struck, considered, asked what he looked like, and learned that he was “very well dressed, ma’am, young, and so polite.” Let us note in passing, parenthetically, that Pyotr Ilyich was a decidedly handsome young man, and knew it. Madame Khokhlakov decided to come out. She was already in her dressing gown and slippers, but threw a black shawl over her shoulders. The “official” was invited into the drawing room, the very room in which Mitya had been received earlier. His hostess came in looking sternly inquisitive and, without asking him to sit, began directly with the question: “What do you want?”

“I’ve ventured to disturb you, madam, concerning our mutual acquaintance Dmitri Fyodorovich Karamazov,” Perkhotin began, but he had scarcely spoken the name when his hostess’s face registered the most violent irritation. Almost shrieking, she interrupted him furiously.

<!-- B09-C01-P004 -->
“How long, how long are people going to torment me with that dreadful man?” she cried in a frenzy. “How dare you, sir, how could you venture to disturb a lady you don’t know, in her own house and at such an hour... and come here to speak to her about a man who came to murder me here, in this very drawing room, only three hours ago, stamped his feet, and left as no one leaves a respectable house! Let me tell you, sir, I shall lodge a complaint against you, I shall not let this pass. Kindly leave me this instant... I’m a mother, I shall immediately... I... I...”

<!-- B09-C01-P005 -->
“Murder you! Then he meant to murder you too?”

<!-- B09-C01-P006 -->
“Has he murdered someone already?” Madame Khokhlakov asked quickly.

<!-- B09-C01-P007 -->
“Kindly hear me out, madam, for just half a minute, and I shall explain everything in a few words,” Perkhotin answered firmly. “Today at five in the afternoon Mr. Karamazov borrowed ten rubles from me as a friend, and I know positively that he had no money; yet at nine this evening he came to my rooms carrying a bundle of hundred-ruble notes openly in his hands, amounting to roughly two or even three thousand rubles. His hands and face were covered in blood, and he seemed almost out of his mind. When I asked where he had got so much money, he told me explicitly that he had just received it from you, and that you had lent him three thousand supposedly to go to the goldfields...”

<!-- B09-C01-P008 -->
An expression of extraordinary, painful agitation suddenly appeared on Madame Khokhlakov’s face.

<!-- B09-C01-P009 -->
“God! He’s murdered his old father!” she cried, throwing up her hands. “I gave him no money, none! Oh, run, run!.. Don’t say another word! Save the old man, run to his father, run!”

<!-- B09-C01-P010 -->
“One moment, madam. Then you gave him no money? You remember definitely that you gave him no sum at all?”

<!-- B09-C01-P011 -->
“I didn’t, I didn’t! I refused him, because he didn’t know how to appreciate it. He left in a fury and stamped his feet. He rushed at me, and I jumped back... And I’ll tell you something else, since I no longer intend to conceal anything from you: he even spat at me, can you imagine? But why are we standing here? Oh, sit down... Forgive me, I... Or better, run, run, you must run and save the poor old man from a terrible death!”

<!-- B09-C01-P012 -->
“But what if he’s already killed him?”

<!-- B09-C01-P013 -->
“Oh, my God, of course! Then what are we to do now? What do you think we ought to do?”

<!-- B09-C01-P014 -->
Meanwhile she had seated Pyotr Ilyich and sat down opposite him. Briefly but quite clearly, he set out the history of the affair, or at least the part he himself had witnessed that day; he also told her of his visit to Fenya just now, and reported the news about the pestle. These details shook the excited lady to the utmost; she kept crying out and covering her eyes with her hands...

<!-- B09-C01-P015 -->
“Imagine, I foresaw it all! I have that gift: whatever I imagine comes to pass. And how many, how many times I’ve looked at that dreadful man and always thought: there’s a man who’ll end by murdering me. And that’s just what happened... That is, if he’s killed only his father now instead of me, it must be because the finger of God was plainly there, protecting me; and besides, he himself was ashamed to kill me because I had put a little icon with a relic of the Great Martyr Barbara around his neck myself, here, on this very spot... And how close to death I was at that moment! I went right up to him, right against him, and he stretched out his whole neck to me! You know, Pyotr Ilyich—forgive me, I think you said your name was Pyotr Ilyich—you know, I don’t believe in miracles, but that little icon and this obvious miracle that’s just happened to me—it shakes me, and I’m beginning to believe in absolutely anything again. Have you heard about the elder Zosima?.. But I don’t know what I’m saying... And imagine, even with that icon around his neck he spat at me... Of course, he only spat, he didn’t kill me, and... and off he rushed! But where are we to go, where should we go now, what do you think?”

<!-- B09-C01-P016 -->
Pyotr Ilyich rose and announced that he would now go straight to the district police chief and tell him everything; it would then be for the chief to decide.

<!-- B09-C01-P017 -->
“Oh, he’s an excellent, excellent man, I know Mikhail Makarovich. Certainly, go straight to him. How resourceful you are, Pyotr Ilyich, and how well you’ve thought it all out; you know, in your place I never would have thought of that!”

<!-- B09-C01-P018 -->
“All the more so since I know the district police chief well myself,” Pyotr Ilyich remarked, still standing and plainly anxious to escape as quickly as possible from this impetuous lady, who would not let him say goodbye and go.

<!-- B09-C01-P019 -->
“And you know, you know,” she babbled, “come and tell me what you see and find out there... and what comes to light... and what they decide about him and where they sentence him to. Tell me, we don’t have the death penalty, do we? But you absolutely must come, even if it’s three in the morning, or four, even half past four... Tell them to wake me, shake me if I won’t get up... Oh, God, but I shan’t even go to sleep. You know, perhaps I should come with you myself?..”

<!-- B09-C01-P020 -->
“N-no, madam, but if you were to write three lines in your own hand now, just in case, stating that you gave Dmitri Fyodorovich no money at all, it might be useful... just in case...”

<!-- B09-C01-P021 -->
“Certainly!” Madame Khokhlakov sprang enthusiastically to her writing desk. “And you know, you amaze me, you simply astound me with your resourcefulness and your skill in these matters... Are you in the service here? How very pleasant to hear that you’re in the service here...”

<!-- B09-C01-P022 -->
Still speaking, she quickly wrote the following three lines in a large hand on half a sheet of notepaper:

<!-- B09-C01-P023 -->
“Never in my life have I lent the unfortunate Dmitri Fyodorovich Karamazov (since he is unfortunate now, whatever else he may be) three thousand rubles today, nor any other money ever, ever! I swear to this by everything holy in our world. Khokhlakov.”

<!-- B09-C01-P024 -->
“Here is the note!” She turned quickly to Pyotr Ilyich. “Go now, save them. This is a great deed you’re doing.”

<!-- B09-C01-P025 -->
And she made the sign of the cross over him three times. She even ran after him as far as the entrance hall to see him off.

<!-- B09-C01-P026 -->
“How grateful I am to you! You won’t believe how grateful I am now that you came to me first. How is it we’ve never met? I should feel very honored to receive you in my home again. And how pleasant to hear that you’re in the service here... and with such precision, such resourcefulness... But they must appreciate you, they must finally understand your worth, and anything I could do for you, believe me... Oh, I love the young so much! I’m in love with the young. Young people are the foundation of our whole suffering Russia today, all her hope... Oh, go, go!..”

<!-- B09-C01-P027 -->
But Pyotr Ilyich had already run out, or she would not have let him go so soon. Madame Khokhlakov had, however, made quite a pleasant impression on him, even somewhat relieving his anxiety at having become involved in such an unpleasant affair. Tastes differ enormously, as everyone knows. “And she isn’t so old at all,” he thought with pleasure. “On the contrary, I’d have taken her for her daughter.”

<!-- B09-C01-P028 -->
As for Madame Khokhlakov herself, she was simply charmed by the young man. “So much ability, such attention to detail, in so young a man nowadays, and all of it combined with those manners and those looks. People say the young men of today can’t do anything—well, here’s an example for them,” and so on and so forth. So that she actually quite forgot the “dreadful occurrence,” and only when she was getting into bed and suddenly remembered again “how close to death she had been” did she say: “Oh, it’s dreadful, dreadful!” But at once she fell into the soundest, sweetest sleep. I would not, however, have dwelt on such trivial, incidental details if this eccentric meeting I have just described, between the young official and the widow who was by no means old, had not afterward laid the foundation for the entire career of this precise and meticulous young man—a circumstance still recalled with amazement in our little town, and about which we too may perhaps say a special word when we bring our long story of the brothers Karamazov to its close.
