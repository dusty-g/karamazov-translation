# Book XII: A Judicial Error

## XI. There Was No Money. There Was No Robbery

<!-- B12-C11-P001 -->
One point in the defense counsel’s speech struck everyone: his outright denial that those fatal three thousand rubles had existed at all, and therefore that they could have been stolen.

<!-- B12-C11-P002 -->
“Gentlemen of the jury,” the defense counsel began, “one most distinctive feature of the present case strikes anyone approaching it with fresh, unprejudiced eyes: an accusation of robbery alongside a complete inability to establish as a fact just what was stolen. Money was stolen, they say, precisely three thousand rubles—but whether it really existed, no one knows. Consider: first, how did we learn that there were three thousand, and who saw them? Only the servant Smerdyakov saw them and stated that they had been placed in a packet bearing an inscription. It was also he who, before the catastrophe, gave this information to the defendant and his brother Ivan Fyodorovich. Madame Svetlova was informed as well. Yet none of these three people saw the money themselves; once again, only Smerdyakov saw it. But here the question arises of itself: if it is true that the money existed and Smerdyakov saw it, when did he last see it? What if his master took the money from under the bedding and put it back in his cashbox without telling him? Notice that, according to Smerdyakov, the money lay under the bedding, under the mattress; the defendant must have pulled it out from under the mattress, yet the bed was not disturbed in the slightest, a fact carefully entered in the record. How could the defendant have disturbed absolutely nothing in the bed and, moreover, with bloodstained hands, have failed to soil the freshly laid fine bed linen, put on expressly for this occasion? But we shall be asked: what about the packet on the floor? That packet is precisely what we ought to discuss. Earlier I was even somewhat surprised: when the highly talented prosecutor began speaking of this packet, he suddenly himself—do you hear, gentlemen, himself—made this statement about it in his speech, at the very point where he was showing the absurdity of the supposition that Smerdyakov had killed the man: ‘Had there been no packet, had it not remained on the floor as evidence, had the robber taken it with him, no one in the whole world would have known that there was a packet with money in it, and that the money had therefore been stolen by the defendant.’ Thus it was solely this torn scrap of paper with its inscription, even by the prosecutor’s own admission, that led to the charge of robbery against the defendant: ‘Otherwise no one would have known that there had been a robbery, or perhaps even that there had been any money.’ But is the mere fact that this scrap lay on the floor proof that it contained money and that the money was stolen? ‘But,’ they answer, ‘Smerdyakov saw it in the packet.’ When, when did he last see it? That is what I am asking. I spoke with Smerdyakov, and he told me he had seen it two days before the catastrophe! Why, then, may I not suppose, for example, some such circumstance as this: old Fyodor Pavlovich, shut up at home, impatiently and hysterically awaiting his beloved, suddenly takes it into his head, for lack of anything better to do, to get out the packet and unseal it. ‘What use is a packet,’ he thinks, ‘she might not even believe me, but if I show her thirty rainbow-colored notes in one bundle, that ought to have more effect, make her mouth water.’ And so he tears the envelope, takes out the money, and tosses the envelope onto the floor with the imperious hand of a master, certainly with no fear of incriminating evidence. Listen, gentlemen of the jury, could any supposition, any occurrence, be more possible? Why is it impossible? But if anything at all of this kind could have happened, the charge of robbery collapses of itself: there was no money, and therefore no robbery. If the packet’s lying on the floor is evidence that it contained money, why may I not argue the reverse: that the packet lay on the floor precisely because it no longer contained any money, its owner having already taken it out? ‘Yes, but where did the money go in that case, if Fyodor Pavlovich himself removed it from the packet? None was found when his house was searched.’ First, some money was found in his cashbox, and second, he might have taken it out that morning, even the previous day, disposed of it otherwise, paid it out, sent it away, or finally changed his idea, his plan of action, completely, without even considering it necessary to report this to Smerdyakov beforehand. And if there exists even the mere possibility of such a supposition, how can we so insistently and firmly accuse the defendant of having committed murder for robbery, and assert that a robbery actually took place? In this way we enter the realm of novels. If you maintain that a particular thing was stolen, you must identify that thing, or at least prove beyond question that it existed. Yet no one even saw it. Recently in Petersburg a young man, almost a boy, eighteen years old, a petty peddler with a tray, entered a money changer’s shop in broad daylight with an ax and, with extraordinary, characteristic audacity, killed the owner and carried off fifteen hundred rubles. Some five hours later he was arrested, and the whole fifteen hundred, apart from fifteen rubles he had already managed to spend, was found on him. Moreover, the clerk, who returned to the shop after the murder, informed the police not only of the sum stolen but of exactly what it consisted: how many rainbow-colored notes, how many blue, how many red, how many gold coins and of what kind; and precisely those notes and coins were found on the arrested murderer. In addition to all this came the murderer’s full and frank confession that he had killed and carried off that very money. That, gentlemen of the jury, is what I call evidence! There I know, see, and can touch the money, and cannot say that it does not or did not exist. Is that the situation in the present case? Yet we are dealing with life and death, with a human being’s fate. ‘True,’ they will say, ‘but he caroused that same night, flung money about, fifteen hundred rubles were found on him—where did he get them?’ But precisely because only fifteen hundred were found, while the other half of the sum could not be found or traced anywhere, that very fact proves that this could have been entirely different money, which had never been in any packet at all. By a calculation of the time—and a most exact one—the preliminary investigation established and proved that, having run from the serving women to the official Perkhotin, the defendant did not go home or anywhere else, and thereafter was among people the entire time; consequently, he could not have separated half of three thousand and hidden it anywhere in town. It was precisely this consideration that prompted the prosecutor’s supposition that the money was hidden somewhere in a crevice in the village of Mokroye. Perhaps in the cellars of the Castle of Udolpho,[^b12-c11-n001] gentlemen? Is this not a fanciful supposition, worthy of a novel? And notice: destroy this single supposition, that the money is hidden in Mokroye, and the entire charge of robbery vanishes into thin air, for where, where then did those fifteen hundred rubles go? By what miracle could they have disappeared, if it is proved that the defendant went nowhere? And with novels like these we are prepared to destroy a human life! They will say: ‘All the same, he could not explain where he obtained those fifteen hundred rubles found on him; besides, everyone knew he had no money before that night.’ Who knew it? The defendant gave clear, firm testimony as to where he had obtained the money, and if you will, gentlemen of the jury, if you will, nothing has ever been or could be more plausible than that testimony, or more consistent with the defendant’s character and soul. The prosecution took a liking to its own novel: a weak-willed man who had decided to accept the three thousand rubles so shamefully offered by his fiancée could not, we are told, have set aside half and sewn it into a pouch. On the contrary, even had he sewn it up, he would have unpicked it every two days and chipped away at it a hundred at a time, thus spending the whole amount in a month. Remember, all this was presented in a tone that brooked no objection. But what if it happened quite otherwise, what if you have created a novel with an entirely different person in it? That is precisely the point: you have created a different person! Perhaps it will be objected: ‘There are witnesses that in the village of Mokroye, a month before the catastrophe, he squandered all three thousand rubles taken from Madame Verkhovtsev at once, down to the last kopeck, and therefore could not have set aside half.’ But who are these witnesses? The degree of their reliability has already emerged in court. Besides, the slice in another person’s hand always looks larger. Finally, none of these witnesses counted the money himself; each merely judged by eye. The witness Maximov, after all, testified that the defendant had twenty thousand in his hands. You see, gentlemen of the jury, since psychology has two ends, allow me to apply the other end here too, and we shall see whether the result is the same.

“A month before the catastrophe, Madame Verkhovtsev entrusted the defendant with three thousand rubles to send by post. But the question is: is it true that they were entrusted to him with such shame and humiliation as was proclaimed here earlier? In Madame Verkhovtsev’s first testimony on the same subject, it came out otherwise, entirely otherwise; in the second we heard only cries of bitterness, vengeance, cries of a hatred long concealed. But the mere fact that the witness once testified falsely, in her first statement, entitles us to conclude that her second statement could also have been false. The prosecutor ‘does not wish, does not dare’—his words—to touch this romance. Very well, I too shall leave it untouched; nevertheless, I shall permit myself to observe that if a pure and highly moral person, such as the highly respected Madame Verkhovtsev undoubtedly is—if such a person, I say, allows herself suddenly, at one stroke, to change her first testimony in court with the direct purpose of destroying the defendant, then it is also clear that her testimony was given neither impartially nor in cold blood. Are we to be denied the right to conclude that a woman taking revenge might have exaggerated a great deal? Yes, specifically exaggerated the shame and disgrace with which she offered the money. On the contrary, it was offered in such a way that it could still be accepted, particularly by so thoughtless a man as our defendant. Above all, he had in view at that time the imminent receipt from his father of those three thousand rubles which, by his reckoning, were owed him. This was thoughtless, but precisely because of his thoughtlessness he was firmly convinced that his father would hand them over, that he would receive them, and therefore could always send by post the money entrusted to him by Madame Verkhovtsev and settle his debt. But the prosecutor will not admit on any account that he could have set aside half the money he received that same day, on that very day, the day now at issue, and sewn it into a pouch: ‘That is not his character,’ he says, ‘he could not have such feelings.’ But you yourself proclaimed how broad Karamazov is, you yourself proclaimed those two extreme abysses that Karamazov can contemplate. Karamazov is precisely such a nature, with two sides, two abysses, that even in the most unrestrained urge to carouse he can stop if something strikes him from the other side. And that other side is love, precisely this new love which then flared up like gunpowder; and that love requires money, requires it more—oh, far more—than even a spree with that very beloved. She will say to him: ‘I’m yours, I don’t want Fyodor Pavlovich,’ and he will seize her and carry her away—provided he has something to carry her away on. That is more important than a spree. Would Karamazov fail to understand it? Why, it was precisely this anxiety that made him ill: what is unbelievable about his having set aside this money and hidden it against an emergency? But time passes, and Fyodor Pavlovich does not give the defendant the three thousand; on the contrary, word comes that he has set it aside precisely to lure away the defendant’s beloved. ‘If Fyodor Pavlovich doesn’t give it to me,’ he thinks, ‘I shall turn out to be a thief in Katerina Ivanovna’s eyes.’ And then the thought occurs to him that he will come and lay these very fifteen hundred rubles, which he continues to carry on him in this pouch, before Madame Verkhovtsev and say to her: ‘I’m a scoundrel, but not a thief.’ And so there is now a double reason to guard these fifteen hundred rubles like the apple of his eye, never to unpick the pouch and chip away at it a hundred rubles at a time. Why deny the defendant a sense of honor? No, he has a sense of honor; let us grant it is misguided, let us grant it is very often mistaken, but it exists, exists to the point of passion, and he has proved it. But now matters grow more complicated, the torments of jealousy reach their utmost pitch, and those same two questions, the same old questions, take shape ever more painfully in the defendant’s fevered brain: ‘If I give it back to Katerina Ivanovna, what will I have to take Grushenka away with?’ If he behaved so madly, got drunk and raged through the taverns throughout that month, perhaps it was precisely because he was bitterly miserable himself and could not bear it. At last those two questions became so acute that they drove him to despair. He sent his younger brother to his father to ask for the three thousand one last time, but without waiting for an answer burst in himself and ended by beating the old man before witnesses. After that there was no one left to get it from: the father he had beaten would not give it. That same evening he beats his breast, specifically the upper part of his breast where this pouch is, and swears to his brother that he has a means of not being a scoundrel, but that he will nevertheless remain a scoundrel because he foresees he will not use that means, will lack the strength of soul, lack the character. Why, why does the prosecution not believe Alexei Karamazov’s testimony, given so purely, so sincerely, without preparation and so plausibly? Why, on the contrary, does it compel me to believe in money in some crevice, in the cellars of the Castle of Udolpho? That same evening, after his conversation with his brother, the defendant writes this fatal letter, and this letter is the principal, the most stupendous evidence implicating the defendant in robbery! ‘I shall ask everyone, and if people won’t give it to me, I’ll kill my father and take it from under his mattress, in the packet tied with a pink ribbon, if only Ivan leaves’—a complete program for the murder, they say; how could it not be he? ‘It happened as written!’ the prosecution exclaims. But first, it is a drunken letter, written in a state of terrible anger; second, once again, he writes about the packet on Smerdyakov’s word, because he had never seen it himself; and third, written it may have been, but what proves that events followed what was written? Did the defendant take the packet from under the pillow, did he find the money, did it even exist? And was it for money that the defendant ran there? Remember, remember! He ran headlong, not to rob but merely to find out where she was, this woman who had shattered him. So he did not run according to a program, according to what was written—that is, for a premeditated robbery—but suddenly, unexpectedly, in a frenzy of jealousy! ‘Yes,’ they will say, ‘but all the same, once he arrived and killed him, he took the money too.’ But did he even kill him or not? I reject the accusation of robbery with indignation: one cannot accuse a man of robbery if one cannot specify exactly what was stolen; that is an axiom! But did he even kill him—without robbery, did he kill him? Has that been proved? Is that not a novel too?”

[^b12-c11-n001]: The castle is from Ann Radcliffe’s Gothic novel *The Mysteries of Udolpho* (1794); counsel mocks the prosecution’s hidden-money theory as fiction.
