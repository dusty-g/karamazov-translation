# Book V: Pro and Contra
## I. An Arrangement

<!-- B05-C01-P001 -->
Madame Khokhlakov was again the first to meet Alyosha. She was in a hurry: something serious had happened. Katerina Ivanovna's hysterics had ended in a faint, followed by “a terrible, dreadful weakness; she lay down, her eyes rolled back, and she began to ramble. Now she has a fever. We've sent for Herzenstube, we've sent for her aunts. The aunts are here already, but Herzenstube isn't. Everyone's sitting in her room, waiting. What's going to happen? She's unconscious. What if it's brain fever!”

<!-- B05-C01-P002 -->
As she cried all this out, Madame Khokhlakov looked seriously frightened. “This is serious now, serious!” she added to everything she said, as though nothing that had ever happened to her before had been serious. Alyosha listened sadly. He began telling her about his own adventures, but she cut him short almost at the first word: she had no time; she asked him to sit with Lise and wait for her there.

<!-- B05-C01-P003 -->
“Lise, my dear Alexei Fyodorovich,” she whispered almost into his ear, “Lise has just surprised me in the strangest way, but touched me too, and so my heart forgives her everything. Imagine, the moment you left, she suddenly began to feel genuinely sorry for supposedly laughing at you yesterday and today. But she wasn't laughing at you, she was only joking. Still, she was so seriously sorry, almost in tears, that I was astonished. She's never been seriously sorry before when she's laughed at me; it's always been a joke. And you know, she laughs at me every minute. But now she's serious, now everything has turned serious. She values your opinion enormously, Alexei Fyodorovich, and if you can, don't be offended with her or hold anything against her. I do nothing but spare her feelings myself, because she's such a clever girl—would you believe it? She was just saying you were her childhood friend—‘the most serious friend of my childhood.’ Imagine that, the most serious—and what about me? She has extraordinarily serious feelings about it, and even memories, and above all these phrases, these little expressions, the most unexpected expressions: you never expect them, and suddenly out one pops. Just recently, about a pine, for instance. There was a pine in our garden when she was very little; perhaps it's still there, so there's no reason to speak in the past tense. Pines aren't people, they stay the same a long time, Alexei Fyodorovich. ‘Mama,’ she says, ‘I remember that pine as though from a dream’—that is, ‘the pine from a dream’[^b05-c01-n001]—she put it differently somehow, because there's a muddle here; ‘pine’ is a silly word. Only she said something so original about it that I simply couldn't undertake to repeat it. Besides, I've forgotten it all. Well, goodbye, I'm terribly shaken and I'm sure I'm going out of my mind. Oh, Alexei Fyodorovich, twice in my life I've gone out of my mind, and they had to treat me. Go to Lise. Cheer her up, as you always know how to do so beautifully. Lise,” she called as she approached her door, “I've brought you Alexei Fyodorovich, whom you've so deeply offended, and he isn't the least bit angry, I assure you. On the contrary, he's astonished you could think such a thing!”

<!-- B05-C01-P004 -->
“Merci, maman. Come in, Alexei Fyodorovich.”

<!-- B05-C01-P005 -->
Alyosha went in. Lise looked embarrassed, then suddenly flushed all over. Clearly something was making her feel ashamed, and, as so often happens, she began talking very, very fast about something quite unrelated, as though this unrelated subject were the only thing that interested her at that moment.

<!-- B05-C01-P006 -->
“Mama has just suddenly told me the whole story, Alexei Fyodorovich, about those two hundred rubles and your errand… to that poor officer… and she told me the whole dreadful story of how he was insulted. You know, though Mama tells things very badly… she keeps jumping about… I listened and cried. Well, what happened? Did you give him the money? And how is the poor man now?…”

<!-- B05-C01-P007 -->
“That's just it, I didn't give it to him, and there's a whole story to it,” Alyosha replied, seeming for his part to be chiefly troubled by his failure to deliver the money. Lise, however, noticed perfectly well that he too was looking away and plainly trying to talk about something else. Alyosha sat down at the table and began his story, but almost at the first word he lost his embarrassment completely, and soon carried Lise along with him. He spoke with deep feeling, under the extraordinarily powerful impression of what had just happened, and managed to tell the story well and fully. Even before, back in Moscow when Lise was still a child, he had liked to visit her and tell her something that had just happened to him, or something he had read, or memories of his own childhood. Sometimes they even made things up together and invented whole stories between them, mostly cheerful, funny ones. Now they both seemed suddenly transported back to those Moscow days, two years before. Lise was deeply moved by his story. With heartfelt warmth, Alyosha brought “little Ilyusha” to life before her. When he had finished his detailed account of the unhappy man's trampling on the money, Lise threw up her hands and cried out, unable to contain herself:

<!-- B05-C01-P008 -->
“So you didn't give him the money? You just let him run away! My God, you should at least have run after him and caught him…”

<!-- B05-C01-P009 -->
“No, Lise, it's better this way, that I didn't run after him,” said Alyosha. He rose from his chair and walked anxiously about the room.

<!-- B05-C01-P010 -->
“How is it better? In what way? Now they've nothing to eat, they'll perish!”

<!-- B05-C01-P011 -->
“They won't perish, because those two hundred rubles will reach them all the same. He'll take them tomorrow anyway. Tomorrow he'll certainly take them,” Alyosha said, pacing thoughtfully. “You see, Lise,” he continued, suddenly stopping before her, “I made a mistake myself, but even that mistake turned out for the best.”

<!-- B05-C01-P012 -->
“What mistake, and why for the best?”

<!-- B05-C01-P013 -->
“This is why. He's a timid man, with a weak character. He's suffered so much, and he's very kind. I keep thinking now: what suddenly hurt him so much that he trampled on the money? Because I assure you, right up to the very last moment he didn't know he would trample on it. And I think a number of things hurt him… It couldn't have been otherwise in his position… First, he was hurt by having shown too much delight in the money in front of me, by not having concealed it from me. If he'd been pleased, but not too pleased, if he hadn't shown it, if he'd put on airs, as other people do when they accept money, and made a show of it, then he could still have borne it and accepted. But he was too honestly delighted, and that's what hurt. Oh, Lise, he's an honest, kind man, and that's the whole trouble in cases like this! All the time he was talking his voice was so weak, so worn out, and he spoke so very, very fast, with a sort of giggle—or perhaps he was crying… really, he was crying, he was so delighted… and he talked about his daughters… and the job they'd give him in another town… And the moment he'd poured his heart out, he suddenly felt ashamed of having shown me his whole soul. At once he hated me. He's one of those desperately ashamed poor people. Above all, it hurt him that he'd taken me for a friend too quickly, had given in to me too quickly. One moment he was flying at me, trying to frighten me, and the next, as soon as he saw the money, he began embracing me. Because he did embrace me, kept touching me with his hands. That must have been exactly how all the humiliation struck him, and just then I made this mistake, a very serious one: I suddenly told him that if he didn't have enough money to move to another town, he'd be given more, and I'd even give him as much of my own money as he wanted. That suddenly hit him: why should I come thrusting myself forward to help him too? You know, Lise, it's terribly hard for someone who's been humiliated when everyone starts looking at him as his benefactor… I've heard that; the elder told me. I don't know how to express it, but I've often seen it myself. I feel exactly the same myself, for that matter. And the main thing is that although he didn't know until the very last moment that he would trample on the banknotes, he did have a presentiment of it, he certainly did. That was why his delight was so intense, because he had that presentiment… And though it's all so dreadful, it's still for the best. I even think it couldn't be better, it couldn't possibly be better…”

<!-- B05-C01-P014 -->
“Why? Why couldn't it possibly be better?” Lise exclaimed, looking at Alyosha in great surprise.

<!-- B05-C01-P015 -->
“Because, Lise, if he'd taken the money instead of trampling on it, an hour or so after he got home he'd have cried over his humiliation; that's certainly what would have happened. He'd have cried, and perhaps tomorrow he'd have come to me at first light, and perhaps thrown the banknotes down in front of me and trampled on them, as he did just now. But as it is, he went away terribly proud and triumphant, even though he knows he's ‘ruined himself.’ So now nothing could be easier than getting him to accept those same two hundred rubles as soon as tomorrow, because he's already proved his honor, thrown the money down and trampled on it… When he trampled on it, he couldn't have known I'd bring it to him again tomorrow. And yet he needs that money desperately. However proud he is now, even today he'll be thinking of the help he's thrown away. Tonight he'll think about it still more, he'll see it in his dreams, and by tomorrow morning he may be ready to come running to me and ask forgiveness. And then I'll turn up: ‘You've proved that you're a proud man. Now take it, and forgive us.’ And then he'll take it!”

<!-- B05-C01-P016 -->
Alyosha said “And then he'll take it!” with a kind of rapture. Lise clapped her hands.

<!-- B05-C01-P017 -->
“Oh, that's true! Oh, I've suddenly understood it all so well! Oh, Alyosha, how do you know all this? So young, and already you know what's in people's hearts… I could never have thought of it…”

<!-- B05-C01-P018 -->
“The main thing now is to convince him that he's on an equal standing with all of us, even though he's accepting money from us,” Alyosha continued in his rapture, “and not just an equal standing, but a higher standing…”

<!-- B05-C01-P019 -->
“‘A higher standing’—that's lovely, Alexei Fyodorovich. But go on, go on!”

<!-- B05-C01-P020 -->
“I mean, I put that badly… about a higher standing… but it doesn't matter, because…”

<!-- B05-C01-P021 -->
“Oh, it doesn't matter, it doesn't matter, of course it doesn't! Forgive me, Alyosha, darling… You know, until now I've hardly respected you at all… I mean, I did respect you, but on an equal standing, and now I'll respect you on a higher one… Darling, don't be angry with me for ‘being witty,’” she hastened to add with deep feeling. “I'm ridiculous and little, but you, you… Listen, Alexei Fyodorovich, in all this reasoning of ours, I mean yours… no, let's say ours… isn't there contempt for him, for that unhappy man… in the way we're picking his soul apart, as though looking down on him? Isn't there, in our deciding so confidently that he'll accept the money?”

<!-- B05-C01-P022 -->
“No, Lise, there isn't any contempt,” Alyosha replied firmly, as though already prepared for the question. “I thought about that myself on the way here. Think: how can there be contempt when we're just like him, when everyone's just like him? Because we are just like him, no better. And even if we were better, we'd still be just the same in his place… I don't know about you, Lise, but I think my own soul is petty in many ways. His isn't petty; on the contrary, it's very sensitive… No, Lise, there's no contempt for him here at all! You know, Lise, my elder once said that we must always look after people as we would children, and some as we would patients in a hospital…”

<!-- B05-C01-P023 -->
“Oh, Alexei Fyodorovich, darling, let's look after people as if they were ill!”

<!-- B05-C01-P024 -->
“Let's, Lise. I'm willing, only I'm not quite ready myself. Sometimes I'm very impatient, and at other times I fail to see what's needed. It's different with you.”

<!-- B05-C01-P025 -->
“Oh, I don't believe it! Alexei Fyodorovich, I'm so happy!”

<!-- B05-C01-P026 -->
“How good of you to say that, Lise.”

<!-- B05-C01-P027 -->
“Alexei Fyodorovich, you're wonderfully good, but sometimes you're a bit of a pedant… and then you look again, and you're no pedant at all. Go and look by the door, open it quietly and see if Mama's listening,” Lise suddenly whispered, in a hurried, nervous whisper.

<!-- B05-C01-P028 -->
Alyosha went, opened the door a crack, and reported that no one was listening.

<!-- B05-C01-P029 -->
“Come here, Alexei Fyodorovich,” Lise went on, blushing more and more. “Give me your hand, like that. Listen, I have a great confession to make: I didn't write you that letter yesterday as a joke. I meant it…”

<!-- B05-C01-P030 -->
She covered her eyes with her hand. It was clear how ashamed she felt making this confession. Suddenly she seized his hand and quickly kissed it three times.

<!-- B05-C01-P031 -->
“Oh, Lise, that's wonderful,” Alyosha exclaimed joyfully. “I was quite sure you meant it.”

<!-- B05-C01-P032 -->
“He was sure, imagine that!” Suddenly she moved his hand away, though she still held it in hers, blushing terribly and laughing a small, happy laugh. “I kissed his hand, and he says, ‘That's wonderful.’” But her reproach was unfair: Alyosha too was in great confusion.

<!-- B05-C01-P033 -->
“I'd like you always to like me, Lise, but I don't know how to manage it,” he stammered out, blushing too.

<!-- B05-C01-P034 -->
“Alyosha, darling, you're cold and impertinent. Just look at him! He's deigned to choose me for his wife, and now he's quite content! He was already sure I meant what I wrote—how do you like that! But it's impertinence, that's what it is!”

<!-- B05-C01-P035 -->
“Is it really bad that I was sure?” Alyosha suddenly laughed.

<!-- B05-C01-P036 -->
“Oh, Alyosha, quite the opposite, it's terribly good,” Lise said, looking at him tenderly and happily. Alyosha stood with his hand still in hers. Suddenly he bent down and kissed her right on the lips.

<!-- B05-C01-P037 -->
“What's this? What's come over you?” Lise cried. Alyosha was completely flustered.

<!-- B05-C01-P038 -->
“Well, forgive me if it was wrong… Perhaps I was terribly stupid… You said I was cold, so I went and kissed you… Only now I see it came out stupidly…”

<!-- B05-C01-P039 -->
Lise laughed and covered her face with her hands.

<!-- B05-C01-P040 -->
“And in those clothes!” she blurted out through her laughter, but suddenly she stopped laughing and became entirely serious, almost stern.

<!-- B05-C01-P041 -->
“Well, Alyosha, we'll wait a little before we kiss again, because neither of us knows how yet, and we still have a very long time to wait,” she suddenly concluded. “Tell me instead why you're taking me, such a fool, a sick little fool, when you're so clever, so thoughtful, so observant. Oh, Alyosha, I'm terribly happy, because I'm not worthy of you at all!”

<!-- B05-C01-P042 -->
“You are worthy, Lise. I'll be leaving the monastery for good in a few days. When you go out into the world, you must marry; that much I know. That's what he told me to do too. Who could I marry better than you… and who would marry me except you? I've already thought about it. First, you've known me since childhood, and second, you have so many abilities I don't have at all. Your soul is more cheerful than mine; above all, you're more innocent than I am, and I've already come into contact with so much, so very much… Oh, you don't know; I'm a Karamazov too! What does it matter that you laugh and joke, even at me? On the contrary, do laugh, it makes me so happy… But you laugh like a little girl, while in private you think like a martyr…”

<!-- B05-C01-P043 -->
“Like a martyr? What do you mean?”

<!-- B05-C01-P044 -->
“Yes, Lise, that question you asked just now: whether we weren't showing contempt for that unhappy man by dissecting his soul like that—that's a martyr's question… You see, I can't express it at all, but anyone who asks questions like that is capable of suffering too. Sitting in your chair, you must already have thought about so much…”

<!-- B05-C01-P045 -->
“Alyosha, give me your hand; why are you taking it away?” Lise said in a little voice that had grown faint and weak with happiness. “Listen, Alyosha, what will you wear when you leave the monastery? What kind of clothes? Don't laugh, don't be angry, it's very, very important to me.”

<!-- B05-C01-P046 -->
“I haven't thought about clothes yet, Lise, but I'll wear whatever you like.”

<!-- B05-C01-P047 -->
“I want you to have a dark blue velvet jacket, a white piqué waistcoat, and a soft, downy gray hat… Tell me, did you really believe me earlier when I said I didn't love you, when I disowned yesterday's letter?”

<!-- B05-C01-P048 -->
“No, I didn't.”

<!-- B05-C01-P049 -->
“Oh, you're impossible, incorrigible!”

<!-- B05-C01-P050 -->
“You see, I knew you… loved me, I think, but I pretended to believe you when you said you didn't, so it would be… easier for you…”

<!-- B05-C01-P051 -->
“Worse still! Worse and better than anything. Alyosha, I love you terribly. Just before you came, I set myself a test: I'll ask him for yesterday's letter, and if he calmly takes it out and gives it back to me—as one can always expect him to—then it means he doesn't love me at all, feels nothing, and is simply a stupid, worthless boy, and I'm lost. But you left the letter in your cell, and that encouraged me. Isn't it true you left it there because you had a feeling I'd demand it back, so you wouldn't have to give it to me? Is that it? Isn't it?”

<!-- B05-C01-P052 -->
“Oh, Lise, not at all. I've got the letter with me now, and I had it with me then, in this very pocket. Here it is.”

<!-- B05-C01-P053 -->
Laughing, Alyosha took out the letter and showed it to her from a distance.

<!-- B05-C01-P054 -->
“Only I won't give it back to you. You can look at it in my hands.”

<!-- B05-C01-P055 -->
“What? Then you lied before? You're a monk and you lied?”

<!-- B05-C01-P056 -->
“I suppose I did,” Alyosha laughed too. “I lied so I wouldn't have to give you the letter. It's very precious to me,” he suddenly added with deep feeling, blushing again. “This is forever now, and I'll never give it to anyone!”

<!-- B05-C01-P057 -->
Lise gazed at him in rapture.

<!-- B05-C01-P058 -->
“Alyosha,” she began falteringly again, “look by the door: is Mama listening?”

<!-- B05-C01-P059 -->
“All right, Lise, I'll look, but wouldn't it be better not to? Why suspect your mother of something so dishonorable?”

<!-- B05-C01-P060 -->
“What do you mean, dishonorable? What's dishonorable? If she listens to her daughter, that's her right, it isn't dishonorable,” Lise flared up. “You can be sure, Alexei Fyodorovich, that when I'm a mother myself and have a daughter like me, I'll certainly listen at her door.”

<!-- B05-C01-P061 -->
“Really, Lise? That's wrong.”

<!-- B05-C01-P062 -->
“Oh, my God, what's dishonorable about it? If it were some ordinary social conversation and I listened, that would be dishonorable. But here her own daughter's shut herself in with a young man… Listen, Alyosha, you should know I'll spy on you too as soon as we're married, and you should know I'll open all your letters and read everything… You've been warned.”

<!-- B05-C01-P063 -->
“Yes, of course, if that's how it is…” Alyosha murmured. “Only it's wrong…”

<!-- B05-C01-P064 -->
“Oh, such contempt! Alyosha, darling, let's not quarrel the very first time. I'd better tell you the whole truth: of course it's very wrong to listen at doors, and of course I'm wrong and you're right, but I'll do it all the same.”

<!-- B05-C01-P065 -->
“Go ahead. You won't find anything to spy on,” Alyosha laughed.

<!-- B05-C01-P066 -->
“Alyosha, will you obey me? We must settle that beforehand too.”

<!-- B05-C01-P067 -->
“Very gladly, Lise, certainly, except in the most important things. In the most important things, if you disagree with me, I'll still do what my duty tells me.”

<!-- B05-C01-P068 -->
“That's as it should be. Then you should know that I'm ready, on the contrary, to obey you not only in the most important things but to give way to you in everything, and I swear it to you now—in everything, for my whole life!” Lise cried passionately. “And happily, happily! More than that, I swear I'll never listen at your door, not once, never, and never read a single one of your letters, because you're right and I'm wrong. And though I'll want terribly to listen, I know I will, I still won't do it, because you think it's dishonorable. Now you're like my providence… Listen, Alexei Fyodorovich, why have you been so sad these last few days, yesterday and today? I know you have worries, troubles, but I can see that besides those there's some special sadness, perhaps a secret one?”

<!-- B05-C01-P069 -->
“Yes, Lise, there's a secret one too,” Alyosha said sadly. “I can see you love me, since you guessed that.”

<!-- B05-C01-P070 -->
“What sadness? What is it about? Can you tell me?” Lise asked with a timid plea.

<!-- B05-C01-P071 -->
“I'll tell you later, Lise… afterward…” Alyosha grew embarrassed. “You might not understand now. Perhaps I couldn't even explain it myself.”

<!-- B05-C01-P072 -->
“I know, besides, that your brothers torment you, your father?”

<!-- B05-C01-P073 -->
“Yes, my brothers too,” Alyosha said, as though lost in thought.

<!-- B05-C01-P074 -->
“I don't like your brother Ivan Fyodorovich, Alyosha,” Lise suddenly remarked.

<!-- B05-C01-P075 -->
Alyosha registered this with some surprise but did not take it up.

<!-- B05-C01-P076 -->
“My brothers are destroying themselves,” he continued, “and my father too. And they're destroying others along with them. It's that ‘earthbound Karamazov force,’ as Father Paisius called it the other day—earthbound and violent, raw… I don't even know whether the spirit of God moves above that force. I only know that I'm a Karamazov too… I'm a monk, a monk? Am I a monk, Lise? You said just a moment ago that I'm a monk, didn't you?”

<!-- B05-C01-P077 -->
“Yes, I did.”

<!-- B05-C01-P078 -->
“And perhaps I don't even believe in God.”

<!-- B05-C01-P079 -->
“You don't believe? What's come over you?” Lise said quietly and cautiously. But Alyosha made no reply. There was something very mysterious and very personal in these all-too-sudden words, perhaps something unclear even to himself, but already undoubtedly tormenting him.

<!-- B05-C01-P080 -->
“And now, on top of everything, my friend is leaving, the foremost man in the world is leaving this earth. If you knew, if you only knew, Lise, how bound I am to him, how our souls are joined! And now I'll be alone… I'll come to you, Lise… From now on we'll be together…”

<!-- B05-C01-P081 -->
“Yes, together, together! Always together from now on, for the rest of our lives. Listen, kiss me. I give you permission.”

<!-- B05-C01-P082 -->
Alyosha kissed her.

<!-- B05-C01-P083 -->
“Now go, and Christ be with you!” (She made the sign of the cross over him.) “Go quickly, while he's still alive. I see how cruelly I've kept you. I'll pray for him today, and for you. Alyosha, we'll be happy! We'll be happy, won't we?”

<!-- B05-C01-P084 -->
“I think we will, Lise.”

<!-- B05-C01-P085 -->
After leaving Lise, Alyosha decided not to call on Madame Khokhlakov and was about to leave the house without saying goodbye. But the moment he opened the door and stepped onto the stairs, there was Madame Khokhlakov herself, appearing from nowhere. At her first words Alyosha guessed that she had been waiting there for him on purpose.

<!-- B05-C01-P086 -->
“Alexei Fyodorovich, this is dreadful. It's childish foolishness, it's all nonsense. I hope you won't start imagining… Nonsense, nonsense, and nonsense!” she assailed him.

<!-- B05-C01-P087 -->
“Only don't say that to her,” said Alyosha, “or she'll get upset, and that would be bad for her now.”

<!-- B05-C01-P088 -->
“I hear a sensible word from a sensible young man. Am I to understand that you only agreed with her because, out of compassion for her illness, you didn't want to upset her by contradicting her?”

<!-- B05-C01-P089 -->
“Oh, no, not at all. I was entirely serious with her,” Alyosha declared firmly.

<!-- B05-C01-P090 -->
“Being serious is impossible here, unthinkable. First, I won't receive you at all now, not once; and second, I'll go away and take her with me. You should know that.”

<!-- B05-C01-P091 -->
“But why?” said Alyosha. “It's still so far off. We may have to wait another year and a half.”

<!-- B05-C01-P092 -->
“Oh, Alexei Fyodorovich, that's true, of course, and in a year and a half you'll quarrel and part a thousand times. But I'm so unhappy, so unhappy! It may all be nonsense, but it's struck me down. Now I'm like Famusov in the final scene, you're Chatsky, and she's Sophia,[^b05-c01-n002] and imagine, I deliberately ran out onto the stairs to meet you, and all the fateful things happened on the stairs there too. I heard everything. I could hardly stay on my feet. So that explains all the horrors of last night and all the hysterics earlier! Love for the daughter, death for the mother. Time to lie down in my coffin. Now, secondly, and most important: what is this letter she wrote you? Show it to me now, at once!”

<!-- B05-C01-P093 -->
“No, there's no need. Tell me, how is Katerina Ivanovna? I very much need to know.”

<!-- B05-C01-P094 -->
“Still lying there delirious; she hasn't come round. Her aunts are here, doing nothing but sighing and looking down their noses at me. Herzenstube came and was so frightened that I didn't know what to do with him or how to save him; I even thought of sending for a doctor. They took him away in my carriage. And then, to crown it all, you suddenly turn up with this letter. Though it's true none of this will happen for another year and a half. In the name of everything great and holy, in the name of your dying elder, show me that letter, Alexei Fyodorovich—me, her mother! If you like, hold it in your fingers and I'll read it in your hands.”

<!-- B05-C01-P095 -->
“No, I won't show it to you, Katerina Osipovna, even if she gives permission. I'll come tomorrow, and if you like, I'll discuss a great many things with you. But now, goodbye!”

<!-- B05-C01-P096 -->
And Alyosha ran down the stairs and out into the street.

[^b05-c01-n001]: The Russian plays on *sosna* (“pine”) and *so sna* (“from sleep”): nearly the same sounds with a different word break.

[^b05-c01-n002]: These characters are from Alexander Griboedov’s comedy *Woe from Wit* (1824), whose final confrontation also takes place on a staircase.
