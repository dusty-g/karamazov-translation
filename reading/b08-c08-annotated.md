# Book VIII: Mitya
## VIII. Delirium

<!-- B08-C08-P001 -->
What began was almost an orgy, a feast for the whole world. Grushenka was the first to call for wine. “I want to drink, I want to get thoroughly drunk, like before. Remember, Mitya, remember how we got to know each other here that time!” Mitya himself was in a kind of delirium and had a foretaste of “his happiness.” Grushenka, however, kept sending him away. “Go, enjoy yourself, tell them to dance, let everyone enjoy themselves, ‘set the house and stove a-dancing,’ like before, like before!” she kept exclaiming. She was terribly excited. And Mitya rushed off to arrange things. The chorus had assembled in the next room. The room where they had been sitting until now was cramped besides, divided in two by a calico curtain behind which stood yet another enormous bed with a plump feather mattress and a mound of pillows in the same calico. Indeed, there were beds everywhere, in all four of the house's “best” rooms. Grushenka settled in the doorway itself; Mitya brought her an armchair. She had sat exactly like this “that time,” on the day of their first spree here, watching the chorus and the dancing from this spot. The same girls had all gathered; the little Jews with their violins and zithers arrived too, and at last the long-awaited troika-load of wine and provisions arrived. Mitya bustled about. Outsiders came into the room to watch, peasants and their women who had already gone to bed but woke up and sniffed out a feast on an unheard-of scale, just like a month before. Mitya greeted and embraced people he knew, remembered faces, uncorked bottles and poured drinks for anyone and everyone. Only the girls were particularly eager for champagne; the men preferred rum and brandy, especially hot punch. Mitya ordered chocolate made for all the girls and three samovars kept boiling all night without a break for tea and punch for everyone who came: anyone who wanted could help himself. In short, something disorderly and absurd began, but Mitya seemed in his natural element, and the more absurd it all became, the more his spirits rose. Had any peasant asked him for money at that moment, he would immediately have pulled out his whole bundle and begun handing it out right and left without counting. That was probably why, to protect Mitya, the innkeeper Trifon Borisych bustled around him almost constantly. He seemed to have abandoned all thought of going to bed that night, but drank little himself—only one small glass of punch—and kept a sharp eye on Mitya's interests in his own fashion. At the necessary moments he would gently and obsequiously restrain and persuade him, preventing him from distributing “cigars and Rhine wine” to the peasants as he had “that time,” and, God forbid, money. He was very indignant that the girls were drinking liqueur and eating sweets. “Nothing but a lousy lot, Mitry Fyodorovich,” he said. “I'd give every one of them a kick with my knee and tell them to think themselves honored—that's what they're worth!” Mitya remembered Andrei again and ordered punch sent to him. “I hurt him earlier,” he repeated in a weakened, tender voice. Kalganov had not wanted to drink, and at first he had not liked the chorus of girls very much, but after another two glasses of champagne he became extremely merry, walked through the rooms laughing, and praised everything and everyone, the singing and the music. Maximov, blissful and tipsy, never left him. Grushenka, who was beginning to get tipsy too, pointed out Kalganov to Mitya. “What a darling he is, what a wonderful boy!” And Mitya ran in delight to kiss Kalganov and Maximov. Oh, he sensed much to come; she had said nothing of that kind to him yet and was even plainly holding back on purpose, only glancing at him now and then with tender but ardent eyes. At last she suddenly caught his hand tightly and pulled him toward her with force. She was sitting in the armchair by the doorway then.

“The way you came in earlier, eh? The way you came in! I was so frightened. How could you mean to give me up to him, eh? Did you really mean to?”

<!-- B08-C08-P003 -->
“I didn't want to ruin your happiness!” Mitya murmured blissfully to her. But she did not even need his answer.

<!-- B08-C08-P004 -->
“Now go... enjoy yourself,” she sent him away again. “And don't cry. I'll call you again.”

<!-- B08-C08-P005 -->
He would run away, and she would go back to listening to the songs and watching the dancing, following him with her eyes wherever he was; but a quarter of an hour later she would call him again, and again he would come running.

<!-- B08-C08-P006 -->
“Now sit beside me and tell me how you heard about me yesterday, that I'd come here. Who was the first you heard it from?”

<!-- B08-C08-P007 -->
Mitya began telling her everything, disjointedly, haphazardly, passionately, but strangely too, often suddenly frowning and breaking off.

<!-- B08-C08-P008 -->
“Why are you frowning?” she asked.

<!-- B08-C08-P009 -->
“Nothing... I left a sick man there. If only he'd recover, if I knew he'd recover, I'd give ten years of my life this instant!”

<!-- B08-C08-P010 -->
“Well, God be with him if he's sick. So you really meant to shoot yourself tomorrow, you fool—and what for? I love reckless men like you,” she murmured to him, her tongue a little heavy. “So you'd do anything for me? Would you? And did you really mean to shoot yourself tomorrow, you silly fool! No, wait for now. Perhaps tomorrow I'll say one little word to you... not today, tomorrow. Would you like it today? No, I don't want to today... Now go, go and enjoy yourself.”

<!-- B08-C08-P011 -->
Once, however, she called him over looking puzzled and concerned.

<!-- B08-C08-P012 -->
“Why are you sad? I can see you're sad... Oh yes, I can see,” she added, looking intently into his eyes. “You may be kissing the peasants and shouting over there, but I can see something. No, enjoy yourself. I'm happy, you be happy too... I love someone here—guess who? Oh, look, my boy's fallen asleep. He's tipsy, the poor dear.”

<!-- B08-C08-P013 -->
She meant Kalganov: he had indeed become tipsy and fallen asleep for a moment, sitting on the sofa. It was not the drink alone that had sent him to sleep; something had suddenly made him sad, or, as he put it, “bored.” By the end the girls' songs had greatly disheartened him too, gradually becoming, as the drinking went on, altogether too bawdy and unbridled. Their dancing too: two girls had dressed up as bears, and Stepanida, a lively girl with a stick in her hand, played the keeper and began to “show them.” “Livelier, Marya!” she shouted. “Or you'll get the stick!” Finally the bears tumbled to the floor in an altogether indecent manner, amid loud laughter from the motley crowd of women and men who had drunk far more than they could hold. “Well, let them, let them,” Grushenka said sententiously with a blissful face. “They get so few days to enjoy themselves—aren't people allowed to be happy?” But Kalganov watched as though he had been dirtied by something. “It's all swinish, all this folk culture,” he remarked, walking away. “These are their spring games, when they watch over the sun all through the summer night.” What he particularly disliked, however, was a “new” little song with a brisk dancing tune, about a gentleman riding by and questioning the girls:

> A gentleman asked the girls,  
> Will the girls love him or not?

But the girls thought they could not love a gentleman:

> The gentleman will beat me sore,  
> I'll love him nevermore.

Then a Gypsy rode by—the singers said *tsýgan*, stressing the first syllable instead of the usual *tsygán*—and he too:

> The gypsy asked the girls,  
> Will the girls love him or not?

<!-- B08-C08-P016 -->
But they could not love a gypsy either:

> The gypsy will be thieving,  
> And I will be grieving.

<!-- B08-C08-P017 -->
And many people rode by like this and questioned the girls, even a soldier:

> The soldier asked the girls,  
> Will the girls love him or not?

<!-- B08-C08-P018 -->
But they rejected the soldier with contempt:

> The soldier will carry his pack,  
> And I behind his back...

<!-- B08-C08-P019 -->
Here followed the most unprintable little line, sung quite openly, which created a sensation among the listeners. At last the matter ended with a merchant:

> The merchant asked the girls,  
> Will the girls love him or not?

<!-- B08-C08-P020 -->
And it turned out they loved him very much indeed, because, they said:

> The merchant will trade his wares,  
> And I will reign like a queen.

<!-- B08-C08-P021 -->
Kalganov even grew angry.

<!-- B08-C08-P022 -->
“That song was made up only yesterday,” he remarked aloud. “Who writes these things for them? All that's missing is for a railway man or a Jew to ride by and question the girls: they'd beat the lot.” And, almost offended, he announced there and then that he was bored, sat down on the sofa and suddenly dozed off. His pretty little face had grown a little pale and tilted back against the sofa cushion.

<!-- B08-C08-P023 -->
“Look how pretty he is,” Grushenka said, bringing Mitya over to him. “I combed his hair earlier. It's like flax, so thick...”

<!-- B08-C08-P024 -->
Bending over him tenderly, she kissed his forehead. Kalganov instantly opened his eyes, looked at her, half rose, and asked with the most concerned expression where Maximov was.

<!-- B08-C08-P025 -->
“So that's who he wants,” Grushenka laughed. “Sit with me for a minute. Mitya, run and fetch his Maximov.”

<!-- B08-C08-P026 -->
It turned out Maximov now never left the girls, only running off now and then to pour himself a little liqueur. He had also drunk two cups of chocolate. His little face was flushed, his nose purple, his eyes moist and sugary. He ran over and announced that he now wished to dance the sabotière “to a certain little tune.”

<!-- B08-C08-P027 -->
“When I was little they taught me all these well-bred society dances, sir...”

<!-- B08-C08-P028 -->
“Well, go, go with him, Mitya, and I'll watch from here while he dances over there.”

<!-- B08-C08-P029 -->
“No, I'll go and watch too!” Kalganov exclaimed, rejecting Grushenka's offer to sit with him with the greatest innocence. And they all went to watch. Maximov did indeed perform his dance, but it aroused little particular enthusiasm in anyone except Mitya. The entire dance consisted of a kind of hopping, with his feet twisted out sideways, soles upward, and at every hop Maximov slapped a sole with his hand. Kalganov did not like it at all, but Mitya even bestowed a kiss upon the dancer.

<!-- B08-C08-P030 -->
“Well, thank you. Tired, perhaps? Why are you looking over here: want a sweet, eh? Perhaps you'd like a little cigar?”

<!-- B08-C08-P031 -->
“A little cigarette, sir.”

<!-- B08-C08-P032 -->
“Would you like a drink?”

<!-- B08-C08-P033 -->
“I've been having a little liqueur here, sir... Do you have any chocolate sweets, sir?”

<!-- B08-C08-P034 -->
“There's a whole cartload on the table. Choose any you like, you gentle soul!”

<!-- B08-C08-P035 -->
“No, sir, I mean ones with vanilla... for old gentlemen, sir... Hee, hee!”

<!-- B08-C08-P036 -->
“No, brother, we haven't any special ones like that.”

<!-- B08-C08-P037 -->
“Listen!” The old man suddenly leaned right up to Mitya's ear. “That girl, Maryushka, sir, hee, hee—how might I, if it were possible, make her acquaintance, through your kindness...”

<!-- B08-C08-P038 -->
“So that's what you're after! No, brother, nothing doing.”

<!-- B08-C08-P039 -->
“I'm not doing anyone any harm, sir,” Maximov whispered dejectedly.

<!-- B08-C08-P040 -->
“All right, all right. We only sing and dance here, brother, though, damn it! Wait... Have something meanwhile: eat, drink, enjoy yourself. Do you need money?”

<!-- B08-C08-P041 -->
“Perhaps later, sir,” Maximov smiled.

<!-- B08-C08-P042 -->
“Very well, very well...”

Mitya's head was burning. He went out into the passage, onto the little upper wooden gallery that ran along part of the building on the inside, facing the courtyard. The fresh air revived him. He stood alone in a dark corner and suddenly clutched his head with both hands. His scattered thoughts suddenly joined together, his sensations fused into one, and everything blazed with light. A dreadful, terrible light! “If I'm going to shoot myself, when should it be but now?” swept through his mind. “Go and get the pistol, bring it here, and finish it in this very corner, dirty and dark.” He stood for nearly a minute, undecided. Earlier, when he was racing here, behind him stood disgrace, the theft he had already committed, already done, and that blood, blood! But then it had been easier, oh, easier! Everything had been over then: he had lost her, yielded her up; she was lost to him, vanished. Oh, the sentence had been easier for him then, at least it had seemed inevitable, necessary, for why go on living in the world? But now! Was now anything like then? Now at least one ghost, one monster was finished with: this “former” man of hers, her undisputed one, this fateful man had vanished without a trace. The terrible ghost had suddenly turned into something so small, so comical; they had carried it by hand into the bedroom and locked it in. It would never return. She was ashamed, and from her eyes he could now see plainly whom she loved. Now was the time to live and... and he could not live, could not—oh, damnation! “God, bring back to life the man struck down by the fence! Let this terrible cup pass me by! You have worked miracles, Lord, for sinners just like me! What if, what if the old man is alive? Oh, then I'll wipe out the shame of the rest of my disgrace. I'll return the stolen money, I'll repay it, I'll dig it out of the earth... No trace of disgrace will remain except in my heart forever! But no, no, oh, impossible, cowardly dreams! Oh, damnation!”

<!-- B08-C08-P044 -->
Yet a ray of bright hope seemed to flash before him in the darkness. He tore himself away and rushed back to the rooms—to her, to her again, his queen forever! “Can one hour, one minute of her love really not be worth all the rest of life, even in the torments of disgrace?” This wild question seized his heart. “To her, only to her, to see her, hear her, think of nothing, forget everything, if only for this night, for an hour, for an instant!” Just before the entrance to the passage, while still on the gallery, he collided with the innkeeper, Trifon Borisych. For some reason the man seemed gloomy and worried, and appeared to be going to look for him.

<!-- B08-C08-P045 -->
“What is it, Borisych? Were you looking for me?”

<!-- B08-C08-P046 -->
“No, sir, not for you.” The innkeeper suddenly seemed taken aback. “Why would I be looking for you? And you... where were you, sir?”

<!-- B08-C08-P047 -->
“Why so gloomy? You're not angry, are you? Wait, you'll be off to bed soon... What time is it?”

<!-- B08-C08-P048 -->
“It'll be three already. Must be after three, even.”

<!-- B08-C08-P049 -->
“We'll finish, we'll finish.”

<!-- B08-C08-P050 -->
“Oh, don't trouble yourself, sir. As long as you like, sir...” “What's the matter with him?” Mitya thought fleetingly, and ran into the room where the girls were dancing. But she was not there. Nor was she in the blue room; only Kalganov was dozing on the sofa. Mitya looked behind the curtains—she was there. She sat on a chest in the corner, her arms and head bent over the bed beside it, crying bitterly, struggling with all her might to suppress her voice so no one would hear. When she saw Mitya she beckoned him over, and when he ran to her she gripped his hand tightly.

<!-- B08-C08-P051 -->
“Mitya, Mitya, I did love him!” she began in a whisper. “I loved him so much, all five years, all, all that time! Was it him I loved or only my spite? No, him! Oh, him! I'm lying when I say I only loved my spite, not him! Mitya, I was only seventeen then. He was so gentle with me, so merry, he sang songs to me... Or perhaps that's how he seemed to me then, the silly girl I was... And now, Lord, it isn't the same man, it isn't him at all. Even his face isn't his, it isn't him at all. I didn't even recognize his face. I came here with Timofei and kept thinking, all the way I thought: ‘How will I meet him, what will I say, how will we look at each other?..’ My whole soul stood still, and here he poured a tubful of slops over me. He talks like a schoolteacher: everything so learned and solemn. He greeted me so solemnly I was completely at a loss. No way to get a word in. At first I thought he was embarrassed by that tall Pole of his. I sit looking at them and think: why is it I can't talk to him about anything now? You know, his wife ruined him, the one he left me to marry back then... She's the one who changed him there. Mitya, what shame! Oh, I'm ashamed, Mitya, ashamed, oh, ashamed of my whole life! Cursed, cursed be those five years, cursed!” She burst into tears again, but would not release Mitya's hand, clinging tightly to it.

<!-- B08-C08-P052 -->
“Mitya, darling, wait, don't go, I want to say one little word to you,” she whispered, suddenly lifting her face to him. “Listen, tell me, who is it I love? I love one person here. Which person is it? That's what you must tell me.” A smile lit up her face, swollen with tears, and her eyes shone in the half-darkness. “A falcon came in earlier, and my heart sank inside me. ‘You fool, here's the man you love,’ my heart whispered at once. You came in and lit up everything. ‘What is he afraid of?’ I thought. But you were afraid, thoroughly afraid, you couldn't speak. It isn't them he's afraid of, I thought—could you be frightened of anyone? He's afraid of me, I thought, only me. Of course Fenya told you, you silly fool, how I shouted through the window to Alyosha that I'd loved Mitenka for a little hour, and now I was going to love... another. Mitya, Mitya, how could I, fool that I am, think I loved anyone else after you! Do you forgive me, Mitya? Do you forgive me or not? Do you love me? Do you love me?”

She sprang up and caught his shoulders in both hands. Mitya, speechless with delight, looked into her eyes, at her face, at her smile, and suddenly, clasping her tightly, began kissing her.

I remembered that, and today I broke a glass too, drinking to ‘my base heart.’ Mitya, my falcon, why aren't you kissing me? He kissed me once and pulled away, now he's looking, listening... Wait, later, I don't want it like this...” She suddenly pushed him away. “Go away, Mitka, I'll go and drink wine now, I want to be drunk, I'm going to dance drunk this minute, I want to, I want to!”

Mitya followed her like a drunken man. “Let it, let it come, whatever happens now—I'll give the whole world for one minute,” flashed through his head. Grushenka did indeed gulp down another glass of champagne and suddenly became very tipsy. She sat down in the armchair in her former place with a blissful smile. Her cheeks blazed, her lips glowed, her sparkling eyes grew languid, her passionate gaze invited. Even Kalganov seemed to feel a bite at his heart, and he went over to her.

“I'm drunk! Drunk already... drunk on you, and now I want to be drunk on wine too.” He drank another glass and—strange as it seemed to him himself—it was only this last glass that made him drunk, suddenly drunk; until then he had been sober all along, he remembered that himself. From that moment everything whirled around him as in a delirium. He walked about, laughed, spoke to everyone, and did it all as though no longer conscious of himself. Only one fixed, burning feeling made itself known in him every moment, “like a hot coal in my soul,” he remembered afterward. He went up to her, sat beside her, looked at her, listened to her... She had become terribly talkative, kept calling everyone over, would suddenly beckon some girl from the chorus, and when the girl came she would either kiss her and send her away, or sometimes make the sign of the cross over her with her hand. Surprise suddenly showed in her face, almost fear.

“This way, please come out here to us,” a voice said to him, not loudly but firmly and insistently.

Mitya came out from behind the curtain and stood motionless. The whole room was full of people, but not the earlier ones: entirely new people. A sudden chill ran down his back and he shuddered. He recognized them all in an instant. This tall, stout old man in an overcoat and a cap with a cockade was the district police chief, Mikhail Makarych. This “consumptive” neat dandy, “always in such polished boots,” was the assistant prosecutor. “He has a four-hundred-ruble chronometer; he showed it.” And this young little man in spectacles... Mitya had just forgotten his surname, but he knew him too, had seen him: he was the investigator, the judicial investigator, “from the School of Jurisprudence,” recently arrived. And this one was the local police officer, Mavriky Mavrikich; him he certainly knew, an acquaintance. But these men with badges—what were they here for? And two others, peasants... And there by the door were Kalganov and Trifon Borisych...
