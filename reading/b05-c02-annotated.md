# Book V: Pro and Contra
## II. Smerdyakov with a Guitar

<!-- B05-C02-P001 -->
And he had no time to spare. An idea had flashed into his mind while he was still saying goodbye to Lise: what cunning means could he use to catch his brother Dmitri, who was plainly hiding from him? It was already getting late, between two and three in the afternoon. With his whole being Alyosha longed to be at the monastery beside his dying “great man,” but the need to see his brother Dmitri overpowered everything. With each hour the conviction grew in Alyosha's mind that some dreadful, inevitable catastrophe was about to occur. Perhaps he could not himself have defined exactly what the catastrophe was, or what he wanted to say to his brother at that moment. “Let my benefactor die without me, but at least I won't reproach myself all my life for perhaps having been able to save something and failing to save it, passing by, hurrying home. By doing this, I'll be acting according to his great teaching…”

<!-- B05-C02-P002 -->
His plan was to catch Dmitri unawares: to climb the fence as he had yesterday, enter the garden, and settle himself in the summerhouse. “And if he isn't there,” Alyosha thought, “I'll hide and wait in the summerhouse till evening if necessary, without letting Foma or the women of the house know I'm here. If he's still watching for Grushenka to come, he may well come to the summerhouse…” Alyosha did not think out his plan's details very thoroughly, but he decided to carry it through, even if that meant not reaching the monastery at all that day…

<!-- B05-C02-P003 -->
Everything went smoothly. He climbed the fence almost at the same spot as yesterday and stole into the summerhouse. He did not want to be noticed: both the landlady and Foma, if he was there, might take his brother's side and follow his orders, and so either refuse to let Alyosha into the garden or warn his brother in time that someone was looking and asking for him. There was no one in the summerhouse. Alyosha sat in the place he had occupied yesterday and began to wait. He looked around. For some reason the summerhouse seemed much more dilapidated than yesterday; this time it struck him as a shabby little place. Yet the day was just as bright as yesterday. There was a ring on the green table, probably left by yesterday's glass of brandy, which must have spilled. Empty, useless thoughts came into his head, as they always do during a tedious wait: why, for instance, when he came in just now, had he sat in exactly the same place as yesterday, and not somewhere else? At last he grew very sad, saddened by the anxious uncertainty. But he had not sat there a quarter of an hour when suddenly, somewhere very close by, a guitar chord sounded. Someone was sitting, or had just sat down, some twenty paces away, certainly no farther, somewhere among the bushes. Alyosha suddenly remembered that yesterday, on leaving his brother in the summerhouse, he had seen—or caught a glimpse of—an old, low, green garden bench among the bushes to the left, by the fence. So that was where the visitors had sat down. Who were they? Suddenly a man's voice began singing a verse in a sugary falsetto, accompanying himself on the guitar:

A force none can defeat  
Binds me to my sweet.  
Lord, have mer-er-cy  
On her and on me!  
On her and on me!  
On her and on me!

<!-- B05-C02-P004 -->
The voice stopped. A lackey's tenor, and a lackey's flourishes in the song. Another voice, a woman's this time, suddenly spoke affectionately and rather timidly, though with a great deal of affectation:

<!-- B05-C02-P005 -->
“Why haven't you visited us for so long, Pavel Fyodorovich? Why do you keep scorning us?”

<!-- B05-C02-P006 -->
“Not at all, ma'am,” the male voice replied politely, but above all with insistent, unshakable dignity. Evidently the man had the upper hand and the woman was making advances. “The man seems to be Smerdyakov,” Alyosha thought, “judging by his voice, at least, and the lady must be the daughter of the woman who owns this little house, the one who came from Moscow, wears a dress with a train, and goes to Marfa Ignatyevna for soup…”

<!-- B05-C02-P007 -->
“I love any verse terribly, so long as it rhymes,” the woman's voice continued. “Why don't you go on?”

<!-- B05-C02-P008 -->
The voice sang again:

A royal crown—  
May my love’s health be sound.  
Lord, have mer-er-cy  
On her and on me!  
On her and on me!  
On her and on me!

<!-- B05-C02-P009 -->
“It came out even better last time,” the woman's voice observed. “After the crown you sang, ‘Good health to my little love.’ It was more tender that way. You must have forgotten today.”

<!-- B05-C02-P010 -->
“Verse is nonsense, ma'am,” Smerdyakov snapped.

<!-- B05-C02-P011 -->
“Oh, no, I do love a little verse.”

<!-- B05-C02-P012 -->
“As for verse, it's absolute nonsense, ma'am. Consider it yourself: who in this world talks in rhyme? And if we all started talking in rhyme, even under orders from the authorities, how much would we manage to say? Verse serves no purpose, Marya Kondratyevna.”

<!-- B05-C02-P013 -->
“How are you so clever about everything? How did you come to know so much?” the woman's voice grew more and more coaxing.

<!-- B05-C02-P014 -->
“I could have done much more, ma'am, I could have known much more, if it weren't for my lot since my earliest childhood. I'd shoot a man dead in a duel if he told me I was a scoundrel because I came from Stinking Lizaveta, without a father. Even in Moscow they threw that in my face; it crept over there from here, thanks to Grigory Vasilyevich. Grigory Vasilyevich reproaches me for rebelling against my birth: ‘You opened her womb,’ he says. Let it be her womb, then, but I'd have let them kill me inside it just so I need never have come into the world at all. They said at the market—and your dear mother also began telling me, in her great lack of delicacy—that she went about with a mat of hair on her head and was only two arshins and a li-ittle more tall. Why ‘a li-ittle more’ when you can just say ‘a little more,’ as everyone does? They wanted to say it tearfully, but that's a peasant tear, so to speak, ma'am, the most peasant sort of feeling. Can a Russian peasant have any feeling compared with an educated person? In his ignorance he can't have any feeling at all. From my earliest childhood, whenever I heard ‘a li-ittle more,’ I could have thrown myself at the wall. I hate all Russia, Marya Kondratyevna.”

<!-- B05-C02-P015 -->
“If you were a little officer cadet, or a young hussar, you wouldn't talk like that. You'd draw your saber and defend all Russia.”

<!-- B05-C02-P016 -->
“Not only do I have no wish to be a little hussar, Marya Kondratyevna, but on the contrary, I wish for the abolition of all soldiers, ma'am.”

<!-- B05-C02-P017 -->
“And when the enemy comes, who'll defend us?”

<!-- B05-C02-P018 -->
“There's no need for anyone to, ma'am. In twelve there was the great invasion of Russia by the French emperor Napoleon the First, the present one's father,[^b05-c02-n001] and it would have been a good thing if those same Frenchmen had conquered us then. An intelligent nation would have conquered a very stupid one and annexed it. We'd have had quite a different order of things, ma'am.”

<!-- B05-C02-P019 -->
“As if the men over there are so much better than ours! There are some of our dandies I wouldn't trade for three of the youngest Englishmen,” Marya Kondratyevna said tenderly, doubtless accompanying these words with the most languishing looks.

<!-- B05-C02-P020 -->
“That depends whom one adores, ma'am.”

<!-- B05-C02-P021 -->
“Why, you're just like a foreigner yourself, the noblest sort of foreigner. I'm telling you that despite my embarrassment.”

<!-- B05-C02-P022 -->
“If you wish to know, when it comes to depravity, theirs and ours are all alike. All rogues, ma'am, except theirs go about in patent-leather boots, while our scoundrel stinks in his poverty and sees nothing wrong with it. The Russian people need flogging, ma'am, as Fyodor Pavlovich rightly said yesterday, though he's a madman himself, along with all his children.”

<!-- B05-C02-P023 -->
“You said yourself you have such respect for Ivan Fyodorovich.”

<!-- B05-C02-P024 -->
“And he referred to me as a stinking lackey. He thinks I might rebel; he's mistaken, ma'am. If I had that sort of money in my pocket, I'd have been gone from here long ago. Dmitri Fyodorovich is worse than any lackey in his behavior, his intelligence, and his poverty, ma'am, and he doesn't know how to do a thing, yet everyone respects him. I may only be a soup cook, but with luck I could open a café-restaurant on Petrovka in Moscow. Because I cook with special expertise, and not one of them in Moscow, apart from the foreigners, can serve food with special expertise. Dmitri Fyodorovich hasn't a penny to his name, ma'am, but if he challenged the son of the grandest count to a duel, the man would meet him. And how is he better than me, ma'am? For he's incomparably more stupid. How much money he's squandered, to no purpose whatever, ma'am.”

<!-- B05-C02-P025 -->
“A duel must be very nice, I think,” Marya Kondratyevna suddenly remarked.

<!-- B05-C02-P026 -->
“In what way, ma'am?”

<!-- B05-C02-P027 -->
“So frightening and brave, especially when young officers shoot at each other with pistols in their hands over some woman. Just like a picture. Oh, if girls were allowed to watch, I'd so terribly like to see one.”

<!-- B05-C02-P028 -->
“It's all very well when you're the one aiming, but when they're aiming right at your own mug, it's the stupidest feeling, ma'am. You'd run away, Marya Kondratyevna.”

<!-- B05-C02-P029 -->
“Would you really run?”

<!-- B05-C02-P030 -->
But Smerdyakov did not deign to answer. After a minute's silence another chord sounded, and the falsetto trilled out the final verse:

However hard one tries,  
I’ll go away,  
Enjoy li-i-ife  
And live in the capital!  
I won't pine away.  
No, I won't pine away,  
I've no intention whatever of pining away!

<!-- B05-C02-P031 -->
Then something unexpected happened: Alyosha suddenly sneezed. The bench fell silent at once. Alyosha stood up and went toward them. It was indeed Smerdyakov, dressed to the nines, his hair pomaded and perhaps even curled, wearing patent-leather shoes. The guitar lay on the bench. The lady was Marya Kondratyevna, the landlady's daughter. Her dress was pale blue, with a train two arshins long. She was still quite young and would not have been bad-looking, except for her excessively round face and dreadful freckles.

<!-- B05-C02-P032 -->
“Will my brother Dmitri be back soon?” Alyosha asked as calmly as he could.

<!-- B05-C02-P033 -->
Smerdyakov slowly rose from the bench; Marya Kondratyevna rose too.

<!-- B05-C02-P034 -->
“Why should I be informed about Dmitri Fyodorovich? It would be another matter if I were employed as his keeper,” Smerdyakov answered quietly, distinctly, and contemptuously.

<!-- B05-C02-P035 -->
“I only asked whether you knew,” Alyosha explained.

<!-- B05-C02-P036 -->
“I know nothing about his whereabouts, nor do I wish to, sir.”

<!-- B05-C02-P037 -->
“But my brother specifically told me you're the one who lets him know everything that happens in the house, and that you promised to tell him when Agrafena Alexandrovna arrives.”

<!-- B05-C02-P038 -->
Slowly and unperturbed, Smerdyakov raised his eyes to him.

<!-- B05-C02-P039 -->
“And how did you happen to come in this time, since the gate here has been bolted for an hour?” he asked, looking hard at Alyosha.

<!-- B05-C02-P040 -->
“I came from the lane, over the fence and straight to the summerhouse. I hope you'll excuse me,” he said to Marya Kondratyevna. “I needed to catch my brother quickly.”

<!-- B05-C02-P041 -->
“Oh, how could we take offense at you?” Marya Kondratyevna drawled, flattered by Alyosha's apology. “Dmitri Fyodorovich often comes to the summerhouse that way too. Before we even know it, he's sitting there.”

<!-- B05-C02-P042 -->
“I'm very anxious to find him now. I'd very much like to see him, or find out from you where he is. Believe me, it's something very important to him himself.”

<!-- B05-C02-P043 -->
“He doesn't tell us,” Marya Kondratyevna faltered.

<!-- B05-C02-P044 -->
“Though I come here as an acquaintance,” Smerdyakov began again, “even here he's made my life inhumanly difficult with his constant questions about the master: what's he doing, what's happening, who comes and who goes, and isn't there something else I can tell him? Twice he's even threatened me with death.”

<!-- B05-C02-P045 -->
“What do you mean, death?” Alyosha asked in surprise.

<!-- B05-C02-P046 -->
“What would that matter to him, sir, with his character, which you yourself had occasion to observe yesterday? If I let Agrafena Alexandrovna slip past and she spends the night here, he says, ‘You'll be the first to die.’ I'm very afraid of him, sir, and if I weren't even more afraid of the consequences, I ought to report him to the town authorities. He might do God knows what, sir.”

<!-- B05-C02-P047 -->
“The other day he told him, ‘I'll pound you in a mortar,’” Marya Kondratyevna added.

<!-- B05-C02-P048 -->
“Well, if it's a mortar, perhaps it's only talk…” Alyosha observed. “If I could meet him now, I could say something to him about that too…”

<!-- B05-C02-P049 -->
“This is the only thing I can tell you,” Smerdyakov said, as though suddenly making up his mind. “I come here on the usual friendly terms between neighbors, and why shouldn't I, sir? On the other hand, Ivan Fyodorovich sent me first thing this morning to his lodgings in Lake Street, without a letter, sir, to tell Dmitri Fyodorovich by word of mouth that he must come to the tavern here on the square, to dine together. I went, sir, but I didn't find Dmitri Fyodorovich at his lodgings, and it was already eight o'clock. ‘He was here, but now he's all gone’—those were his landlord's very words. It's as though they've some sort of plot together, sir, a mutual one. Now perhaps at this very moment he's sitting in that tavern with his brother Ivan Fyodorovich, since Ivan Fyodorovich didn't come home to dinner, and Fyodor Pavlovich dined alone an hour ago and has now lain down for a rest. But I most earnestly ask you to say nothing to him about me or about what

<!-- B05-C02-P050 -->
I've told you, sir, because he'd kill me over nothing.”

<!-- B05-C02-P051 -->
“My brother Ivan invited Dmitri to the tavern today?” Alyosha quickly asked again.

<!-- B05-C02-P052 -->
“Exactly so, sir.”

<!-- B05-C02-P053 -->
“The Capital City tavern, on the square?”

<!-- B05-C02-P054 -->
“That very one, sir.”

<!-- B05-C02-P055 -->
“That's quite possible!” Alyosha exclaimed in great excitement. “Thank you, Smerdyakov, that's important news. I'll go there at once.”

<!-- B05-C02-P056 -->
“Don't give me away, sir,” Smerdyakov said after him.

<!-- B05-C02-P057 -->
“Oh, no, I'll turn up at the tavern as if by chance, don't worry.”

<!-- B05-C02-P058 -->
“Where are you going? I'll open the gate for you,” Marya Kondratyevna called out.

<!-- B05-C02-P059 -->
“No, it's shorter this way. I'll go over the fence again.”

<!-- B05-C02-P060 -->
The news had shaken Alyosha profoundly. He set off for the tavern. It would be improper to enter a tavern in his clothes, but he could inquire on the stairs and have them called out. Yet the moment he approached, a window opened and his brother Ivan himself called down to him:

<!-- B05-C02-P061 -->
“Alyosha, can you come in here with me now, or can't you? You'd do me a tremendous favor.”

<!-- B05-C02-P062 -->
“I certainly can, only I don't know about my clothes.”

<!-- B05-C02-P063 -->
“I happen to have a private room. Come up to the entrance, I'll run down to meet you…”

<!-- B05-C02-P064 -->
A minute later Alyosha was sitting beside his brother. Ivan was alone, eating dinner.

[^b05-c02-n001]: Napoleon III was Napoleon I’s nephew, not his son. Smerdyakov’s mistake is preserved here.
