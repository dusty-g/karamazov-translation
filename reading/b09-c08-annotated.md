# Book IX: The Preliminary Investigation
## VIII. The Witnesses’ Testimony. The Babe

<!-- B09-C08-P001 -->
The questioning of the witnesses began. But we shall no longer continue our account in the detail we have maintained until now. We shall therefore omit how Nikolai Parfenovich impressed on each witness summoned that he must testify truthfully and conscientiously, and would subsequently have to repeat his testimony under oath; how, finally, every witness was required to sign the record of his testimony, and so on, and so on. Let us note only that the principal point on which the questioners chiefly concentrated was still the same question of the three thousand: whether there had been three thousand or fifteen hundred the first time, during Dmitri Fyodorovich’s first spree here in Mokroye a month ago, and whether there had been three thousand or fifteen hundred yesterday, during his second spree. Alas, all the testimony, every last statement, went against Mitya, with not one in his favor, and some statements even introduced new, almost staggering facts that contradicted his account. The first to be questioned was Trifon Borisych. He appeared before his questioners without the slightest fear; on the contrary, he wore an air of stern and severe indignation against the accused, which undoubtedly lent him an appearance of exceptional truthfulness and personal dignity. He spoke little and with restraint, waited for questions, and answered precisely and thoughtfully. Firmly and without hesitation he testified that no less than three thousand could have been spent a month ago, that all the peasants here would testify they had heard about the three thousand from “Mitry Fedorych” himself: “Just think how much you threw to the Gypsy women alone. They must have had over a thousand.”

<!-- B09-C08-P002 -->
“Perhaps I didn’t even give them five hundred,” Mitya remarked gloomily. “Only I didn’t count it then, I was drunk, more’s the pity.”

<!-- B09-C08-P003 -->
Mitya was sitting to one side now, his back to the curtains, listening gloomily, his sad and tired appearance seeming to say: “Oh, testify to whatever you like, it makes no difference now!”

<!-- B09-C08-P004 -->
“More than a thousand went on them, Mitry Fyodorovich,” Trifon Borisovich firmly contradicted him. “You threw it about for nothing, and they picked it up. They’re thieves and swindlers, those people, horse thieves. They were driven out of here, or perhaps they could tell us themselves how much they got out of you. I saw the money in your hands myself then—I didn’t count it, you wouldn’t let me, that’s true, but by the look of it, I remember, there was much more than fifteen hundred... Fifteen hundred, indeed! We’ve seen money too, we can judge...”

<!-- B09-C08-P005 -->
As for yesterday’s sum, Trifon Borisovich stated outright that Dmitri Fyodorovich himself, as soon as he got out of the carriage, had announced that he had brought three thousand.

<!-- B09-C08-P006 -->
“Come now, is that so, Trifon Borisych?” Mitya tried to object. “Did I really state so positively that I’d brought three thousand?”

<!-- B09-C08-P007 -->
“You said it, Mitry Fyodorovich. You said it in front of Andrei. Andrei himself is here, he hasn’t left yet, call him. And then in the main room, when you were treating the singers, you shouted right out that you were leaving your sixth thousand here—including the previous money, that’s how it’s to be understood. Stepan and Semyon heard you, and Pyotr Fomich Kalganov was standing beside you then, perhaps he remembers too...”

<!-- B09-C08-P008 -->
The testimony about the sixth thousand made an extraordinary impression on the questioners. They liked this new formulation: three and three made six, so three thousand then and three thousand now, and there were all six thousand, clear as day.

<!-- B09-C08-P009 -->
Everyone Trifon Borisovich had named was questioned: the peasants Stepan and Semyon, the driver Andrei, and Pyotr Fomich Kalganov. The peasants and the driver confirmed Trifon Borisych’s testimony without hesitation. In addition, they made a particular record of Andrei’s account of his conversation with Mitya on the road, about “where will I, Dmitri Fyodorovich, go—to heaven or to hell, and will I be forgiven in the next world or not?” The “psychologist” Ippolit Kirillovich listened to all this with a subtle smile, and concluded by recommending that this testimony too, about where Dmitri Fyodorovich would go, be “added to the case.”

<!-- B09-C08-P010 -->
Summoned for questioning, Kalganov came in unwillingly, sullen and peevish, and spoke to the prosecutor and Nikolai Parfenovich as though he had never seen them before in his life, although he had known them a long time and met them every day. He began by saying that he “knew nothing about any of it and wanted to know nothing.” But it emerged that he too had heard about the sixth thousand; he admitted to standing nearby at the time. Asked how much money he thought Mitya had in his hands, he said, “I don’t know.” He confirmed that the Poles had cheated at cards. Under repeated questioning he also explained that after the Poles were expelled, Mitya’s prospects with Agrafena Alexandrovna had indeed improved, and that she herself had said she loved him. He spoke of Agrafena Alexandrovna with restraint and respect, as though she were a lady of the best society, and never once allowed himself to call her “Grushenka.” Despite the young man’s obvious aversion to giving testimony, Ippolit Kirillovich questioned him at length, and it was only from him that he learned all the details of what might be called Mitya’s “romance” that night. Mitya never once stopped Kalganov. At last the young man was dismissed, and left in undisguised indignation.

<!-- B09-C08-P011 -->
The Poles were questioned too. Although they had gone to bed in their little room, they had not slept all night, and when the authorities arrived they hurriedly dressed and tidied themselves, realizing they would certainly be summoned. They appeared with dignity, though not without some fear. The principal one, the short gentleman, proved to be a retired civil servant of the twelfth class who had served as a veterinarian in Siberia; his surname was Mussyalovich. Pan Wróblewski proved to be a dentist in private practice—a tooth doctor, in plain Russian. As soon as they entered, despite Nikolai Parfenovich’s questions, both began addressing their answers to Mikhail Makarovich, who was standing aside, mistaking him for the highest official and the person in charge, and addressing him at every turn as “panie pułkowniku.” Only after several repetitions and instructions from Mikhail Makarovich himself did they realize they must address their answers exclusively to Nikolai Parfenovich. It emerged that they could speak Russian very, very correctly, apart from the pronunciation of certain words. Pan Mussyalovich began speaking heatedly and proudly about his past and present relations with Grushenka, until Mitya at once lost his temper and shouted that he would not allow the “scoundrel” to speak that way in his presence. Pan Mussyalovich immediately drew attention to the word “scoundrel” and asked for it to be entered in the record. Mitya seethed with fury.

<!-- B09-C08-P012 -->
“And a scoundrel, a scoundrel! Put that in, and put in too that despite the record I still shout that he’s a scoundrel!” he shouted.

<!-- B09-C08-P013 -->
Although Nikolai Parfenovich entered it in the record, he displayed the most commendable efficiency and presence of mind in this unpleasant incident: after sternly admonishing Mitya, he himself immediately stopped all further questions about the romantic side of the matter and quickly turned to the essentials. On these essentials the gentlemen supplied one piece of testimony that excited extraordinary interest among the investigators: how Mitya, in that little room, had tried to bribe Pan Mussyalovich, offering him three thousand to withdraw, with seven hundred in cash and the remaining twenty-three hundred “tomorrow morning in town,” pledging his word of honor and declaring that he did not have that much money with him here in Mokroye for the present, but that the money was in town. In the heat of the moment Mitya began to object that he had not said he would definitely pay tomorrow in town, but Pan Wróblewski confirmed the testimony, and Mitya himself, after thinking for a minute, gloomily agreed that it must have been as the gentlemen said: he had been excited then, and so might indeed have said it. The prosecutor fastened on this testimony. It was now clear to the investigators—as they did in fact later conclude—that half or part of the three thousand that had come into Mitya’s hands might indeed remain hidden somewhere in town, or possibly even here in Mokroye. This also explained a circumstance troublesome to the investigation, that only eight hundred rubles had been found on Mitya—a circumstance that until now had been the only, and a rather slight, but nonetheless some evidence in Mitya’s favor. Now even this sole piece of evidence in his favor was demolished. Asked by the prosecutor where he would have obtained the remaining twenty-three hundred to pay the gentleman the next day, since he maintained he had only fifteen hundred altogether and yet had pledged his word of honor, Mitya firmly answered that he had intended to offer the “wretched little Pole” the following day not money but a formal document concerning his rights to the Chermashnya estate, the very rights he had offered Samsonov and Khokhlakov. The prosecutor actually smiled at “the artlessness of the evasion.”

<!-- B09-C08-P014 -->
“And you think he would have agreed to accept these ‘rights’ in place of twenty-three hundred rubles in cash?”

<!-- B09-C08-P015 -->
“He certainly would have,” Mitya retorted heatedly. “Good God, he could have grabbed not just two, but four, even six thousand out of it! He’d have rounded up his pettifogging lawyers at once, his Polacks and Yids, and they’d have wrung not three thousand but the whole of Chermashnya out of the old man.”

<!-- B09-C08-P016 -->
Of course, Pan Mussyalovich’s testimony was entered in the record in the fullest detail. The gentlemen were then dismissed. Their cheating at cards was scarcely mentioned; Nikolai Parfenovich was already too grateful to them and did not wish to trouble them over trifles, especially since it was all merely a drunken squabble over cards and nothing more. There had been plenty of revelry and outrageous behavior that night... And so the money, the two hundred rubles, remained in the gentlemen’s pockets.

<!-- B09-C08-P017 -->
Old Maximov was summoned next. He appeared timidly, approached in little steps, and looked disheveled and very sad. All this time he had been sheltering downstairs beside Grushenka, sitting with her in silence, and “every so often he’d start whimpering over her and wiping his eyes with a little blue checked handkerchief,” as Mikhail Makarovich later related. So that she herself had had to calm and comfort him. The old man at once tearfully confessed his guilt in having borrowed “ten rubles, sir, on account of my poverty, sir,” from Dmitri Fyodorovich, and said he was ready to return them... To Nikolai Parfenovich’s direct question whether he had noticed exactly how much money Dmitri Fyodorovich held, since in taking the loan he could have seen the money in his hands more closely than anyone, Maximov answered most decidedly that there had been “twenty thousand, sir.”

<!-- B09-C08-P018 -->
“And have you ever seen twenty thousand anywhere before?” Nikolai Parfenovich asked with a smile.

<!-- B09-C08-P019 -->
“Certainly, sir, I have, only not twenty thousand, sir, but seven thousand, when my wife mortgaged my little estate. She only let me look from a distance, showing off to me. A very thick bundle, sir, all rainbow-colored hundred-ruble notes. And Dmitri Fyodorovich’s were all rainbow-colored too...”

<!-- B09-C08-P020 -->
He was soon dismissed. At last it was Grushenka’s turn. The investigators were visibly apprehensive about the effect her appearance might have on Dmitri Fyodorovich, and Nikolai Parfenovich even murmured a few words of admonition, but Mitya silently bowed his head in reply, indicating that “there would be no disturbance.” Mikhail Makarovich himself brought Grushenka in. She entered with a stern, somber, almost calm-looking face, and quietly sat down on the chair indicated to her opposite Nikolai Parfenovich. She was very pale, seemed cold, and wrapped herself tightly in her beautiful black shawl. In fact, she was beginning to feel a slight feverish chill—the beginning of the long illness she suffered after that night. Her grave appearance, her direct and serious gaze, and her calm manner made a very favorable impression on everyone. Nikolai Parfenovich was even somewhat “carried away” at once. He himself admitted, when telling the story here and there afterward, that only on that occasion had he realized how “good-looking” this woman was; although he had seen her before, he had always considered her something of a “provincial courtesan.” “Her manners are those of the very highest society,” he once blurted out enthusiastically in a circle of ladies. But they heard him with the utmost indignation and immediately called him a “naughty fellow” for it, which pleased him very much. On entering the room, Grushenka had given Mitya only a fleeting glance, as it were, while he in turn looked anxiously at her, but her appearance immediately reassured him too. After the first necessary questions and admonitions, Nikolai Parfenovich, stumbling a little but maintaining the most courteous manner, asked her “what her relations had been with the retired Lieutenant Dmitri Fyodorovich Karamazov.” To which Grushenka quietly and firmly replied:

<!-- B09-C08-P021 -->
“He was an acquaintance. I received him as an acquaintance this past month.”

<!-- B09-C08-P022 -->
To further curious questions she stated directly and with complete candor that although she had liked him “at times,” she had not loved him, but had led him on out of “my own nasty spite,” just as she had “that old man.” She had seen that Mitya was terribly jealous of Fyodor Pavlovich and everyone else, but had merely amused herself with it. She had never intended to go to Fyodor Pavlovich at all, but had only been laughing at him. “All that month I had no time for either of them; I was waiting for another man who’d wronged me... Only I think,” she concluded, “you’ve no business prying into that, and I’ve no need to answer you, because that’s my own private affair.”

<!-- B09-C08-P023 -->
Nikolai Parfenovich immediately complied: once again he stopped pressing the “romantic” points and went straight to serious matters, that same principal question of the three thousand. Grushenka confirmed that three thousand rubles had indeed been spent in Mokroye a month ago, and although she had not counted the money herself, she had heard from Dmitri Fyodorovich himself that it was three thousand rubles.

<!-- B09-C08-P024 -->
“Did he tell you this privately or in someone’s presence, or did you only hear him saying it to others in your presence?” the prosecutor immediately inquired.

<!-- B09-C08-P025 -->
To this Grushenka stated that she had heard it in company, had heard him saying it to others, and had heard it privately from him himself as well.

<!-- B09-C08-P026 -->
“Did you hear it from him privately once or on several occasions?” the prosecutor inquired again, and learned that Grushenka had heard it on several occasions.

<!-- B09-C08-P027 -->
Ippolit Kirillych was very pleased with this testimony. Further questioning also established that Grushenka knew where the money came from, and that Dmitri Fyodorovich had taken it from Katerina Ivanovna.

<!-- B09-C08-P028 -->
“And did you ever hear, even once, that less than three thousand had been squandered a month ago, and that Dmitri Fyodorovich had kept a whole half for himself?”

<!-- B09-C08-P029 -->
“No, I never heard that,” Grushenka testified.

<!-- B09-C08-P030 -->
It further emerged that, on the contrary, throughout that whole month Mitya had often told her he did not have a kopeck. “He kept expecting to get money from his father,” Grushenka concluded.

<!-- B09-C08-P031 -->
“And did he ever say in your presence... either in passing or in a moment of anger,” Nikolai Parfenovich suddenly plunged in, “that he intended to make an attempt on his father’s life?”

<!-- B09-C08-P032 -->
“Oh, he did!” Grushenka sighed.

<!-- B09-C08-P033 -->
“Once or several times?”

<!-- B09-C08-P034 -->
“He mentioned it several times, always in a rage.”

<!-- B09-C08-P035 -->
“And you believed he would carry it out?”

<!-- B09-C08-P036 -->
“No, I never believed it!” she answered firmly. “I trusted in his nobility.”

<!-- B09-C08-P037 -->
“Gentlemen, allow me,” Mitya suddenly cried, “allow me to say just one word to Agrafena Alexandrovna in your presence.”

<!-- B09-C08-P038 -->
“Go ahead,” Nikolai Parfenovich permitted him.

<!-- B09-C08-P039 -->
“Agrafena Alexandrovna,” Mitya half rose from his chair, “believe in God and in me: I’m not guilty of the blood of my father, who was murdered yesterday!”

<!-- B09-C08-P040 -->
Having said this, Mitya sat down again. Grushenka rose a little and devoutly crossed herself before the icon.

<!-- B09-C08-P041 -->
“Glory to you, Lord!” she said in a fervent, deeply moved voice, and before sitting down, turned to Nikolai Parfenovich and added: “Believe what he’s just said! I know him: he may say anything off the top of his head, for a joke or out of stubbornness, but if it goes against his conscience he’ll never deceive you. He’ll tell the plain truth, believe that!”

<!-- B09-C08-P042 -->
“Thank you, Agrafena Alexandrovna, you’ve strengthened my soul!” Mitya replied in a trembling voice.

<!-- B09-C08-P043 -->
Asked about yesterday’s money, she stated that she did not know how much there had been, but had heard him tell people many times yesterday that he had brought three thousand. As to where he had obtained it, he had told her alone that he had “stolen” it from Katerina Ivanovna, and she had answered that he had not stolen it and that the money must be returned the very next day. To the prosecutor’s insistent question about which money he had said he stole from Katerina Ivanovna—yesterday’s or the three thousand spent here a month ago—she declared that he had meant the money from a month ago, and that that was how she had understood him.

<!-- B09-C08-P044 -->
At last Grushenka was dismissed, and Nikolai Parfenovich hastened to tell her she could return to town that very moment, and that if he could be of any assistance, with horses, for instance, or if she wanted an escort, for instance, then he... for his part...

<!-- B09-C08-P045 -->
“Thank you kindly,” Grushenka said with a bow. “I’ll go with that old man, the landowner, I’ll take him home, and meanwhile, if you’ll allow it, I’ll wait downstairs to see what you decide about Dmitri Fyodorovich.”

<!-- B09-C08-P046 -->
She went out. Mitya was calm and even looked quite encouraged, but only for a minute. A strange bodily weakness kept overcoming him more and more. His eyes were closing from exhaustion. The questioning of the witnesses was finally over. They began preparing the final version of the record. Mitya got up, moved from his chair to the corner by the curtain, lay down on the innkeeper’s large carpet-covered chest, and fell asleep in an instant. He had a strange dream, somehow entirely out of place and time. He seemed to be traveling somewhere on the steppe, where he had served long ago, in earlier days, and a peasant was driving him through the slush in a cart drawn by a pair of horses. Mitya felt cold; it was early November, and snow was falling in large wet flakes that melted as soon as they touched the ground. And the peasant drove him briskly, waving with an easy flourish; he had such a long fair beard, and he was not an old man, about fifty perhaps, wearing a shabby gray peasant coat. And there, not far off, was a village, its huts black, black as could be, and half the huts had burned down, with only charred timbers sticking up. By the road out of the village, women stood lined up on the road, many women, a whole row, all thin and hollow-cheeked, their faces a sort of brown. One in particular stood at the end, so bony and tall; she looked about forty, or perhaps only twenty, with a long, thin face, and in her arms a little baby was crying, and her breasts must have been so dried up, without a drop of milk in them. And the child cried and cried, stretching out its little bare arms, its little fists quite blue-gray with cold.

<!-- B09-C08-P047 -->
“Why are they crying? What are they crying for?” Mitya asks as he flies briskly past them.

<!-- B09-C08-P048 -->
“The babe,” the driver answers him. “The babe’s crying.” And Mitya is struck by his saying it in his own peasant way, “babe,” not “child.” And he likes the peasant’s saying “babe”: there seems to be more pity in it.

<!-- B09-C08-P049 -->
“But why is it crying?” Mitya persists, like a fool. “Why are its little arms bare, why don’t they wrap it up?”

<!-- B09-C08-P050 -->
“The babe’s chilled through, its little clothes are frozen, so they don’t keep it warm.”

<!-- B09-C08-P051 -->
“But why is it like that? Why?” foolish Mitya keeps on asking.

<!-- B09-C08-P052 -->
“They’re poor, burned out of house and home, haven’t a bit of bread, they’re begging for help after the fire.”

<!-- B09-C08-P053 -->
“No, no,” Mitya still seems not to understand, “you tell me: why are the burned-out mothers standing there, why are people poor, why is the babe poor, why is the steppe bare, why don’t they embrace and kiss each other, why don’t they sing songs of joy, why have they turned so black with black misery, why don’t they feed the babe?”

<!-- B09-C08-P054 -->
And he feels within himself that although his questions are mad and senseless, he must ask in just this way, and this is precisely how he ought to ask. And he feels something else: a tenderness such as he has never known is rising in his heart, he wants to cry, he wants to do something for them all so that the babe will cry no more, nor the babe’s dark, dried-up mother, so that from this moment no one will shed any tears at all, and to do it at once, at once, without delay and despite everything, with all the unbridled force of a Karamazov.

<!-- B09-C08-P055 -->
“And I’m with you too, I won’t leave you now, I’ll go with you all my life,” Grushenka’s dear, deeply felt words sound beside him. And his whole heart blazes up and strains toward some light, and he wants to live and live, to go on and on along some path toward a new, beckoning light, and quickly, quickly, this very moment, now!

<!-- B09-C08-P056 -->
“What? Where?” he cries, opening his eyes and sitting up on his chest, just as though coming out of a faint, with a bright smile on his face. Nikolai Parfenovich is standing over him, inviting him to listen to the record and sign it. Mitya realized he had slept an hour or more, but did not listen to Nikolai Parfenovich. He was suddenly struck by the pillow that had appeared beneath his head, though it had not been there when he sank exhausted onto the chest.

<!-- B09-C08-P057 -->
“Who brought a pillow for my head? Who was so kind!” he exclaimed with a sort of rapturous gratitude and a voice full of tears, as though some tremendous kindness had been done him. It never did become known who the kind person was; perhaps one of the official witnesses, or Nikolai Parfenovich’s clerk, had arranged out of compassion to have a pillow put under his head, but his whole soul seemed shaken by tears. He went to the table and declared that he would sign anything they liked.

<!-- B09-C08-P058 -->
“I had a good dream, gentlemen,” he said in a strange way, his face somehow new, as though lit with joy.
