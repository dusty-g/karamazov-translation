# Book V: Pro and Contra
## VII. It's Interesting to Talk with an Intelligent Man

<!-- B05-C07-P001 -->
And so was his speech. Meeting Fyodor Pavlovich in the drawing room as soon as he entered, he suddenly shouted, waving his arms: “I'm going upstairs to my room, not to you. Goodbye,” and walked past, even trying not to look at his father. It is quite possible that the old man was unbearably hateful to him at that moment, but such an unceremonious display of hostility was unexpected even for Fyodor Pavlovich. The old man had apparently wanted to tell him something as quickly as possible, and had come into the drawing room expressly to meet him. On hearing this courteous greeting, he stopped without a word and followed his dear son with a mocking look as he climbed the stairs to the upper floor, until he disappeared from sight.

<!-- B05-C07-P002 -->
“What's the matter with him?” he quickly asked Smerdyakov, who had come in behind Ivan Fyodorovich.

<!-- B05-C07-P003 -->
“He's angry about something, sir. Who can make him out?” the other muttered evasively.

<!-- B05-C07-P004 -->
“To hell with him! Let him be angry! Bring the samovar, then clear out yourself, quickly. Any news?”

<!-- B05-C07-P005 -->
Then began precisely the sort of questioning Smerdyakov had just complained of to Ivan Fyodorovich, all about the expected visitor, and we shall omit that questioning here. Half an hour later the house was locked, and the crazed old man was walking alone through the rooms, trembling with expectation that at any moment the five agreed knocks would sound, occasionally peering into the dark windows and seeing nothing in them but night.

<!-- B05-C07-P006 -->
It was already very late, but Ivan Fyodorovich was still awake, thinking. He went to bed late that night, at about two. But we shall not recount the whole course of his thoughts, nor is it time for us to enter this soul: this soul's turn will come. And even if we tried to recount something, it would be very difficult, because there were no thoughts, only something very indefinite and, above all, too agitated. He himself felt he had lost hold of every thread. Various strange and almost wholly unexpected desires tormented him too. After midnight, for instance, he suddenly felt an insistent, unbearable desire to go downstairs, unlock the door, cross to the servants' lodge, and beat Smerdyakov. But had you asked why, he could not have given a single precise reason, except perhaps that this servant had become as hateful to him as the worst offender against him one could find in the world. On the other hand, more than once that night his soul was seized by an inexplicable, humiliating timidity, which seemed suddenly to rob him even of his physical strength—he felt it happening. His head ached and spun. Something hateful pinched his soul, as though he were preparing to take revenge on someone. He even hated Alyosha as he recalled their conversation earlier, and at moments he hated himself deeply too. He had almost forgotten to think about Katerina Ivanovna, which afterward astonished him greatly, especially since he clearly remembered how, only yesterday morning, when he had so grandly boasted at her house that he was leaving for Moscow the next day, he had whispered to himself in his soul: “That's nonsense. You won't go, and breaking away won't be as easy as you're boasting now.” Long afterward, recalling that night, Ivan Fyodorovich remembered with particular disgust how he would suddenly rise from the sofa and quietly, as though terribly afraid someone might see him, open the door, go onto the stairs, and listen to Fyodor Pavlovich stirring and walking about in the rooms below. He listened for a long time, five minutes at a stretch, with a strange curiosity, holding his breath, his heart pounding. Why he did all this, why he listened, he certainly did not know himself. For the rest of his life he called this “act” “vile,” and all his life, deep within himself, in the secret places of his soul, he considered it the basest act of his entire life. Yet at those moments he felt no hatred at all toward Fyodor Pavlovich himself, only an intense curiosity for some reason: how was he walking about down there, what exactly might he be doing now? He pictured and worked out how the old man must be peering into the dark windows, then suddenly stopping in the middle of the room and waiting, waiting—was someone knocking? Ivan Fyodorovich went onto the stairs twice for this purpose. When everything fell quiet and Fyodor Pavlovich too had gone to bed, at about two, Ivan Fyodorovich lay down as well, firmly determined to fall asleep quickly, for he felt terribly exhausted. And indeed, he suddenly fell into a deep, dreamless sleep, but woke early, at about seven, when it was already light. Opening his eyes, to his astonishment he suddenly felt a surge of extraordinary energy, jumped up quickly and dressed quickly, then pulled out his suitcase and immediately began packing in haste. All his linen had happened to come back from the laundry only yesterday morning. Ivan Fyodorovich even smiled at the thought of everything's falling into place, with nothing to delay his sudden departure. And his departure really was sudden. Although he had said yesterday—to Katerina Ivanovna, Alyosha, and then Smerdyakov—that he would leave the next day, he remembered very well that when he went to bed last night he had not been thinking of leaving at all; at least, he had never imagined that his first movement on waking would be to rush to pack his suitcase. At last his suitcase and traveling bag were ready. It was about nine when Marfa Ignatyevna came upstairs with her usual daily question: “Where would you like to take your tea—in your room, or will you come downstairs?” Ivan Fyodorovich went downstairs looking almost cheerful, though there was something expansive and hurried in his manner, his words, and his gestures. He greeted his father cordially and even made a special inquiry about his health, but without waiting for the answer to finish, announced abruptly that he was leaving for Moscow in an hour, for good, and asked him to send for horses. The old man heard the news without the slightest surprise, most improperly forgetting to grieve over his dear son's departure. Instead he suddenly grew very busy, having remembered, most conveniently, an urgent matter of his own.

“Oh, you! What a fellow! Didn't say so yesterday… Well, no matter, we can still arrange it. Do me a tremendous favor, my dear fellow, stop at Chermashnya. From Volovya station you only have to turn left, a mere twelve little versts, and there you are at Chermashnya.”

<!-- B05-C07-P008 -->
“Really, I can't. It's eighty versts to the railway, and the train leaves the station for Moscow at seven this evening. There's only just time to catch it.”

<!-- B05-C07-P009 -->
“Catch it tomorrow, or the day after, and turn off to Chermashnya today. What does it cost you to ease your father's mind? If I didn't have business here I'd have flown there myself long ago, because it's urgent and important, and this isn't the right time for me here… You see, there's that woodland of mine, in two plots, on the waste lands at Begichevo and Dyachkino. The Maslovs, the old man and his son, merchants, offer only eight thousand for the timber, whereas just last year a buyer turned up who offered twelve—but he wasn't local, that's the thing. The locals have no market now: the Maslovs monopolize it, father and son, worth a hundred thousand. Whatever they offer, you take, and none of the locals dares compete with them. But the priest at Ilyinskoye suddenly wrote here last Thursday to say Gorstkin had arrived, another little merchant. I know him, only the precious thing is that he isn't local, he's from Pogrebovo, so he isn't afraid of the Maslovs, because he isn't local. ‘I'll give eleven thousand for the woodland,’ he says. Do you hear? And the priest writes that he'll only stay another week. So if you went and came to terms with him…”

<!-- B05-C07-P010 -->
“Write to the priest, then. He can come to terms with him.”

<!-- B05-C07-P011 -->
“He can't, that's the trouble. That priest doesn't know how to read a man. He's worth his weight in gold; I'd give him twenty thousand to keep for me this moment without a receipt. But he can't see a thing, as though he weren't human at all; a crow could fool him. Yet he's a learned man, imagine that. This Gorstkin looks like a peasant, in a blue coat, but in character he's an absolute scoundrel. That's the trouble for all of us: he lies, that's the thing. Sometimes he lies so much you can only wonder why. Two years ago he lied that his wife had died and he had married another, and none of it was true, imagine: his wife never died, she's still alive and beats him once every three days. So now we have to find out whether he's lying or telling the truth when he says he wants to buy and pay eleven thousand.”

<!-- B05-C07-P012 -->
“I can't do anything there either. I haven't an eye for it myself.”

<!-- B05-C07-P013 -->
“Wait, wait, you'll do too, because I'll tell you all the signs to look for in Gorstkin. I've dealt with him for a long time. You see, you must watch his beard. It's a reddish little beard, a nasty, thin little thing. If it shakes while he talks and gets angry, all's well: he's telling the truth and wants to do business. But if he strokes it with his left hand and chuckles, then he means to swindle you, he's playing a trick. Never look him in the eyes; you can't tell anything from them, they're murky water, the rogue. Watch his beard. I'll give you a note to show him. He's Gorstkin, only he isn't Gorstkin, he's Lyagavy, but don't tell him he's Lyagavy, he'll take offense. If you come to terms with him and see it's all right, write here at once. Just write: ‘He isn't lying.’ Hold out for eleven. You can come down a thousand, but no more. Think: eight and eleven—that's three thousand difference. Those three thousand would be as good as found money. How soon will I find another buyer? And I need the money desperately. Let me know it's serious, and I'll fly over myself and settle it, somehow find the time. Why should I rush over there now if the fellow's making it all up? Well, are you going or not?”

<!-- B05-C07-P014 -->
“Oh, I haven't time. Let me off.”

<!-- B05-C07-P015 -->
“Oh, do your father a favor, I'll remember it! You're all heartless, that's what! What's a day or two to you? Where are you off to now, Venice? Your Venice won't fall to pieces in two days. I'd send Alyoshka, but what use is Alyoshka in these things? I'm only asking because you're an intelligent man—don't I see it? You don't deal in timber, but you've got an eye. All you need is to see whether the man is serious. I tell you, watch his beard: if the little beard shakes, he means it.”

<!-- B05-C07-P016 -->
“So you're pushing me toward that damned Chermashnya yourself, are you?” Ivan Fyodorovich exclaimed with an angry smile.

<!-- B05-C07-P017 -->
Fyodor Pavlovich did not see the anger, or did not wish to, but seized on the smile:

<!-- B05-C07-P018 -->
“So you're going, you're going? I'll scribble you a note at once.”

<!-- B05-C07-P019 -->
“I don't know whether I'm going. I don't know. I'll decide on the road.”

<!-- B05-C07-P020 -->
“Why on the road? Decide now. My dear fellow, decide! When you've agreed, write me two lines and give them to the priest; he'll send me your little note in a flash. After that I won't keep you—off you go to Venice. The priest will take you back to Volovya station with his own horses…”

<!-- B05-C07-P021 -->
The old man was simply delighted. He scribbled the note, horses were sent for, refreshments and brandy were brought. Whenever he was pleased he always became expansive, but this time he seemed to restrain himself. About Dmitri Fyodorovich, for instance, he said not a single word. Nor was he touched in the least by the parting. He even seemed unable to find anything to say, and Ivan Fyodorovich noticed this keenly: “He really is tired of me,” he thought. Only when he saw his son off from the porch did the old man seem to grow a little flustered and make a move to kiss him. But Ivan Fyodorovich hurriedly offered his hand to shake, plainly warding off the kisses. The old man understood at once and checked himself immediately.

<!-- B05-C07-P022 -->
“Well, God go with you, God go with you!” he repeated from the porch. “You'll come back sometime in your life, won't you? Do come, I'll always be glad. Well, Christ be with you!”

<!-- B05-C07-P023 -->
Ivan Fyodorovich climbed into the traveling carriage.

<!-- B05-C07-P024 -->
“Goodbye, Ivan, don't curse me too much!” his father called one last time.

<!-- B05-C07-P025 -->
The whole household had come out to see him off: Smerdyakov, Marfa, and Grigory. Ivan Fyodorovich gave each of them ten rubles. When he was already seated in the carriage, Smerdyakov darted over to adjust the rug.

<!-- B05-C07-P026 -->
“You see… I'm going to Chermashnya…” Ivan Fyodorovich suddenly blurted out. Again, as yesterday, the words slipped out of their own accord, this time with a nervous little laugh. He remembered it for a long time afterward.

<!-- B05-C07-P027 -->
“Then people are right to say it's interesting to talk with an intelligent man,” Smerdyakov answered firmly, giving Ivan Fyodorovich a penetrating look.

<!-- B05-C07-P028 -->
The carriage started and raced away. The traveler's soul was troubled, but he gazed hungrily around him at the fields, the hills, the trees, a flock of geese flying high above him through the clear sky. Suddenly he felt so happy. He tried talking to the driver, and something in the peasant's reply interested him enormously; but a minute later he realized it had all gone past his ears and he had not actually understood what the man had said. He fell silent. It was good like this too: the air clean, fresh, cool, the sky clear. Images of Alyosha and Katerina Ivanovna flickered through his mind, but he smiled softly and softly blew on those dear phantoms, and they flew away. “Their time will come,” he thought. They quickly covered the first stage, changed horses, and sped on toward Volovya. “Why is it interesting to talk with an intelligent man? What did he mean by that?” Suddenly his breath caught. “And why did I report to him that I was going to Chermashnya?” They reached Volovya station. Ivan Fyodorovich got out of the carriage and the drivers crowded around him. They bargained for the trip to Chermashnya, twelve versts by a country road, with privately hired horses. He told them to harness up. He went into the station house, looked around, glanced at the stationmaster's wife, and suddenly went back out onto the porch.

<!-- B05-C07-P029 -->
“No need to go to Chermashnya. Will I be in time for the railway at seven, lads?”

<!-- B05-C07-P030 -->
“We'll make it just right. Shall we harness up?”

<!-- B05-C07-P031 -->
“Harness up at once. Will any of you be in town tomorrow?”

<!-- B05-C07-P032 -->
“Of course. Mitry here will be.”

<!-- B05-C07-P033 -->
“Could you do me a favor, Mitry? Call on my father, Fyodor Pavlovich Karamazov, and tell him I didn't go to Chermashnya. Can you do that?”

<!-- B05-C07-P034 -->
“Why shouldn't I call? I will. We've known Fyodor Pavlovich a very long time.”

<!-- B05-C07-P035 -->
“Here's a tip, because he probably won't give you one…” Ivan Fyodorovich laughed cheerfully.

<!-- B05-C07-P036 -->
“He certainly won't,” Mitry laughed too. “Thank you, sir. I'll see to it without fail…”

<!-- B05-C07-P037 -->
At seven in the evening Ivan Fyodorovich boarded the train and flew toward Moscow. “Away with everything old! The old world is finished forever. Let there be no news, no echo from it. Into a new world, new places, and no looking back!” But instead of elation, such darkness suddenly descended on his soul, such sorrow ached in his heart as he had never felt before in all his life. He thought all night; the train sped on, and only at dawn, as they were entering Moscow, did he suddenly seem to come to himself.

<!-- B05-C07-P038 -->
“I'm a scoundrel!” he whispered to himself.

<!-- B05-C07-P039 -->
After seeing his dear son off, Fyodor Pavlovich remained very pleased. For two whole hours he felt almost happy and sipped his brandy. Then suddenly something happened in the house that was most annoying and unpleasant for everyone, and instantly threw Fyodor Pavlovich into great confusion: Smerdyakov went into the cellar for some reason and fell from the top step to the bottom. Fortunately Marfa Ignatyevna happened to be in the yard and heard him in time. She did not see the fall, but she heard a cry, a peculiar, strange cry, though long familiar to her—the cry of an epileptic falling into a fit. Whether the fit had struck him as he was going down the steps, so that he must at once have tumbled unconscious to the bottom, or whether, on the contrary, the fall and the shock had brought on a fit in Smerdyakov, a known epileptic, was impossible to determine. But they found him at the bottom of the cellar writhing and convulsing, thrashing and foaming at the mouth. At first they thought he must have broken something, an arm or a leg, and badly hurt himself; but “the Lord preserved him,” as Marfa Ignatyevna put it. Nothing of the sort had happened; the only difficulty was getting him out of the cellar into God's daylight. They asked the neighbors for help and somehow managed it. Fyodor Pavlovich himself was present throughout this proceeding and helped, plainly frightened and seemingly at a loss. The patient, however, did not regain consciousness. The fits stopped for a time, but then began again, and everyone concluded it would be just as it had been the previous year, when he had also accidentally fallen from the attic. They remembered that ice had been put on the crown of his head then. There was still some ice in the cellar, and Marfa Ignatyevna saw to it. Toward evening Fyodor Pavlovich sent for Dr. Herzenstube, who came immediately. After examining the patient carefully—he was the most thorough and attentive doctor in the entire province, an elderly and most respectable man—he concluded that the attack was exceptional and “might prove dangerous,” that for the moment he, Herzenstube, did not yet understand everything, but that tomorrow morning, if the present remedies did not help, he would resolve to try others. The patient was put to bed in the servants' lodge, in a little room beside the quarters of Grigory and Marfa Ignatyevna. From then on, Fyodor Pavlovich suffered nothing but misfortune after misfortune all day. Marfa Ignatyevna cooked dinner, and compared with Smerdyakov's cooking the soup was “like swill,” while the chicken was so dried out that it was impossible to chew. To the master's bitter though just reproaches, Marfa Ignatyevna replied that the chicken had been very old to begin with and that she herself had never trained as a cook. By evening there was another worry: Fyodor Pavlovich was told that Grigory, who had been unwell for two days, was now almost completely bedridden, his lower back having given out. Fyodor Pavlovich finished his tea as early as possible and locked himself alone in the house. He waited in dreadful anxiety. The fact was that this very evening he expected Grushenka's arrival almost for certain; at least, early that morning he had received something close to an assurance from Smerdyakov that “she has definitely promised to come, sir.” The restless old man's heart beat anxiously as he walked through his empty rooms and listened. He had to keep his ears open: Dmitri Fyodorovich might be lying in wait for her somewhere, and when she knocked at the window—Smerdyakov had assured Fyodor Pavlovich two days earlier that he had told her where to come and where to knock—he must open the door as quickly as possible and on no account keep her waiting in the entrance hall for a single unnecessary second, lest, God forbid, something frighten her and she run away. Fyodor Pavlovich had much to fret over, but never had his heart bathed in sweeter hope: it was almost certain that this time she would come without fail!…
