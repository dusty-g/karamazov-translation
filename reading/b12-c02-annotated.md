# Book XII: A Judicial Error
## II. Dangerous Witnesses

<!-- B12-C02-P001 -->
I do not know whether the presiding judge had divided the prosecution and defense witnesses into groups, or in precisely what order he intended to call them. No doubt all this had been arranged. I know only that the prosecution witnesses were called first. I repeat, I do not intend to describe every examination step by step. Besides, my account would be partly superfluous, since the prosecutor’s and defense counsel’s speeches, when the arguments began, brought the entire course and meaning of all the testimony heard and given into a single, vividly and distinctively illuminated focus, as it were. Those two remarkable speeches I wrote down in full, at least in places, and shall reproduce in due course, along with one extraordinary and wholly unexpected episode that erupted suddenly before the arguments and undoubtedly influenced the trial’s terrible, fatal outcome. I shall note only that from its very first minutes one peculiar feature of this “case” stood out plainly, noticed by everyone: the extraordinary strength of the prosecution compared with the resources available to the defense. Everyone grasped this the moment the facts began to gather and concentrate in that forbidding courtroom, and all the horror and all the blood gradually came to light. Perhaps everyone understood from the very outset that this was not even a disputable case, that there was no room for doubt, that arguments were essentially unnecessary and would be a mere formality, while the criminal was guilty, plainly guilty, conclusively guilty. I even think that all the ladies, every last one of them, who so impatiently longed to see the fascinating defendant acquitted, were at the same time completely certain of his guilt. More than that, I think they would actually have been disappointed if his guilt had not been so thoroughly established, because then the ending, when the criminal was acquitted, would have lacked its effect. And that he would be acquitted—strange to say, all the ladies remained utterly convinced of this almost to the final moment: “He is guilty, but they will acquit him out of humanity, because of the new ideas, the new feelings people have nowadays,” and so on and so forth. That was precisely why they had flocked here so eagerly. The men, however, were chiefly interested in the contest between the prosecutor and the renowned Fetyukovich. Everyone wondered and asked himself what even a talent like Fetyukovich could make of such a lost cause, such an utterly worthless case; and so they followed his exploits step by step with intense attention. Yet until the very end, until his speech itself, Fetyukovich remained an enigma to everyone. Experienced observers sensed that he had a method, that something had already taken shape in his mind, that he had an objective ahead of him; but what it was they could scarcely guess. His confidence and self-assurance, however, were unmistakable. Everyone also noticed with pleasure at once that in his brief stay among us, perhaps no more than three days, he had managed to become astonishingly familiar with the case and had “studied it down to the finest points.” Afterward, for instance, people delighted in recounting how he had managed to “trap” each prosecution witness at just the right moment, to throw them off wherever possible, and above all to tarnish their moral reputations and consequently, of course, their testimony. Still, it was assumed he was doing this at most for sport, for a little legal brilliance, so to speak, lest any of the recognized tricks of advocacy be forgotten; for everyone was convinced that all this “tarnishing” could bring him no great or decisive advantage, and that he probably understood this better than anyone, keeping some idea of his own in reserve, some still-concealed weapon of defense that he would suddenly reveal when the time came. Meanwhile, conscious of his strength, he seemed to be playing and enjoying himself. When Grigory Vasilyev, Fyodor Pavlovich’s former valet, was being examined, for instance, and gave his crucial evidence about the “open door into the garden,” counsel fairly fastened on him when his own turn to ask questions came. I should mention that Grigory Vasilyevich appeared in court quite undaunted by either the majesty of the proceedings or the vast audience listening to him, his bearing calm and almost stately. He gave his evidence as confidently as if he were talking alone with his Marfa Ignatyevna, only perhaps more respectfully. He was impossible to shake. The prosecutor first questioned him at length about every detail of the Karamazov family. The family portrait emerged vividly. One could hear and see that the witness was simplehearted and impartial. Despite his profoundest respect for his late master’s memory, he stated, for example, that Fyodor Pavlovich had treated Mitya unjustly and “hadn’t brought the children up right. When he was a little boy, the lice would have eaten him alive if it hadn’t been for me,” he added, describing Mitya’s childhood. “Nor was it right for the father to wrong his son over the estate that came to him from his mother’s family.” Asked by the prosecutor what grounds he had for asserting that Fyodor Pavlovich had cheated his son in the settlement, Grigory Vasilyevich, to everyone’s surprise, produced no substantial facts at all, yet continued to insist that the settlement with the son had been “wrong,” and that “several thousand more ought to have been paid him.” I may note in passing that the prosecutor afterward put this question—whether Fyodor Pavlovich really had underpaid Mitya—with particular persistence to every witness he could ask, including Alyosha and Ivan Fyodorovich, but obtained no definite information from any of them. All asserted the fact, and no one could produce anything like clear proof. When Grigory described the scene at the dinner table, when Dmitri Fyodorovich burst in and beat his father, threatening to return and kill him, a somber impression swept through the courtroom, all the more because the old servant spoke calmly, without unnecessary words, in his own distinctive language, and the result was terribly eloquent. As for the injury Mitya had done him, striking him in the face then and knocking him down, he remarked that he bore no anger and had forgiven him long ago. Crossing himself, he said of the late Smerdyakov that the lad had abilities but was foolish and afflicted by illness, and above all a godless man; it was Fyodor Pavlovich and the elder son who had taught him godlessness. But he vouched almost ardently for Smerdyakov’s honesty, recounting then and there how Smerdyakov had once found some money his master had dropped, and instead of keeping it had brought it to him, whereupon his master had “given him a gold piece” and begun to trust him in everything. He confirmed the open garden door with stubborn insistence. Still, he was questioned about so much that I cannot remember it all. At last the questioning passed to the defense counsel, who began at once to inquire about the packet in which Fyodor Pavlovich had “supposedly” concealed three thousand rubles for “a certain person.” “Did you see it yourself—you, who had been so close to your master for so many years?” Grigory replied that he had not, and had never heard of any such money from anyone “until now, when everyone’s started talking about it.” For his part, Fetyukovich put this question about the packet to every witness he could ask, with the same persistence as the prosecutor’s question about the division of the estate, and from everyone he too obtained the same answer: no one had seen the packet, though a great many had heard of it. Everyone noticed counsel’s persistence on this point from the beginning.

“Now may I ask you a question, if you will permit me,” Fetyukovich suddenly inquired, quite unexpectedly. “What were the ingredients of that balm, or tincture, let us say, with which, as we know from the preliminary investigation, you rubbed your ailing lower back that evening before going to bed, in the hope of curing it?”

Grigory looked blankly at his questioner, and after a brief silence muttered:

“There was sage in it.”

<!-- B12-C02-P005 -->
“Only sage? Can you remember anything else?”

<!-- B12-C02-P006 -->
“Plantain too.”

<!-- B12-C02-P007 -->
“And pepper, perhaps?” Fetyukovich inquired.

<!-- B12-C02-P008 -->
“There was pepper.”

<!-- B12-C02-P009 -->
“And so forth. All steeped in a little vodka?”

<!-- B12-C02-P010 -->
“In spirits.”

A faint ripple of laughter passed through the courtroom.

<!-- B12-C02-P012 -->
“You see, spirits, no less. And after rubbing your back, you were pleased to drink what remained in the bottle, with a certain pious prayer known only to your wife. Is that correct?”

<!-- B12-C02-P013 -->
“I drank it.”

<!-- B12-C02-P014 -->
“Roughly how much did you drink? Roughly? A small glass or two?”

<!-- B12-C02-P015 -->
“A tumblerful, near enough.”

<!-- B12-C02-P016 -->
“A whole tumblerful. Perhaps a tumbler and a half?”

<!-- B12-C02-P017 -->
Grigory fell silent. He seemed to have understood something.

<!-- B12-C02-P018 -->
“A nice tumbler and a half of pure spirits—that’s not bad at all, is it? You might see ‘the gates of paradise standing open’ after that, never mind the garden door?”

<!-- B12-C02-P019 -->
Grigory remained silent. Another ripple of laughter passed through the courtroom. The presiding judge stirred.

<!-- B12-C02-P020 -->
“Do you know for certain,” Fetyukovich pressed ever harder, “whether you were asleep at the moment when you saw the open garden door?”

<!-- B12-C02-P021 -->
“I was on my feet.”

<!-- B12-C02-P022 -->
“That is not yet proof that you were awake.” More and more laughter in the courtroom. “Could you, for example, have answered a question at that moment—say, what year it was?”

<!-- B12-C02-P023 -->
“That I don’t know.”

<!-- B12-C02-P024 -->
“And do you happen to know what year it is now, in our era, counting from the birth of Christ?”

<!-- B12-C02-P025 -->
Grigory stood looking bewildered, staring straight at his tormentor. Strange as it was, he really did seem not to know what year it was.

<!-- B12-C02-P026 -->
“Perhaps you do know, though, how many fingers you have on your hand?”

<!-- B12-C02-P027 -->
“I’m a man under orders,” Grigory suddenly said, loudly and distinctly. “If those in authority choose to make fun of me, I have to bear it.”

<!-- B12-C02-P028 -->
Fetyukovich seemed a little taken aback; the presiding judge also intervened, admonishing counsel to ask more appropriate questions. Having heard him out, Fetyukovich bowed with dignity and announced that he had finished his examination. Of course, a little worm of doubt might remain in the minds of both audience and jury about the testimony of a man capable, under a certain course of treatment, of “seeing the gates of paradise,” and who did not even know the current year from the birth of Christ; so counsel had achieved his purpose after all. But before Grigory left, there was another episode. Turning to the defendant, the presiding judge asked whether he had any comments on the evidence given.

“Except for the door, everything he said was true!” Mitya cried loudly. “For combing the lice out of my hair, I thank him; for forgiving the blows, I thank him. The old man’s been honest all his life, and as faithful to my father as seven hundred poodles.”

<!-- B12-C02-P030 -->
“Defendant, choose your words carefully,” the presiding judge said sternly.

<!-- B12-C02-P031 -->
“I’m no poodle,” Grigory grumbled too.

<!-- B12-C02-P032 -->
“Then I’m the poodle, I am!” Mitya shouted. “If it’s an insult, I take it on myself and ask his forgiveness. I was a brute and cruel to him! I was cruel to Aesop too.”

<!-- B12-C02-P033 -->
“What Aesop?” the presiding judge demanded sternly again.

<!-- B12-C02-P034 -->
“Well, Pierrot… my father, Fyodor Pavlovich.”

<!-- B12-C02-P035 -->
Once more, and then once more, the presiding judge impressed upon Mitya, now with the utmost severity, that he must choose his expressions more carefully.

<!-- B12-C02-P036 -->
“You are damaging your own standing in the eyes of those who are to judge you.”

Counsel handled the examination of the witness Rakitin with equal skill. I should mention that Rakitin was one of the most important witnesses, and one the prosecutor undoubtedly valued. He turned out to know everything, an astonishing amount: he had been everywhere, seen everything, spoken to everyone, and knew the life stories of Fyodor Pavlovich and all the Karamazovs in the greatest detail. True, he too had heard about the packet containing three thousand only from Mitya himself. On the other hand, he described Mitya’s exploits at the Capital City tavern in detail, all his compromising words and gestures, and related the story of Staff Captain Snegiryov’s “scrubber.” But on that particular point of whether Fyodor Pavlovich had still owed Mitya anything after the settlement over the estate, even Rakitin himself could offer no information and evaded the question with contemptuous generalities: “Who could work out which of them was to blame, or calculate who still owed what to whom, in that senseless Karamazov muddle where no one could understand himself or establish where he stood?” He portrayed the whole tragedy of the crime on trial as a product of the ingrained habits of serfdom and a Russia plunged into disorder, suffering for lack of proper institutions. In short, they let him have his say. It was with this trial that Mr. Rakitin first announced himself and began to attract attention. The prosecutor knew that the witness was preparing an article about the crime for a journal, and later, in his speech, quoted several ideas from it, as we shall see below; he must therefore already have been familiar with it. The picture the witness drew was dark and ominous, and greatly strengthened the “prosecution.” In general, Rakitin’s account captivated the audience with its independence of thought and the extraordinary nobility of its intellectual flight. Two or three bursts of applause even escaped, precisely at the passages about serfdom and Russia suffering from disorder. But Rakitin, being after all a young man, made a small mistake of which counsel at once took splendid advantage. Answering certain questions about Grushenka, carried away by his success, of which he was surely already conscious, and by the heights of nobility to which he had soared, he allowed himself to refer to Agrafena Alexandrovna somewhat contemptuously as “the merchant Samsonov’s kept woman.” He would have given a great deal afterward to take back that little phrase, for it was precisely there that Fetyukovich caught him. All because Rakitin had never imagined that in so short a time the lawyer could have acquainted himself with such intimate details of the case.

“May I ask,” counsel began with the most gracious and even respectful smile when his turn to question came, “you are, of course, the very same Mr. Rakitin whose pamphlet, published by the diocesan authorities, *The Life of the Elder Father Zosima, Departed in God*, full of profound religious reflections, and with an excellent and pious dedication to His Grace, I recently read with such pleasure?”

<!-- B12-C02-P039 -->
“I didn’t write it for publication… They published it afterward,” Rakitin muttered, as though suddenly disconcerted by something, and almost ashamed.

<!-- B12-C02-P040 -->
“Oh, splendid! A thinker such as yourself can, and indeed must, take a very broad view of every social phenomenon. Through His Grace’s patronage, your most useful pamphlet circulated and did a certain amount of good… But here is the chief thing I should like to ask you about: you have just stated that you were very closely acquainted with Miss Svetlova?” (Nota bene: Grushenka’s surname turned out to be “Svetlova.” I learned this for the first time only that day, during the trial.)

<!-- B12-C02-P041 -->
“I can’t answer for all my acquaintances… I’m a young man… and who can answer for everyone he meets?” Rakitin flushed all over.

<!-- B12-C02-P042 -->
“I understand, I understand only too well!” Fetyukovich exclaimed, as though embarrassed himself and rushing headlong to apologize. “You, like anyone else, might in your turn have been interested in the acquaintance of a young and beautiful woman who willingly received the flower of the local youth. But… I wanted only to inquire: we know that some two months ago Svetlova was most eager to meet the youngest Karamazov, Alexei Fyodorovich, and that merely for bringing him to her, specifically in the monastic dress he then wore, she promised to pay you twenty-five rubles the moment you brought him. As we know, this took place on the evening of the very day that ended in the tragic catastrophe on which the present case is founded. You brought Alexei Karamazov to Miss Svetlova, and—did you then receive those twenty-five rubles from Svetlova as your reward? That is what I should like to hear from you.”

<!-- B12-C02-P043 -->
“It was a joke… I don’t see why it should interest you. I took it as a joke… and meant to give it back afterward…”

<!-- B12-C02-P044 -->
“So you took it. But you still haven’t given it back… or have you?”

<!-- B12-C02-P045 -->
“It’s nothing…” Rakitin muttered. “I can’t answer questions of that sort… Of course I’ll give it back.”

The presiding judge intervened, but counsel announced that he had finished his questions to Mr. Rakitin. Mr. Rakitin left the stage somewhat soiled. The impression made by the lofty nobility of his speech had certainly been spoiled, and Fetyukovich’s eyes, following his departure, seemed to say to the audience: “There, you see, those are your noble accusers!” I remember that this too did not pass without an episode involving Mitya: enraged by the tone in which Rakitin had spoken of Grushenka, he suddenly shouted from his seat, “Bernard!” When, after Rakitin’s examination was over, the presiding judge asked the defendant whether he had anything to add, Mitya roared:

<!-- B12-C02-P047 -->
“He borrowed money even from me—and now I’m the one on trial! A contemptible Bernard and a careerist! He doesn’t believe in God and he’s hoodwinked His Grace!”

<!-- B12-C02-P048 -->
Mitya was, of course, admonished again for the violence of his language, but Mr. Rakitin was finished. Staff Captain Snegiryov’s testimony fared no better, though for an entirely different reason. He appeared in torn, filthy clothes and dirty boots, and despite all precautions and a preliminary “expert examination,” suddenly proved to be quite drunk. Asked about the insult Mitya had inflicted on him, he abruptly refused to answer.

<!-- B12-C02-P049 -->
“God be with them, sir. Ilyushechka told me not to. God will repay me up there, sir.”

<!-- B12-C02-P050 -->
“Who told you not to speak? Whom are you referring to?”

<!-- B12-C02-P051 -->
“Ilyushechka, my little son: ‘Papa, Papa, how he humiliated you!’ Said it by the stone. He’s dying now, sir…”

<!-- B12-C02-P052 -->
The staff captain suddenly burst into sobs and flung himself down at the presiding judge’s feet. He was quickly led out, to the audience’s laughter. The effect the prosecutor had prepared failed to materialize at all.

Counsel, meanwhile, continued to use every means at his disposal, astonishing everyone more and more with his knowledge of the case down to its smallest details. Trifon Borisovich’s testimony, for example, had initially made a very powerful impression, and certainly one extremely unfavorable to Mitya. Almost counting on his fingers, he calculated that on his first visit to Mokroye, nearly a month before the catastrophe, Mitya could not have spent less than three thousand, “or only the tiniest bit short of it. Think what he threw away on those Gypsy girls alone! As for our lousy peasants, he didn’t just ‘fling half-rubles about the street,’ he gave them twenty-five-ruble notes at the very least, never anything smaller. And think how much was simply stolen from him then, sir! A thief doesn’t leave his hand behind, does he, so how were we to catch him, sir, with the gentleman himself throwing money about anyhow? Our people are robbers, sir; they take no care for their souls. And what went to the girls, our village girls! They’ve been rich ever since, that’s what, sir, where before there was poverty.” In short, he recalled every expense and worked it all out precisely on an abacus. This made the supposition that only fifteen hundred had been spent and the other half put aside in the devotional pouch seem inconceivable. “I saw it myself, saw it in his hands, three thousand to the last kopeck, beheld it with my own eyes! As if we didn’t know how to count, sir!” Trifon Borisovich exclaimed, doing his utmost to please “the authorities.” But when the examination passed to counsel, he scarcely even tried to refute this testimony and suddenly brought up the fact that during that first spree in Mokroye, a month before the arrest, the driver Timofei and another peasant, Akim, had found a hundred rubles on the floor in the entryway, dropped by Mitya while drunk, and handed the money to Trifon Borisovich, who had given them a ruble each. “Well, did you return that hundred rubles to Mr. Karamazov at the time, or not?” Trifon Borisovich dodged as much as he could, but after the peasants were questioned he admitted that the hundred-ruble note had been found, adding only that he had faithfully returned and handed over the whole sum to Dmitri Fyodorovich at once, “out of sheer honesty, only the gentleman himself, being completely drunk at the time, sir, can scarcely remember it.” But since until the peasant witnesses were called he had denied that the hundred rubles had been found at all, his assertion that he had returned the money to the drunken Mitya naturally came under grave suspicion. Thus one of the prosecution’s most dangerous witnesses departed, once again, under suspicion and with his reputation badly smeared. The same thing happened to the Poles, who appeared proud and independent. They loudly testified, first, that both had “served the crown,” and that “Pan Mitya” had offered them three thousand to buy their honor, and that they themselves had seen large sums in his hands. Pan Mussyalovich inserted a tremendous number of Polish words into his sentences and, seeing that this only raised him in the eyes of the presiding judge and the prosecutor, finally grew so elated that he began speaking entirely in Polish. But Fetyukovich caught them in his net as well: however much Trifon Borisovich dodged when recalled, he had to admit that Pan Wróblewski had substituted his own pack of cards for the innkeeper’s, and that Pan Mussyalovich, while dealing the bank, had switched a card. Kalganov confirmed this when his turn came to testify, and the two gentlemen withdrew in some disgrace, even to the audience’s laughter.

Almost all the most dangerous witnesses met with the same fate. Fetyukovich managed to besmirch the character of each and send him away looking something of a fool. Enthusiasts and lawyers could only admire him, while again wondering what great, decisive purpose all this could possibly serve; for, I repeat, everyone felt the irresistible force of the prosecution, which kept gathering strength and tragic weight. Yet from the confidence of the “great magician” they could see that he was untroubled, and they waited: surely “a man like that” had not come from Petersburg for nothing, nor was he the sort of man to go home empty-handed.
