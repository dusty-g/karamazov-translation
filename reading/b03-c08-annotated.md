# Book III: The Lustful
## VIII. Over Cognac

<!-- B03-C08-P001 -->
The argument was over, but strangely enough, Fyodor Pavlovich, who had been so amused, suddenly frowned at the end of it. He frowned and knocked back some cognac, and this glass was decidedly one too many.

<!-- B03-C08-P002 -->
“Out with you, you Jesuits!” he shouted at the servants. “Go on, Smerdyakov. I'll send you the gold coin I promised today, but off you go. Don't cry, Grigory, go to Marfa, she'll comfort you and put you to bed. The scoundrels won't let a man sit in peace after dinner,” he suddenly snapped in annoyance once the servants had promptly left at his command. “Smerdyakov comes nosing in here at dinner every day now. It's you he's so curious about. What have you done to win him over?” he added to Ivan Fyodorovich.

<!-- B03-C08-P003 -->
“Nothing whatever,” Ivan replied. “He's taken it into his head to respect me. He's a lackey and a lout. Still, meat for the vanguard when the time comes.”

<!-- B03-C08-P004 -->
“The vanguard?”

<!-- B03-C08-P005 -->
“There'll be others, better ones, but there'll be men like him too. His kind first, and the better ones after them.”

<!-- B03-C08-P006 -->
“And when will the time come?”

<!-- B03-C08-P007 -->
“The rocket will flare up, though it may go out before it's finished burning. The people aren't very keen on listening to these broth-makers yet.”

<!-- B03-C08-P008 -->
“That's just it, my boy: an ass like that, Balaam's ass, thinks and thinks, and the devil knows what he ends up thinking to himself.”

<!-- B03-C08-P009 -->
“He'll accumulate ideas,” Ivan said with a smirk.

<!-- B03-C08-P010 -->
“You see, I know he can't stand me, or anyone else, and you just as much, though you imagine he's ‘taken it into his head to respect’ you. Alyoshka most of all—he despises Alyoshka. But he won't steal, that's the thing. He doesn't gossip, he keeps quiet, he won't air our dirty laundry, he makes splendid savory pies, and on top of all that, to tell the truth, the devil take him. Is he worth talking about?”

<!-- B03-C08-P011 -->
“Certainly not.”

<!-- B03-C08-P012 -->
“And as for what he thinks up in private, the Russian peasant, generally speaking, needs flogging. I've always said so. Our peasant is a swindler, there's no point pitying him, and it's a good thing he still gets thrashed now and then. The birch keeps Russia strong. Wipe out the forests and Russia's done for. I stand with the intelligent people. We were so clever we stopped thrashing the peasants, but they go on flogging one another. And good for them. With the same measure ye measure, it shall be measured unto you, or however it goes… In a word, it shall be measured. And Russia is a pigsty. My dear fellow, if you knew how I hate Russia… that is, not Russia, but all these vices… though perhaps Russia too. Tout cela c'est de la cochonnerie.[^b03-c08-n001] Do you know what I love? I love wit.”

<!-- B03-C08-P013 -->
“You've had another glass. You've had enough.”

<!-- B03-C08-P014 -->
“Wait, I'll have one more and then another, and then I'll stop. No, wait, you interrupted me. I was passing through Mokroye and asked an old man there, and he said, ‘We're mighty fond of flogging the girls above all, when the village orders it, and we always let the young men do the flogging. Then tomorrow the young fellow takes the very girl he flogged today for his bride, so our girls take kindly to it,’ he says. What marquises de Sade, eh? Say what you like, it's witty. We ought to go and watch, eh? Alyoshka, you're blushing? Don't be ashamed, child. It's a pity I didn't stay for dinner at the superior's earlier and tell the monks about the Mokroye girls. Alyoshka, don't be angry that I offended your superior so badly today. I get angry, my boy. If God exists, if there is a God—well, of course I'm guilty then, and I'll answer for it; but if there isn't one at all, don't those fathers of yours deserve worse? Cutting their heads off wouldn't be enough then, because they're holding back progress. Do you believe me, Ivan, when I say it torments my feelings? No, you don't, I can see it in your eyes. You believe people when they say I'm nothing but a buffoon. Alyosha, do you believe I'm more than a buffoon?”

<!-- B03-C08-P015 -->
“I believe you're more than a buffoon.”

<!-- B03-C08-P016 -->
“And I believe you believe it, and mean what you say. Your eyes are sincere, and your words are sincere. But Ivan isn't. Ivan's arrogant… All the same, I'd put an end to that little monastery of yours. Take all this mysticism and abolish it across the whole of Russia in one stroke, bring all the fools to their senses once and for all. And think of all the silver and gold that would pour into the mint!”

<!-- B03-C08-P017 -->
“But why abolish it?” said Ivan.

<!-- B03-C08-P018 -->
“So the truth can shine forth sooner, that's why.”

<!-- B03-C08-P019 -->
“But once that truth shines forth, you'll be the first one they rob, and then… abolish.”

<!-- B03-C08-P020 -->
“Ha! You may well be right. Oh, what an ass I am!” Fyodor Pavlovich suddenly exclaimed, giving his forehead a little slap. “Well, let your little monastery stand, Alyoshka, if that's the way of it. And we intelligent people will sit in the warm and enjoy our cognac. Do you know, Ivan, God himself must surely have arranged it that way on purpose? Ivan, tell me: is there a God or isn't there? Wait: tell me for certain, be serious! What are you laughing at now?”

<!-- B03-C08-P021 -->
“I'm laughing at that witty remark you made earlier about Smerdyakov's belief in the existence of two elders who can move mountains.”

<!-- B03-C08-P022 -->
“Is this like that, then?”

<!-- B03-C08-P023 -->
“Very.”

<!-- B03-C08-P024 -->
“Well then, I'm a Russian too, I have the Russian trait, and you, philosopher, could be caught out the same way, through a trait of your own. Shall I catch you? I'll bet I catch you tomorrow. But all the same, tell me: is there a God or isn't there? Seriously, now! I need a serious answer.”

<!-- B03-C08-P025 -->
“No, there's no God.”

<!-- B03-C08-P026 -->
“Alyoshka, is there a God?”

<!-- B03-C08-P027 -->
“There is a God.”

<!-- B03-C08-P028 -->
“Ivan, is there immortality, any sort at all, even a little, the tiniest bit?”

<!-- B03-C08-P029 -->
“No immortality either.”

<!-- B03-C08-P030 -->
“None at all?”

<!-- B03-C08-P031 -->
“None at all.”

<!-- B03-C08-P032 -->
“You mean absolutely zero, or is there something? Perhaps there's something of some kind? It can't be nothing!”

<!-- B03-C08-P033 -->
“Absolutely zero.”

<!-- B03-C08-P034 -->
“Alyoshka, is there immortality?”

<!-- B03-C08-P035 -->
“There is.”

<!-- B03-C08-P036 -->
“God and immortality?”

<!-- B03-C08-P037 -->
“God and immortality. Immortality is in God.”

<!-- B03-C08-P038 -->
“Hmm. More likely Ivan's right. Lord, to think how much faith, how much strength of every sort mankind has wasted on that dream, for all these thousands of years! Who is making such a mockery of us? Ivan? One last time, a definite answer: is there a God or isn't there? I'm asking for the last time!”

<!-- B03-C08-P039 -->
“And for the last time, no.”

<!-- B03-C08-P040 -->
“Who is mocking us, Ivan?”

<!-- B03-C08-P041 -->
“The devil, I suppose,” Ivan Fyodorovich said with a smirk.

<!-- B03-C08-P042 -->
“And is there a devil?”

<!-- B03-C08-P043 -->
“No, there's no devil either.”

<!-- B03-C08-P044 -->
“A pity. Devil take it, what I'd do to the man who first invented God, then! Hanging him from a bitter aspen wouldn't be enough.”

<!-- B03-C08-P045 -->
“There would have been no civilization at all if God hadn't been invented.”

<!-- B03-C08-P046 -->
“There wouldn't? Without God, you mean?”

<!-- B03-C08-P047 -->
“No. And there'd be no cognac either. But I really will have to take the cognac away from you.”

<!-- B03-C08-P048 -->
“Wait, wait, wait, my dear boy, one more little glass. I've offended Alyosha. You're not angry, Alexei? My dear little Alexei, my little Alexei!”

<!-- B03-C08-P049 -->
“No, I'm not angry. I know what you think. Your heart is better than your head.”

<!-- B03-C08-P050 -->
“My heart is better than my head? Lord, and listen to who's saying it! Ivan, do you love Alyoshka?”

<!-- B03-C08-P051 -->
“I do.”

<!-- B03-C08-P052 -->
“Love him.” (Fyodor Pavlovich was getting very drunk.) “Listen, Alyosha, I was rude to your elder earlier. But I was agitated. There's wit in that elder, though, what do you think, Ivan?”

<!-- B03-C08-P053 -->
“There may well be.”

<!-- B03-C08-P054 -->
“There is, there is, il y a du Piron là-dedans.[^b03-c08-n002] He's a Jesuit, a Russian one, that is. Being a noble creature, he's boiling with secret indignation at having to pretend… to wrap himself in holiness.”

<!-- B03-C08-P055 -->
“But he believes in God.”

<!-- B03-C08-P056 -->
“Not a bit. Didn't you know? He tells everyone so himself—that is, not everyone, but all the intelligent people who come to see him. He told Governor Schultz straight out: credo,[^b03-c08-n003] but I don't know in what.”

<!-- B03-C08-P057 -->
“Really?”

<!-- B03-C08-P058 -->
“Precisely. But I respect him. There's something Mephistophelean about him, or rather, something out of A Hero of Our Time… Arbenin, or whatever his name is… You see, he's lustful; so lustful that even now I'd be afraid for my daughter or my wife if she went to him for confession. You should hear him telling stories… Two years ago he invited us in for tea, and a little liqueur—the ladies send him liqueur—and once he started describing the old days, we nearly split our sides… Especially how he'd cured a paralyzed woman. ‘If my legs didn't hurt,’ he said, ‘I'd do a dance for you.’ Ah, what do you think of that? ‘I've played the Athos monk plenty in my time,’ he says. He grabbed sixty thousand from Demidov, the merchant.”

<!-- B03-C08-P059 -->
“What, stole it?”

<!-- B03-C08-P060 -->
“The man brought it to him, trusting his goodness: ‘Keep this safe, brother, they're searching my place tomorrow.’ And he kept it all right. ‘You donated it to the church,’ he says. I told him, ‘You're a scoundrel,’ I said. ‘No,’ he says, ‘not a scoundrel. I'm broad-minded…’ But actually, that wasn't him… It was someone else. I've got mixed up and started talking about someone else… without noticing. Well, one more little glass and that'll do; take the bottle away, Ivan. I was lying. Why didn't you stop me, Ivan… and tell me I was lying?”

<!-- B03-C08-P061 -->
“I knew you'd stop yourself.”

<!-- B03-C08-P062 -->
“Liar! You did it out of spite, nothing but spite against me. You despise me. You've come to my house and you despise me in my own home.”

<!-- B03-C08-P063 -->
“And I'll leave. The cognac's getting to you.”

<!-- B03-C08-P064 -->
“I begged you, for Christ's sake, to go to Chermashnya… for a day or two, and you won't go.”

<!-- B03-C08-P065 -->
“I'll go tomorrow if you insist so much.”

<!-- B03-C08-P066 -->
“You won't go. You want to stay here spying on me, that's what you want, you spiteful soul—isn't that why you won't go?”

<!-- B03-C08-P067 -->
The old man wouldn't stop. He had reached that point of drunkenness when certain drunks, quiet until then, suddenly feel an irresistible urge to get angry and show what they're made of.

<!-- B03-C08-P068 -->
“What are you staring at me for? What sort of eyes are those? Your eyes look at me and say, ‘You drunken mug.’ Those suspicious eyes of yours, those contemptuous eyes… You've come here with plans of your own. But look at Alyoshka looking at me, his eyes are shining. Alyosha doesn't despise me. Alexei, don't love Ivan…”

<!-- B03-C08-P069 -->
“Don't be angry with my brother! Stop insulting him,” Alyosha suddenly said firmly.

<!-- B03-C08-P070 -->
“Well, perhaps I will. Oh, my head hurts. Take the cognac away, Ivan, that's the third time I've told you.” He fell into thought, then suddenly gave a long, sly smile. “Don't be angry with a feeble old man, Ivan. I know you don't love me, but don't be angry all the same. There's nothing to love me for. Go to Chermashnya, and I'll come to see you myself, bring you a treat. I'll point out a girl there; I've had my eye on her for a long time. She's still a barefoot little thing. Don't be afraid of the barefoot ones, don't look down on them—pearls!”

<!-- B03-C08-P071 -->
And he planted a smacking kiss on his own hand.

<!-- B03-C08-P072 -->
“For me,” he suddenly brightened all over, as though he had sobered up for a moment now that he'd hit on his favorite subject, “for me… Ah, you boys! You babies, you little piglets, for me… in all my life there's never been an ugly woman, that's my rule! Can you understand that? How could you? You've still got milk in your veins instead of blood, you're not hatched yet! My rule is that in every woman you can find something extraordinarily interesting, damn it, something you won't find in any other woman—you just have to know how to find it, that's the trick! That's talent! There were no ugly ones for me: the very fact that she's a woman is half of it already… but how could you understand! Even in old maids you sometimes find something that makes you marvel at the rest of those fools: how could they have let her grow old without ever noticing! With a barefoot girl or an ugly one, the first thing is to astonish her—that's how you go about it. Didn't you know? Astonish her till she's delighted, pierced to the heart, ashamed that a fine gentleman has fallen in love with a dark-haired girl like her. It's a truly splendid thing that there always are and always will be louts and gentlemen in the world; then there'll always be a little floor-scrubber like that, and always her master, and that's all you need for a happy life! Wait… listen, Alyoshka, I was always astonishing your late mother, only it came out differently. I'd never show her any affection, and then suddenly, when the moment came, I'd fall all over her, crawl on my knees, kiss her feet, and I'd always, always bring her—I remember it as if it were now—to a sort of little laugh, a rippling, ringing laugh, not loud, nervous, peculiar. Only she had that laugh. I'd know that this was always how her illness began, that the very next day she'd start shrieking like a klikusha, and that this little laugh just then meant no delight at all, but even if it was a deception, it was still delight. That's what it means to know how to find your own special touch in everything! Once Belyavsky—there was a rich, handsome fellow here, he was chasing her and took to visiting me—suddenly slapped my face, in my own house, with her there. And she, that sheep—I thought she'd beat me for that slap, the way she flew at me: ‘You've been struck now, struck, you've let him slap your face!’ she said. ‘You were selling me to him… How dare he hit you in front of me! And don't you ever come near me again, ever! Go this instant and challenge him to a duel…’ So I took her to a monastery then to make her humble; the holy fathers said the exorcism prayers over her. But as God is my witness, Alyosha, I never hurt my little klikusha! Except perhaps just once, in the first year: even then she prayed a great deal, kept the feasts of the Mother of God with particular devotion, and at those times she drove me away from her into the study. I thought, I'll knock this mysticism out of her! ‘Look,’ I said, ‘look, here's your icon. Here it is, I'll take it down. Now watch: you think it works miracles, but I'm going to spit on it right in front of you, and nothing will happen to me for it!..’ When she saw it, Lord, I thought, she'll kill me now, but she just jumped up, flung up her hands, then suddenly covered her face with them, shook all over and fell to the floor… just sank down… Alyosha, Alyosha! What's wrong, what's wrong!” The old man jumped up in alarm. From the moment he'd begun speaking about his mother, Alyosha's face had gradually changed. He flushed, his eyes blazed, his lips trembled… The drunken old fellow was spraying spittle and noticed nothing until the very moment when something extremely strange suddenly happened to Alyosha: exactly what he'd just described happening to the “klikusha” happened to him. Alyosha suddenly sprang up from the table, exactly as his mother had in the story, flung up his hands, then covered his face with them, dropped back onto his chair as though his legs had been cut from under him, and began shaking all over in a hysterical fit of sudden, convulsive, silent tears. His extraordinary resemblance to his mother particularly struck the old man.

<!-- B03-C08-P073 -->
“Ivan, Ivan! Water for him, quickly. Just like her, exactly like her, like his mother then! Spray water on him from your mouth, that's what I used to do with her. It's because of his mother, his mother…” he muttered to Ivan.

<!-- B03-C08-P074 -->
“But his mother was my mother too, I believe. What do you think?” Ivan suddenly burst out, unable to restrain his angry contempt. The old man flinched at his flashing eyes. But here something very strange happened, though only for a second: the old man really did seem to have forgotten that Alyosha's mother was Ivan's mother too…

<!-- B03-C08-P075 -->
“What do you mean, your mother?” he muttered, uncomprehending. “What are you on about? Which mother do you mean?.. Was she really… Oh, the devil! Of course, she was yours too! Oh, the devil! Well, my boy, that's a blackout like I've never had before, forgive me, I thought, Ivan… Heh-heh-heh!” He stopped. A long, drunken, half-vacant grin spread across his face. And suddenly, at that very moment, a terrible noise and crash broke out in the entryway, frenzied shouts were heard, the door flew open, and Dmitri Fyodorovich burst into the drawing room. The old man rushed to Ivan in terror:

<!-- B03-C08-P076 -->
“He'll kill me, he'll kill me! Don't let him get me, don't let him!” he shrieked, clutching the skirt of Ivan Fyodorovich's frock coat.
