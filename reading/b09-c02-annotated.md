# Book IX: The Preliminary Investigation
## II. Alarm

<!-- B09-C02-P001 -->
Our district police chief, Mikhail Makarovich Makarov, a retired lieutenant colonel whose military rank had been converted to the civil rank of court councilor, was a widower and a good man. He had arrived among us only three years before, but had already won everyone’s affection, chiefly because he “knew how to bring people together.” His house was never without guests, and it seemed he could not have lived without them himself. Someone invariably dined with him every day: there might be two guests, or only one, but without guests no one sat down to table. There were formal dinners too, under all sorts of pretexts, sometimes quite unexpected ones. The food, though not refined, was abundant; the stuffed pies were excellent, and what the wines lacked in quality they made up for in quantity. In the front room stood a billiard table, with quite appropriate furnishings—even pictures of English racehorses in black frames on the walls, which, as everyone knows, are an indispensable ornament of any bachelor’s billiard room. Cards were played every evening, if only at a single table. But very often all the best society in our town would assemble there, with the mothers and young ladies, to dance. Though a widower, Mikhail Makarovich lived with his family: his daughter, herself long widowed and in her turn the mother of two girls, his granddaughters, lived with him. The girls were grown up and had finished their education; they were not unpleasant to look at, had cheerful dispositions, and, although everyone knew they would have no dowries, still drew our fashionable young men to their grandfather’s house. Mikhail Makarovich was not particularly gifted in official matters, but performed his duties no worse than many others. To speak plainly, he was a decidedly uneducated man and even careless about understanding the precise limits of his administrative authority. It was not so much that he could not fully grasp certain reforms of the present reign: he understood them with certain errors, sometimes very conspicuous ones, not through any particular incapacity of his own, but simply through his easygoing nature, because he never had time to look into them. “I’m more of a military soul than a civilian one, gentlemen,” was how he described himself. Even of the precise principles underlying the emancipation of the peasants he still seemed to have acquired no settled, definite understanding; he learned about them, so to speak, from year to year, adding to his knowledge through practice and without intending to, although he was himself a landowner. Pyotr Ilyich knew for certain that he would find guests at Mikhail Makarovich’s that evening, but did not know who they would be. As it happened, at that very moment the prosecutor and our district doctor, Varvinsky, were there playing the card game yeralash. Varvinsky was a young man who had just arrived from Petersburg, having graduated with distinction from the Medical Academy there. The prosecutor—that is, the assistant prosecutor, whom everyone here called the prosecutor—Ippolit Kirillovich, was a distinctive figure among us. He was not old, only about thirty-five, but had a marked tendency toward consumption; he was married to a very fat, childless lady, and was touchy and irritable, though a man of very sound intelligence and even a kind heart. The whole trouble with his character seems to have been that he thought rather more highly of himself than his real merits warranted. That was why he always seemed ill at ease. He also had certain higher, even artistic aspirations: to psychological insight, for instance, to a special knowledge of the human soul, a special gift for understanding a criminal and his crime. In this respect he considered himself somewhat wronged and passed over in the service, and was always convinced that up there, in the higher spheres, they had failed to appreciate him and that he had enemies. In gloomy moments he even threatened to cross over and become a criminal defense lawyer. The unexpected Karamazov case, the case of the father’s murder, seemed to shake his whole being awake: “A case that could become known throughout Russia.” But here I am getting ahead of myself.

In the next room, with the young ladies, sat our young judicial investigator, Nikolai Parfenovich Nelyudov, who had arrived from Petersburg only two months earlier. Later people here remarked, and even marveled, that all these officials had assembled, as though by design, on the evening of “the crime,” at the home of the executive authority. But it was much simpler than that, and had happened quite naturally: Ippolit Kirillovich’s wife was in her second day of toothache, and he had to escape somewhere from her groans; the doctor, by his very nature, could spend the evening nowhere except at cards. And Nikolai Parfenovich Nelyudov had been planning for three days to turn up at Mikhail Makarovich’s that evening, quite by chance as it were, and spring a sly surprise on his elder girl, Olga Mikhailovna: he knew her secret, he knew it was her birthday and that she had deliberately wanted to conceal it from our society so as not to invite the whole town to a dance. There would be plenty of laughter and allusions to her age, to her being afraid to reveal it, to his telling everyone tomorrow now that he possessed her secret, and so on and so forth. The dear young fellow was a great tease in this way; our ladies even nicknamed him “the tease,” and he seemed to like it very much. He did, however, come from very good society and a good family, had a good education and good feelings, and although he enjoyed himself, his pleasures were entirely innocent and always respectable. In appearance he was short, slight, and delicately built. Several extraordinarily large rings always glittered on his slender, pale fingers. When performing his duties, however, he became exceptionally solemn, as though his sense of his own importance and responsibilities approached the sacred. He was particularly good at disconcerting murderers and other evildoers from the common people when questioning them, and did indeed inspire in them, if not respect for himself, at least a certain astonishment.

On entering the district police chief’s house, Pyotr Ilyich was simply stunned: he suddenly saw that they already knew everything. Indeed, the cards had been abandoned, everyone was standing about discussing the matter, and even Nikolai Parfenovich had hurried in from the young ladies, looking thoroughly combative and eager. Pyotr Ilyich was met with the shattering news that old Fyodor Pavlovich had indeed, in actual fact, been murdered in his house that evening—murdered and robbed. They had learned this only moments before, in the following way.

Marfa Ignatyevna, the wife of Grigory, who had been struck down beside the fence, was sleeping soundly in her bed and might have slept on until morning, but suddenly woke. What helped wake her was Smerdyakov’s terrible epileptic cry from the little room next door, where he lay unconscious—the cry with which his fits always began, and which had always, all her life, terrified Marfa Ignatyevna and affected her painfully. She could never get used to it. Still half asleep, she sprang up and rushed, almost beside herself, into Smerdyakov’s little room. But it was dark there, and she could only hear the sick man beginning to snore horribly and thrash about. Marfa Ignatyevna cried out herself and began calling her husband, then suddenly realized that Grigory had not seemed to be in bed when she got up. She ran to the bed and felt it over again, but it was indeed empty. He must have gone out, then—where? She ran onto the porch and timidly called to him from there. There was, of course, no answer, but in the stillness of the night she heard what sounded like groans somewhere far off in the garden. She listened; the groans came again, and it was clear that they really were coming from the garden. “Lord, just like that time with Stinking Lizaveta!” flashed through her troubled mind. She went timidly down the steps and saw that the gate into the garden was open. “He must be there, poor dear,” she thought, went up to the gate, and suddenly heard Grigory distinctly calling her: “Marfa, Marfa!” in a weak, groaning, dreadful voice. “Lord, preserve us from misfortune,” Marfa Ignatyevna whispered, and rushed toward his voice; that was how she found Grigory. But she found him not beside the fence, not where he had been struck down, but a good twenty paces from it. It emerged later that, on regaining consciousness, he had crawled away, and had probably been crawling for a long time, repeatedly losing consciousness and sinking back into oblivion. She immediately saw that he was covered in blood and now began screaming at the top of her lungs. Grigory muttered softly and incoherently: “Killed... killed his father... what are you screaming for, fool... run, fetch someone...” But Marfa Ignatyevna would not stop screaming, and suddenly, noticing that the master’s window was open and a light shone in it, she ran over and began calling Fyodor Pavlovich. Looking through the window, however, she saw a terrible sight: the master lay on his back on the floor, motionless. His light-colored dressing gown and the white shirt over his chest were soaked with blood. The candle on the table brightly illuminated the blood and Fyodor Pavlovich’s still, dead face. Now utterly terrified, Marfa Ignatyevna rushed away from the window, ran out of the garden, unfastened the main gate, and raced around to the back, to their neighbor Marya Kondratyevna. Both neighbors, mother and daughter, were already asleep, but Marfa Ignatyevna’s frantic, insistent pounding on the shutters and her cries woke them, and they sprang to the window. Incoherently, shrieking and shouting, Marfa Ignatyevna nevertheless conveyed the essential facts and called for help. The wanderer Foma happened to be spending that night with them. They roused him in an instant, and the three of them ran to the scene of the crime. On the way Marya Kondratyevna recalled that earlier, between eight and nine, she had heard a terrible, piercing cry from their garden, loud enough to be heard all over the neighborhood—and this, of course, was precisely Grigory’s cry when he seized Dmitri Fyodorovich’s leg as the latter sat astride the fence and shouted, “Parricide!” “Someone gave one cry, then suddenly stopped,” Marya Kondratyevna testified as she ran. Reaching the place where Grigory lay, the two women, with Foma’s help, carried him into the servants’ wing. They lit a light and saw that Smerdyakov was still not quiet: he was writhing in his little room, his eyes rolled sideways, foam running from his lips. They washed Grigory’s head with water and vinegar, and the water fully restored his senses. At once he asked: “Is the master killed or not?” The two women and Foma then went to the master’s house, and when they entered the garden they saw this time that not only the window but also the door from the house into the garden stood unlocked and wide open, although for the past week the master had locked himself in tightly every evening and would not allow even Grigory to knock under any circumstances. At the sight of this open door all of them, both women and Foma, immediately grew afraid to go in to the master, “in case something came of it afterward.” And when they returned, Grigory told them to run straight to the district police chief himself. So Marya Kondratyevna ran off and raised the alarm at the police chief’s. She had arrived only five minutes before Pyotr Ilyich, so that he appeared now with more than his own conjectures and deductions: he was an eyewitness whose account further confirmed everyone’s suspicion as to who the criminal was—something which, deep down, he himself had still refused to believe up to this very last moment.

They decided to act vigorously. The assistant to the town police superintendent was immediately instructed to assemble up to four official witnesses, and, observing all the formalities, which I shall not describe here, they entered Fyodor Pavlovich’s house and conducted an investigation on the spot. The district doctor, an eager man and a newcomer, had all but asked outright to accompany the police chief, prosecutor, and investigator. I shall give only a brief outline: Fyodor Pavlovich proved to have been well and truly murdered, his skull smashed in—but with what? Most probably with the same weapon that afterward struck down Grigory. And they did indeed find the weapon, after hearing from Grigory, who had received what medical assistance could be given, a fairly coherent account, though delivered in a weak and faltering voice, of how he had been struck down. They searched beside the fence with a lantern and found the brass pestle, thrown right onto the garden path in plain sight. No particular disorder was noticed in the room where Fyodor Pavlovich lay, but behind the screens, beside his bed, they picked up from the floor a large official-size envelope of heavy paper bearing the inscription: “A little gift of three thousand rubles for my angel Grushenka, if she chooses to come”; below this, probably added later by Fyodor Pavlovich himself, were the words “and my little chick.” The envelope bore three large seals of red wax, but it had been torn open and was empty: the money had been taken. On the floor they also found the narrow pink ribbon that had been tied around it. Among the details in Pyotr Ilyich’s testimony, one made an extraordinary impression on the prosecutor and investigator: his conjecture that Dmitri Fyodorovich would certainly shoot himself by dawn—that he had decided to do so himself, had told Pyotr Ilyich himself, had loaded the pistol in his presence, written a note and put it in his pocket, and so on and so forth. When Pyotr Ilyich, still unwilling to believe him, had threatened to go and tell someone so as to prevent his suicide, Mitya himself had reportedly replied with a grin: “You won’t be in time.” They therefore had to hurry to the scene, to Mokroye, to catch the criminal before he might actually take it into his head to shoot himself. “It’s clear, it’s clear!” the prosecutor kept repeating in great excitement. “That is exactly how scoundrels of that sort do things: tomorrow I’ll kill myself, but a spree before I die.” The story of the wine and goods he had collected at the shop only excited the prosecutor still further. “Remember that fellow, gentlemen, who murdered the merchant Olsufyev, robbed him of fifteen hundred, and went straight off to have his hair curled, then, without even putting the money properly away, carrying it almost openly in his hands too, went to the girls?” But the investigation, the search of Fyodor Pavlovich’s house, the formalities, and so on detained them all. This took time, so they sent the local police officer, Mavriky Mavrikievich Shmertsov, to Mokroye some two hours ahead of them; he happened to have arrived in town the previous morning to collect his salary. Mavriky Mavrikievich was instructed to keep the “criminal” under constant observation on reaching Mokroye, without raising any alarm, until the proper authorities arrived, and also to have official witnesses, village constables, and so forth ready. Mavriky Mavrikievich did just that: he remained incognito and took only Trifon Borisovich, an old acquaintance, partly into his confidence. This coincided precisely with the moment when Mitya met the innkeeper looking for him in the dark on the little gallery, and noticed at once some sudden change in Trifon Borisovich’s face and manner of speaking. Thus neither Mitya nor anyone else knew they were being watched; his box of pistols, meanwhile, had long since been spirited away by Trifon Borisovich and hidden in a secluded place. Only between four and five in the morning, almost at daybreak, did the authorities—the police chief, prosecutor, and investigator—arrive in two carriages, each drawn by a troika. The doctor remained at Fyodor Pavlovich’s house, intending to perform an autopsy on the murdered man in the morning; chiefly, however, he had become interested in the condition of the sick servant Smerdyakov. “Such violent and prolonged epileptic fits, recurring incessantly for two days, are rarely encountered, and this is a matter for science,” he told his departing card partners excitedly, and they laughingly congratulated him on his discovery. The prosecutor and investigator also remembered very clearly that the doctor added, in the most categorical tone, that Smerdyakov would not live until morning.
