# Book V · Pro and Contra
## IV. Rebellion

<!-- B05-C04-P001 -->
“I have a confession to make,” Ivan began. “I've never been able to understand how anyone can love their neighbors. It's precisely one's neighbors, to my mind, that one can't love; perhaps only people far away. I once read somewhere about ‘John the Merciful’—a saint—who, when a hungry, frozen traveler came to him and asked to be warmed, got into bed with him, embraced him, and began breathing into his mouth, which was festering and stank from some terrible disease. I'm convinced he did it by straining himself into a lie, out of a love commanded by duty, as a penance he'd forced on himself. To love a person, you need them to stay hidden; the moment they show their face, love is gone.”

<!-- B05-C04-P002 -->
“Elder Zosima has spoken about that more than once,” Alyosha observed. “He too said that a person's face often prevents people still inexperienced in love from loving them. But there's a great deal of love in humanity too, love almost like Christ's. I know that myself, Ivan...”

<!-- B05-C04-P003 -->
“Well, I don't know it yet, and I can't understand it, and countless people are in the same position. The question is whether this comes from people's bad qualities or simply from their nature. To my mind, Christ's love for people is a kind of miracle impossible on earth. True, he was God. But we aren't gods. Suppose, for example, that I'm suffering deeply; another person can never know how deeply I suffer, because he's another person, not me, and besides, people will rarely agree to acknowledge someone else as a sufferer—as though it were a rank. Why won't they, do you think? Because, for example, I smell bad, or I have a stupid face, or I once stepped on their foot. Besides, there are different sorts of suffering: humiliating suffering, suffering that degrades me—hunger, for example—my benefactor will still allow me, but anything a little higher, suffering for an idea, say, no, he'll only allow that in very rare cases, because he'll look at me, for example, and suddenly see that my face isn't at all the face that, in his imagination, someone suffering for that particular idea ought to have. So he withdraws his favors at once, without even being hardhearted. Beggars, especially beggars of noble birth, ought never to appear in public, but ask for charity through the newspapers. It's still possible to love your neighbor in the abstract, and sometimes even from a distance, but close up, almost never. If everything were like the stage, like a ballet, where beggars, when they appear, come in silken rags and torn lace, asking for alms as they dance gracefully—well, then you could still admire them. Admire them, but still not love them. Enough of that, though. I only needed to put you in my position. I meant to speak about human suffering in general, but we'd better stick to the suffering of children alone. That will reduce the scope of my argument tenfold, but better just the children. All the less to my advantage, of course. But first, you can love children even close up, even dirty ones, even ugly ones—though it seems to me children are never ugly. Second, another reason I won't discuss adults is that, besides being repulsive and undeserving of love, they have their punishment: they ate the apple and came to know good and evil, and became ‘as gods.’ They're still eating it now. But the little children haven't eaten anything and aren't guilty of anything yet. Do you love children, Alyosha? I know you do, and you'll understand why I only want to talk about them now. If they too suffer terribly on earth, then of course it's for their fathers: they're punished for their fathers who ate the apple—but that's reasoning from another world, something the human heart here on earth can't understand. An innocent person mustn't suffer for someone else, especially someone so innocent! Marvel at me, Alyosha: I too love little children terribly. And notice, cruel people, passionate, flesh-eating people, Karamazovs, sometimes love children very much. Children, while they're children—up to seven, say—are terribly far removed from people: almost another kind of creature, with another nature. I knew a bandit in prison: in the course of his career, when he broke into houses at night to rob them and slaughtered entire families, he'd sometimes cut the throats of a few children along with the rest. But in prison he loved them to a strange degree. All he ever did was look out of the prison window at the children playing in the prison yard. He got one little boy into the habit of coming beneath his window, and they became great friends... Do you know why I'm telling you all this, Alyosha? My head aches somehow, and I feel sad.”

<!-- B05-C04-P004 -->
“You have a strange look as you talk,” Alyosha observed anxiously, “as though you were in some kind of frenzy.”

<!-- B05-C04-P005 -->
“By the way, a Bulgarian in Moscow recently told me,” Ivan Fyodorovich went on, as though he hadn't heard his brother, “how the Turks and Circassians are committing atrocities everywhere there in Bulgaria, afraid of a general uprising of the Slavs—burning, slaughtering, raping women and children, nailing prisoners' ears to fences and leaving them there till morning, then hanging them in the morning, and so on. You can't imagine it all. Really, people sometimes speak of man's ‘bestial’ cruelty, but that's terribly unfair and insulting to beasts: a beast can never be as cruel as a human being, so artfully, so artistically cruel. A tiger simply bites and tears—that's all it knows. It would never think of nailing people up by their ears overnight, even if it could do it. These Turks, among other things, took sensual pleasure in torturing children too, everything from cutting them out of their mothers' wombs with daggers to tossing nursing babies into the air and catching them on bayonets before their mothers' eyes. Before their mothers' eyes—that was the chief pleasure. But here's one picture that particularly interested me. Imagine a little nursing baby in its trembling mother's arms, with the Turks who've come in standing all around. They dream up a merry little game: they caress the baby and laugh, trying to make it laugh. They succeed; the baby laughs. At that moment a Turk aims a pistol at it from about seven inches away. The little boy laughs joyfully, reaches out his little hands to catch the pistol, and suddenly the artist pulls the trigger right in his face and shatters his little head. Artistic, isn't it? By the way, they say the Turks are very fond of sweets.”

<!-- B05-C04-P006 -->
“Brother, what's all this for?” Alyosha asked.

<!-- B05-C04-P007 -->
“I think that if the devil doesn't exist, and consequently man created him, he created him in his own image and likeness.”

<!-- B05-C04-P008 -->
“In that case, so did he create God.”

<!-- B05-C04-P009 -->
“You have a remarkable way of turning words about, as Polonius says in *Hamlet*,” Ivan laughed. “You've caught me out in my own words; very well, I'm glad. A fine God yours is, if man created him in his own image and likeness. You asked just now what all this was for: you see, I'm an enthusiast and collector of certain little facts, and would you believe it, I write down and collect particular sorts of little anecdotes from newspapers, from things people tell me, from wherever I can, and I already have a nice collection. The Turks have gone into it, of course, but they're all foreigners. I have some homegrown little items too, even better than the Turkish ones. You know, with us it's more beatings, more birch rods and lashes—that's our national way. Ears nailed up are unthinkable here; we're Europeans after all. But the birch, but the lash—those really are ours, and they can't be taken away from us. Abroad they seem hardly to beat anyone at all nowadays: perhaps their manners have improved, or their laws are now such that one person hardly dares flog another. But they've made up for it with something else, something just as thoroughly national as ours, so national that it seems almost impossible here—though it does seem to be taking root here too, especially since the religious movement in our high society. I have a delightful little pamphlet, translated from the French, about how in Geneva, quite recently, only five years ago, they executed a criminal and murderer called Richard, a lad of twenty-three, I think, who repented and converted to Christianity just before going to the scaffold. This Richard was somebody's illegitimate child, and while he was still a little child, six years old, his parents gave him away to some Swiss mountain shepherds, who brought him up to put him to work. He grew up among them like a little wild animal. The shepherds taught him nothing; instead, at seven they were already sending him out to tend the flock in wet weather and cold, almost unclothed and hardly fed. And of course none of them ever gave this a thought or felt any remorse; on the contrary, they considered themselves perfectly entitled, since Richard had been given to them like an object, and they didn't even consider it necessary to feed him. Richard himself testifies that in those years, like the prodigal son in the Gospel, he desperately longed for even some of the mash given to the pigs being fattened for sale, but he wasn't allowed even that, and they beat him when he stole from the pigs. That was how he spent his entire childhood and youth, until he grew up and, with his new strength, went off to steal on his own. The savage began earning money as a day laborer in Geneva, drank away his earnings, lived like a brute, and ended by murdering and robbing an old man. They caught him, tried him, and sentenced him to death. They don't go in for sentimentality there. And in prison he was immediately surrounded by pastors and members of various Christian brotherhoods, charitable ladies, and so on. They taught him to read and write in prison, began explaining the Gospel to him, appealed to his conscience, persuaded him, pressed him, nagged him, squeezed him, and at last he himself solemnly confessed his crime. He was converted; he wrote to the court himself saying he was a monster and had at last been found worthy for the Lord to enlighten him too and send him grace. All Geneva was stirred, all charitable and pious Geneva. Everyone of the highest standing and best breeding rushed to see him in prison; they kissed Richard, embraced him: ‘You are our brother, grace has descended on you!’ And Richard himself did nothing but weep with emotion: ‘Yes, grace has descended on me! Before, through all my childhood and youth, I was glad to have pigs' food, and now grace has descended on me too. I die in the Lord!’ ‘Yes, yes, Richard, die in the Lord. You have shed blood and must die in the Lord. Granted, it wasn't your fault that you knew nothing of the Lord when you envied the pigs their food and were beaten for stealing it from them—which was very wrong of you, since stealing is forbidden—but you have shed blood and must die.’ And then the last day comes. Richard, overcome with weakness, weeps and does nothing but repeat every minute: ‘This is the best day of my life. I'm going to the Lord!’ ‘Yes,’ cry the pastors, the judges, and the charitable ladies, ‘this is your happiest day, for you're going to the Lord!’ They all proceed to the scaffold, in carriages and on foot, following the cart of shame in which Richard is being taken. They've reached the scaffold: ‘Die, our brother,’ they cry to Richard, ‘die in the Lord, for grace has descended on you too!’ And so, covered with his brothers' kisses, brother Richard was dragged up onto the scaffold, laid under the guillotine, and had his head duly lopped off in brotherly fashion, because grace had descended on him too. No, that's characteristic. This little pamphlet was translated into Russian by some Russian high-society philanthropists with Lutheran leanings and distributed free with newspapers and other publications to enlighten the Russian people. What's good about the Richard business is that it's national. Here it would seem absurd to chop off a brother's head merely because he'd become our brother and grace had descended on him, but I repeat, we have something of our own almost as good. We have an age-old, immediate and very intimate pleasure in the torture of beating. Nekrasov has a poem about a peasant whipping a horse across the eyes, ‘across its gentle eyes.’ Who hasn't seen that? It's a Russian specialty. He describes a feeble little horse that has been overloaded and is stuck with its cart, unable to pull it out. The peasant beats it, beats it savagely, finally beats it without knowing what he's doing, drunk on the beating, whipping it painfully, endlessly: ‘You've no strength, but pull! Die, but pull!’ The poor nag strains, and then he begins whipping the defenseless thing across its weeping, its ‘gentle eyes.’ Beside itself, it gives a wrench and pulls the cart out, then goes on, trembling all over, breathless, somehow sideways, in a kind of hopping gait, unnatural and shameful—it's terrible in Nekrasov. But it's only a horse, after all; God himself gave us horses to whip. That's what the Tatars explained to us, and they gave us the knout as a keepsake. But you can whip people too. So here's an intelligent, educated gentleman and his lady whipping their own daughter, a little child of seven, with birch rods—I have this recorded in detail. Papa's pleased that the rods have knots in them: ‘They'll sting harder,’ he says, and starts ‘stinging’ his own daughter. I know for certain there are people doing the whipping who get more and more excited with each blow, to the point of sensual pleasure, literal sensual pleasure, with every succeeding blow more and more, steadily increasing. They whip for a minute, then for five minutes, they whip for ten minutes, on and on, harder, faster, sharper. The child screams, finally the child can't scream, she gasps for breath. ‘Papa, Papa, Daddy, Daddy!’ Through some devilish, indecent accident the case reaches court. A lawyer is hired. The Russian people long ago gave the lawyer a name: ‘An advocut—a hired conscience.’ The lawyer shouts in defense of his client. ‘Such a simple, domestic, everyday business, you see: a father gave his daughter a whipping, and now, to the shame of our times, it has come to court!’ Convinced, the jurors retire and return an acquittal. The public roars with happiness that the torturer's been acquitted. Ah, I wasn't there, or I'd have roared out a proposal to establish a scholarship in the torturer's name! Delightful pictures. But I have even better ones about children; I've collected a great, great many about Russian children, Alyosha. A little five-year-old girl was hated by her father and mother, ‘highly respectable people with official standing, educated and well brought up.’ You see, I positively insist once again that many people have a particular trait: a love of torturing children, but only children. These very same torturers treat all other specimens of the human race quite kindly and gently, like educated, humane Europeans, but they love torturing children, they even love the children themselves in that sense. It's precisely the defenselessness of these creatures that tempts the torturers, the angelic trust of a child with nowhere to go and no one to turn to—that's what inflames the torturer's foul blood. Of course there's a beast hidden in every person, a beast of anger, a beast that grows heated with sensual pleasure at the screams of its tortured victim, an unrestrained beast let off its chain, a beast of diseases acquired through debauchery, of gout and sick livers, and so on. These educated parents subjected the poor five-year-old girl to every kind of torture. They beat her, whipped her, kicked her without knowing why themselves, covered her whole body with bruises; finally they attained the height of refinement: in the cold, in freezing weather, they locked her in the privy all night, and because she hadn't asked to use the privy at night—as though a five-year-old child sleeping its sound, angelic sleep could have learned at that age to ask!—for this they smeared her whole face with her excrement and forced her to eat that excrement, and it was her mother, her mother who forced her! And that mother could sleep while the poor little child, locked in that foul place, could be heard moaning in the night! Can you understand it, when a little creature who can't even grasp what's happening to her beats her tormented little chest with her tiny fist, in that foul place, in the dark and cold, and weeps her bloody, unresentful, gentle little tears to ‘dear God’ to protect her—can you understand this nonsense, my friend and brother, my humble novice of God, can you understand why this nonsense is so necessary and was created! Without it, they say, man couldn't even have existed on earth, because he wouldn't have known good and evil. Why know this damned good and evil when it costs so much? The whole world of knowledge isn't worth those little tears of a child to ‘dear God.’ I'm not talking about the suffering of grown-ups: they ate the apple, and to hell with them, let the devil take them all, but these children, these children! I'm tormenting you, Alyoshka, you seem beside yourself. I'll stop if you like.”

<!-- B05-C04-P010 -->
“It's all right, I want to suffer too,” Alyosha murmured.

<!-- B05-C04-P011 -->
“One more picture, just one, and only out of curiosity: it's so characteristic, and above all I just read it in one of our collections of historical documents, in *The Archive* or *Old Times*, I think—I must check, I've even forgotten where I read it. This was in the darkest days of serfdom, at the beginning of the century—and long live the people's liberator! There was a general then, at the beginning of the century, a general with powerful connections and an immensely wealthy landowner, but one of those—admittedly there seem to have been very few even then—who, when they retired from the service, were almost convinced they'd earned the right of life and death over their subjects. Such men did exist then. Well, this general lives on his estate of two thousand souls, swaggering about, treating his less wealthy neighbors as his hangers-on and buffoons. Kennels with hundreds of dogs and nearly a hundred kennelmen, all in uniform, all mounted. And one day a household serf boy, a little boy of only eight, throws a stone while he's playing and hurts the leg of the general's favorite hound. ‘Why has my favorite dog gone lame?’ They report that this very boy, here, threw a stone at her and hurt her leg. ‘Ah, so it was you,’ the general looks him over, ‘take him!’ They take him, take him from his mother, and he spends all night in the lockup. Next morning at first light the general rides out to hunt in full splendor, mounts his horse, and around him are his hangers-on, dogs, kennelmen, huntsmen, all mounted. The household serfs are gathered around for a lesson, and at the very front is the guilty boy's mother. They bring the boy out of the lockup. A gloomy, cold, foggy autumn day, a fine day for hunting. The general orders the boy stripped; they strip the little child stark naked, he trembles, he's out of his mind with fear, doesn't dare make a sound... ‘Send him off!’ commands the general. ‘Run, run!’ the kennelmen shout at him, and the boy runs... ‘Sic him!’ screams the general, and sets his whole pack of borzois on him. He hunted him down before his mother's eyes, and the dogs tore the child to pieces!.. I think the general was placed under guardianship. Well... what should be done with him? Shoot him? Shoot him to satisfy our moral sense? Speak, Alyoshka!”

<!-- B05-C04-P012 -->
“Shoot him!” Alyosha said softly, lifting his eyes to his brother with a pale, strangely twisted smile.

<!-- B05-C04-P013 -->
“Bravo!” Ivan cried in a kind of ecstasy. “If even you've said it, that means... Some monk of the strictest rule! So that's the little devil hiding in your heart, Alyoshka Karamazov!”

<!-- B05-C04-P014 -->
“What I said was absurd, but...”

<!-- B05-C04-P015 -->
“Exactly, but...” Ivan cried. “Know this, novice: absurdities are all too necessary on earth. The world rests on absurdities, and without them perhaps nothing at all would ever happen in it. We know what we know!”

<!-- B05-C04-P016 -->
“What do you know?”

<!-- B05-C04-P017 -->
“I don't understand anything,” Ivan continued, as though delirious, “and now I don't want to understand anything either. I want to stick to the fact. I decided long ago not to understand. If I try to understand anything, I'll immediately betray the fact, and I've decided to stick to the fact...”

<!-- B05-C04-P018 -->
“Why are you testing me?” Alyosha cried in anguished distress. “Will you finally tell me?”

<!-- B05-C04-P019 -->
“Of course I will; that's what I've been leading up to. You're dear to me, I don't want to lose you, and I won't surrender you to your Zosima.”

<!-- B05-C04-P020 -->
Ivan was silent for a minute; his face suddenly grew very sad.

<!-- B05-C04-P021 -->
“Listen to me: I chose only children to make it clearer. I'm not saying another word about all the other human tears that have soaked the earth from its crust to its center. I've deliberately narrowed my subject. I'm a bedbug, and I acknowledge with all humility that I can't understand at all why everything is arranged this way. So people themselves are to blame: they were given paradise, they wanted freedom, and they stole fire from heaven, knowing they would become unhappy, so there's no point pitying them. Oh, with my mind, my pitiful, earthly, Euclidean mind, all I know is that suffering exists, that no one is to blame, that everything follows directly and simply from everything else, that everything flows and finds its balance—but that's only Euclidean nonsense, I know that, and I can't possibly agree to live by it! What good is it to me that no one is to blame, and that I know it? I need retribution, or I'll destroy myself. And retribution not somewhere and sometime in infinity, but here, already on earth, and I must see it myself. I have believed, and I want to see for myself, and if I'm dead by then, let them resurrect me, because if it all happens without me it'll be too insulting. Surely I didn't suffer so that I, my crimes and my suffering, could manure a future harmony for someone else. I want to see with my own eyes the doe lying down beside the lion, and the murdered man rising and embracing his murderer. I want to be there when everyone suddenly learns why it all happened as it did. All the religions on earth rest on this desire, and I believe. But then there are the children, and what am I to do with them then? That's a question I can't resolve. For the hundredth time I repeat: there are many questions, but I chose only children, because here what I need to say is irrefutably clear. Listen: if everyone must suffer to buy eternal harmony with their suffering, what have children got to do with it, tell me, please? It's utterly incomprehensible why they too should have had to suffer, why they should buy harmony with their suffering. Why were they too made into raw material, why did they manure a future harmony for someone else with themselves? I understand solidarity among people in sin, I understand solidarity in retribution too, but surely not solidarity in sin with children, and if the truth really is that they too share with their fathers in all their fathers' crimes, then of course that truth is not of this world and is beyond my understanding. Some joker may say the child would have grown up anyway and had time to sin, but this one didn't grow up, he was hunted down by dogs at eight years old. Oh, Alyosha, I'm not blaspheming! I understand what an upheaval of the universe there must be when everything in heaven and under the earth merges into a single voice of praise, and everything living and that ever lived cries out: ‘Just are you, Lord, for your ways have been revealed!’ When the mother embraces the torturer who had her son torn apart by dogs, and all three proclaim through their tears, ‘Just are you, Lord,’ then of course the crown of knowledge will have come and everything will be explained. But there's the snag, that's what I can't accept. And while I'm still on earth, I hurry to take my precautions. You see, Alyosha, perhaps it really will happen that when I myself live to see that moment, or am resurrected to see it, I too may cry out with everyone else, looking at the mother embracing her child's torturer, ‘Just are you, Lord!’ But I don't want to cry out then. While there's still time, I hurry to protect myself, and so I utterly renounce that higher harmony. It isn't worth even one little tear of that tortured child who beat her chest with her little fist and prayed to ‘dear God’ in her stinking hole with her unredeemed little tears! It isn't worth it, because her little tears remain unredeemed. They must be redeemed, otherwise there can be no harmony. But how, how will you redeem them? Is that even possible? By avenging them? But what do I want with vengeance for them, what do I want with a hell for the torturers? What can hell put right here, when the children have already been tortured? And what sort of harmony can there be with a hell? I want to forgive, I want to embrace, I don't want any more suffering. And if children's suffering went to make up the sum of suffering needed to buy the truth, then I declare in advance that all the truth there is isn't worth that price. Finally, I don't want the mother to embrace the torturer who had her son torn apart by dogs! She has no right to forgive him! If she wants, let her forgive him for herself, let her forgive the torturer her own immeasurable suffering as a mother; but she has no right to forgive the suffering of her torn-apart child, she has no right to forgive the torturer even if the child himself forgave him for it! And if that's so, if they have no right to forgive, where's the harmony? Is there a being in the whole world who could forgive and would have the right to? I don't want harmony, out of love for humanity I don't want it. I'd rather remain with suffering unavenged. I'd rather keep my unavenged suffering and my unappeased indignation, even if I'm wrong. Besides, they've set too high a price on harmony; we can't possibly afford to pay so much for admission. And so I hurry to return my admission ticket. And if I'm an honest man, I'm obliged to return it as far in advance as possible. That's what I'm doing. It's not God I don't accept, Alyosha; I just most respectfully return him the ticket.”

<!-- B05-C04-P022 -->
“That's rebellion,” Alyosha said softly, his eyes lowered.

<!-- B05-C04-P023 -->
“Rebellion? I wouldn't have wanted that word from you,” Ivan said feelingly. “Can one live by rebellion? And I want to live. Tell me yourself, directly—I call on you to answer: imagine that you're building the edifice of human destiny, with the aim of making people happy in the end, of finally giving them peace and rest, but to do it you would necessarily and inevitably have to torture just one tiny creature to death, that very child beating her chest with her little fist, and found your edifice on her unavenged little tears. Would you agree to be the architect on those terms? Tell me, and don't lie!”

<!-- B05-C04-P024 -->
“No, I wouldn't,” Alyosha said softly.

<!-- B05-C04-P025 -->
“And can you accept the idea that the people you're building it for would themselves agree to accept their happiness at the cost of the unjustified bloodshed of that little tortured child, and, having accepted it, remain happy forever?”

<!-- B05-C04-P026 -->
“No, I can't accept that. Brother,” Alyosha said suddenly, his eyes flashing, “you just said, is there a being in the whole world who could forgive and would have the right to? But there is such a being, and he can forgive everything, everyone and everything, and for everything, because he himself gave his innocent blood for everyone and everything. You've forgotten him, but it's on him that the edifice is founded, and it's to him they'll cry out, ‘Just are you, Lord, for your ways have been revealed.’”

<!-- B05-C04-P027 -->
“Ah, the ‘only sinless one’ and his blood! No, I haven't forgotten him; on the contrary, all this time I've been wondering why you were taking so long to bring him out, because usually in arguments all your people produce him first of all. You know, Alyosha, don't laugh, but I composed a poem once, about a year ago. If you can waste another ten minutes on me, shall I tell it to you?”

<!-- B05-C04-P028 -->
“You wrote a poem?”

<!-- B05-C04-P029 -->
“Oh no, I didn't write it down,” Ivan laughed, “and I've never composed even two lines of verse in my life. But I imagined this poem and remembered it. Imagined it in a fervor. You'll be my first reader, or rather, listener. Why should an author lose even a single listener, after all?” Ivan smiled. “Shall I tell it or not?”

<!-- B05-C04-P030 -->
“I'm listening very closely,” Alyosha said.

<!-- B05-C04-P031 -->
“My poem is called ‘The Grand Inquisitor.’ It's an absurd thing, but I want to share it with you.”
