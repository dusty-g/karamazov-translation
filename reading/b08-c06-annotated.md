# Book VIII — Mitya
## VI. Here I Come!

<!-- B08-C06-P001 -->
Meanwhile Dmitri Fyodorovich was flying down the road. Mokroye was a little over twenty versts away, but Andrei’s troika galloped at a pace that could cover it in an hour and a quarter. The fast drive seemed suddenly to revive Mitya. The air was fresh and rather cold; large stars shone in the clear sky. It was the same night, perhaps the very same hour, when Alyosha fell to the earth and “ecstatically swore to love it forever and ever.” But Mitya’s soul was troubled, deeply troubled, and though many things were tormenting it now, at that moment his whole being was irresistibly drawn toward her alone, toward his queen, whom he was flying to see for the last time. I will say only this: his heart did not argue even for a moment. Perhaps no one will believe me if I say that this jealous man felt not the slightest jealousy of this new man, this new rival who had sprung up out of the ground, this “officer.” Had anyone else appeared, he would immediately have been jealous and might once more have drenched his terrible hands in blood; but toward this man, toward “her first,” as he flew along now in his troika he felt neither jealous hatred nor even hostility—though, to be sure, he had not yet seen him. “There’s no disputing this; it’s her right and his. This is her first love, whom she hasn’t forgotten in five years: that means she’s loved only him all these five years. And I—why did I come along? What am I doing here, what have I to do with it? Step aside, Mitya, and make way! And what am I now anyway? Now it’s all over even without the officer; even if he hadn’t appeared at all, everything would still be over…”

<!-- B08-C06-P002 -->
Those are approximately the words in which he might have expressed his feelings, if only he had been capable of reasoning. But by then he could no longer reason. His present resolve had been born without reasoning, in a single instant; he had felt it at once and accepted it whole, with all its consequences, earlier at Fenya’s, from her very first words. Yet for all the resolve he had embraced, his soul remained troubled, troubled to the point of anguish: even resolve had brought no peace. Too much stood behind him, tormenting him. At moments this seemed strange to him: he had already written his own sentence with pen on paper—“I execute myself and punish myself”—and the paper lay here, in his pocket, ready; the pistol was already loaded; he had already decided how he would meet the first hot ray of “golden-haired Phoebus” tomorrow. And yet there was no settling accounts with the past, with everything that stood behind him and tormented him. He felt that agonizingly, and the thought pierced his soul with despair. At one moment on the road he suddenly wanted to stop Andrei, jump out of the cart, take out his loaded pistol, and end it all without even waiting for dawn. But the moment flew past like a spark. And the troika too flew on, “devouring space,” and as he drew nearer his destination, the thought of her, of her alone, seized his spirit more and more powerfully once again, driving all the other terrible phantoms from his heart. Oh, how he wanted to look at her, if only for an instant, if only from a distance! “She’s with him now, so I’ll see what she’s like with him now, with her old love, and that’s all I need.” Never yet had so much love risen from his breast for this woman who had been fatal to his destiny, so much new feeling, feeling he had never known, unexpected even to himself, feeling tender to the point of worship, to the point of vanishing before her. “And I shall vanish!” he suddenly said in a fit of almost hysterical ecstasy.

<!-- B08-C06-P003 -->
They had been galloping for nearly an hour. Mitya was silent, and Andrei, though a talkative peasant, had not uttered a word either, as though afraid to speak; he only drove his “nags” briskly on, his lean but spirited troika of bays. Suddenly Mitya exclaimed in terrible anxiety:

<!-- B08-C06-P004 -->
“Andrei! What if they’re asleep?”

<!-- B08-C06-P005 -->
The thought had suddenly struck him; until then it had not occurred to him.

<!-- B08-C06-P006 -->
“I should think they’ve gone to bed by now, Dmitri Fyodorovich.”

<!-- B08-C06-P007 -->
Mitya frowned painfully. What if he really did fly there… with such feelings… and they were asleep… and she was asleep too, perhaps right there… An angry feeling boiled up in his heart.

<!-- B08-C06-P008 -->
“Drive on, Andrei, drive, Andrei, faster!” he cried in a frenzy.

<!-- B08-C06-P009 -->
“Or perhaps they haven’t gone to bed yet,” Andrei reasoned after a pause. “Timofey said earlier there was quite a crowd gathered there…”

<!-- B08-C06-P010 -->
“At the posting station?”

<!-- B08-C06-P011 -->
“Not the station, at the Plastunovs’, at the inn—a private station, you might say.”

<!-- B08-C06-P012 -->
“I know. But what do you mean, a crowd? Where’s a crowd? Who are they?” Mitya started at the unexpected news, terribly anxious.

<!-- B08-C06-P013 -->
“Well, Timofey said they were all gentlemen: two from town, who they are I don’t know, but Timofey said two of the local gentlemen, and those two who’d apparently come from elsewhere. Perhaps there are others too; I didn’t ask him properly. They’d started playing cards, he said.”

<!-- B08-C06-P014 -->
“Cards?”

<!-- B08-C06-P015 -->
“So perhaps they’re not asleep if they’ve started on cards. I reckon it’s only getting on for eleven now, no later.”

<!-- B08-C06-P016 -->
“Drive on, Andrei, drive on!” Mitya cried nervously again.

<!-- B08-C06-P017 -->
“Let me ask you something, sir,” Andrei began again after a silence. “Only I’m afraid of angering you, sir.”

<!-- B08-C06-P018 -->
“What is it?”

<!-- B08-C06-P019 -->
“Earlier Fedosya Markovna fell at your feet, begging you not to destroy her mistress and someone else… Well, sir, here I am driving you there… Forgive me, sir, it’s just my conscience; perhaps I’ve said something foolish.”

<!-- B08-C06-P020 -->
Mitya suddenly seized him by the shoulders from behind.

<!-- B08-C06-P021 -->
“You’re a driver? A driver?” he began in a frenzy.

<!-- B08-C06-P022 -->
“A driver…”

<!-- B08-C06-P023 -->
“You know you have to make way. Just because you’re a driver, does that mean you never give way to anyone—run them down, here I come! No, driver, don’t run them down! You mustn’t run a person down, you mustn’t spoil people’s lives; and if you’ve spoiled a life, punish yourself… if only you’ve spoiled it, if only you’ve ruined someone’s life, execute yourself and go.”

<!-- B08-C06-P024 -->
All this burst from Mitya as though in complete hysterics. Andrei was surprised at his master, but kept up the conversation.

<!-- B08-C06-P025 -->
“That’s true, dear Dmitri Fyodorovich, you’re right there: you mustn’t run a person down, or torment them either, or any creature, because every creature is a created creature, even a horse, say, because some will lash away for nothing, even one of our own drivers… And there’s no holding him back, he just charges on, straight on he charges.”

<!-- B08-C06-P026 -->
“Into hell?” Mitya suddenly interrupted, bursting into his unexpected, short laugh. “Andrei, simple soul,” he seized him firmly by the shoulders again, “tell me: will Dmitri Fyodorovich Karamazov end up in hell or not? What do you think?”

<!-- B08-C06-P027 -->
“I don’t know, dear sir, that depends on you, because you’re… You see, sir, when the Son of God was crucified on the cross and died, he went down from the cross straight into hell and freed all the sinners who were suffering there. And hell groaned over it, thinking no sinners would ever come to it anymore. Then the Lord said to hell: ‘Groan not, O hell, for henceforth all manner of grandees, governors, chief judges, and rich men shall come to thee, and thou shalt be filled again just as thou hast been for ever and ever, until the time when I come again.’ That’s certain, those words were spoken…”

<!-- B08-C06-P028 -->
“A folk legend, splendid! Give the left one a stroke, Andrei!”

<!-- B08-C06-P029 -->
“So that’s who hell is meant for, sir,” Andrei said, whipping the left horse. “But you, sir, you’re just like a little child to us… that’s how we look on you… And though you’ve a temper, sir, that’s true, the Lord will forgive you for your simple heart.”

<!-- B08-C06-P030 -->
“And you, will you forgive me, Andrei?”

<!-- B08-C06-P031 -->
“What have I to forgive you for? You’ve done me no harm.”

<!-- B08-C06-P032 -->
“No, for everyone, you alone for everyone, now, this very moment, here on the road—will you forgive me on everyone’s behalf? Speak, soul of the common people!”

<!-- B08-C06-P033 -->
“Oh, sir! I’m afraid even to drive you, you’re talking so strangely…”

<!-- B08-C06-P034 -->
But Mitya did not hear. He was praying frantically, whispering wildly to himself.

<!-- B08-C06-P035 -->
“Lord, receive me in all my lawlessness, but do not judge me. Let me pass without thy judgment… Do not judge, for I have condemned myself; do not judge, for I love thee, Lord! I am vile myself, but I love thee. Send me to hell and I will love thee there, and from there I will cry out that I love thee forever and ever… But let me love to the end… here, now, love to the end, just five hours until thy hot ray… For I love the queen of my soul. I love her and cannot help loving her. Thou seest all of me. I’ll drive up, fall before her: you were right to pass me by… Farewell, and forget your victim, never trouble yourself!”

<!-- B08-C06-P036 -->
“Mokroye!” Andrei called, pointing ahead with his whip.

<!-- B08-C06-P037 -->
Through the pale darkness of the night a solid mass of buildings suddenly showed black, spread over an enormous area. Mokroye was a village of two thousand souls, but at this hour it was all asleep; only here and there a few scattered lights still flickered out of the dark.

<!-- B08-C06-P038 -->
“Drive, drive, Andrei, here I come!” Mitya exclaimed as though feverish.

<!-- B08-C06-P039 -->
“They’re not asleep!” Andrei said again, pointing his whip toward the Plastunovs’ inn, right at the entrance to the village, where all six windows facing the street were brightly lit.

<!-- B08-C06-P040 -->
“Not asleep!” Mitya echoed joyfully. “Make a racket, Andrei, drive at a gallop, ring the bells, come up with a crash. Let everyone know who’s arrived! Here I come! Here I come myself!” Mitya cried in a frenzy.

<!-- B08-C06-P041 -->
Andrei urged the exhausted troika into a gallop and did indeed drive up with a crash to the high front steps, reining in his sweating, half-suffocated horses. Mitya jumped down from the cart, and just then the innkeeper, who was in fact on his way to bed, looked out from the steps, curious to see who had driven up like that.

<!-- B08-C06-P042 -->
“Trifon Borisych, is that you?”

<!-- B08-C06-P043 -->
The innkeeper bent forward, peered, raced down the steps, and rushed toward his guest in servile delight.

<!-- B08-C06-P044 -->
“Dear Dmitri Fedorych! Can we be seeing you again?”

<!-- B08-C06-P045 -->
This Trifon Borisych was a stocky, healthy peasant of medium height, with a rather fleshy face and a stern, unforgiving manner, especially toward the peasants of Mokroye, but with a gift for instantly transforming his face into the most servile expression whenever he smelled a profit. He dressed in Russian fashion, in a side-fastening shirt and fitted coat, and possessed considerable money, but dreamed tirelessly of rising higher still. More than half the peasants were in his claws, all deeply in debt to him. He leased land from the landowners and bought land himself, and the peasants worked it for him to pay off debts they could never escape. He was a widower with four grown daughters. One was already a widow and lived with him with her two young children, his granddaughters, working for him like a day laborer. Another peasant daughter was married to an official, some petty clerk who had risen through the ranks; on the wall in one of the inn’s rooms, among the miniature family photographs, you could see a photograph of this little official too, in uniform with the shoulder straps of his rank. The two younger daughters, on the patronal feast or when going visiting, wore blue or green dresses made in the latest fashion, tight at the back with a yard-long train; but the very next morning, as every day, they rose at first light and, birch brooms in hand, swept the rooms, carried out the slops, and cleared away the guests’ refuse. Despite the thousands he had already amassed, Trifon Borisych dearly loved to fleece a carousing guest. Remembering that less than a month earlier he had made more than two hundred rubles, if not a full three hundred, off Dmitri Fyodorovich in a single day during his spree with Grushenka, he now greeted him with delighted haste, smelling fresh prey from the very way Mitya had driven up to his steps.

<!-- B08-C06-P046 -->
“Dear Dmitri Fyodorovich, have we found you among us again?”

<!-- B08-C06-P047 -->
“Wait, Trifon Borisych,” Mitya began, “first, the most important thing: where is she?”

<!-- B08-C06-P048 -->
“Agrafena Alexandrovna?” the innkeeper understood at once, looking keenly into Mitya’s face. “Why, she’s staying here too.”

<!-- B08-C06-P049 -->
“With whom, with whom?”

<!-- B08-C06-P050 -->
“Guests passing through, sir… One’s an official, must be a Pole, judging by his speech. He’s the one who sent the horses for her from here. The other’s a friend of his or a traveling companion, who can tell? They’re dressed as civilians…”

<!-- B08-C06-P051 -->
“Well, are they carousing? Are they rich?”

<!-- B08-C06-P052 -->
“Carousing, indeed! Small fry, Dmitri Fyodorovich.”

<!-- B08-C06-P053 -->
“Small? And the others?”

<!-- B08-C06-P054 -->
“They’re from town, two gentlemen… Coming back from Chernye, and they stayed… One, the young one, must be a relation of Mr. Miusov’s, only I’ve forgotten his name… And the other I suppose you know too: the landowner Maximov. Says he came to your monastery there on a pilgrimage, and now he’s traveling with this young relation of Mr. Miusov’s…”

<!-- B08-C06-P055 -->
“Is that everyone?”

<!-- B08-C06-P056 -->
“Everyone.”

<!-- B08-C06-P057 -->
“Wait, be quiet, Trifon Borisych, now tell me the most important thing: what about her, how is she?”

<!-- B08-C06-P058 -->
“Well, she arrived a while ago, and she’s sitting with them.”

<!-- B08-C06-P059 -->
“Is she cheerful? Is she laughing?”

<!-- B08-C06-P060 -->
“No, doesn’t seem to be laughing much… She’s sitting there quite bored, even. She was combing the young gentleman’s hair.”

<!-- B08-C06-P061 -->
“The Pole’s, the officer’s?”

<!-- B08-C06-P062 -->
“He’s hardly young, and he’s no officer at all. No, sir, not him, this young nephew of Miusov’s… only I’ve forgotten the name.”

<!-- B08-C06-P063 -->
“Kalganov?”

<!-- B08-C06-P064 -->
“That’s it, Kalganov.”

<!-- B08-C06-P065 -->
“Good, I’ll decide for myself. Are they playing cards?”

<!-- B08-C06-P066 -->
“They were, but they stopped. They’ve finished their tea, and the official asked for fruit liqueur.”

<!-- B08-C06-P067 -->
“Wait, Trifon Borisych, wait, my dear fellow, I’ll decide for myself. Now answer the most important thing: are there no Gypsies?”

<!-- B08-C06-P068 -->
“No sign of any Gypsies now, Dmitri Fyodorovich; the authorities drove them off. But there are Yids here who play the dulcimer and the fiddle, in Rozhdestvenskaya. We could send for them even now. They’ll come.”

<!-- B08-C06-P069 -->
“Send for them, certainly send for them!” Mitya cried. “And can you rouse the girls like last time, Marya especially, and Stepanida, Arina? Two hundred rubles for the choir!”

<!-- B08-C06-P070 -->
“For that money I’ll rouse the whole village for you, though they’ve gone off snoring now. But are the peasants here worth such kindness, dear Dmitri Fyodorovich, or the girls either? To set aside such a sum for such low, coarse creatures! Is our peasant fit to smoke cigars? Yet you gave them some. The scoundrel stinks, he does. And the girls, every last one, are crawling with lice. I’ll rouse my own daughters for you for nothing, never mind a sum like that. They’ve only just gone to bed, but I’ll kick them in the back and make them sing for you. Giving peasants champagne the other day, ah!”

<!-- B08-C06-P071 -->
Trifon Borisych wasted his pity on Mitya: he himself had kept back about half a dozen bottles of champagne from him that time, and picked up a hundred-ruble note under the table and closed his fist around it. And in his fist it had remained.

<!-- B08-C06-P072 -->
“Trifon Borisych, I scattered more than one thousand here that time. Remember?”

<!-- B08-C06-P073 -->
“You did, dear sir. How could we forget you? You left three thousand with us, I daresay.”

<!-- B08-C06-P074 -->
“Well, that’s what I’ve come for again now, see?”

<!-- B08-C06-P075 -->
He took out his bundle of notes and held it right under the innkeeper’s nose.

<!-- B08-C06-P076 -->
“Now listen and understand: in an hour the wine will come, and the snacks, pies, and sweets—everything straight upstairs. This crate Andrei has, take that straight upstairs too, open it, and serve champagne at once. Above all, the girls, the girls, and Marya without fail.”

<!-- B08-C06-P077 -->
He turned to the cart and pulled his pistol case from under the seat.

<!-- B08-C06-P078 -->
“Time to pay, Andrei, here you are! Fifteen rubles for the troika, and fifty for a drink… for your willingness, for your love… Remember your gentleman Karamazov!”

<!-- B08-C06-P079 -->
“I’m afraid, sir…” Andrei hesitated. “Give me five rubles for a tip, but I won’t take any more. Trifon Borisych is my witness. Forgive my foolish words…”

<!-- B08-C06-P080 -->
“What are you afraid of?” Mitya looked him up and down. “Well, to hell with you, then!” he cried, throwing him five rubles. “Now, Trifon Borisych, take me in quietly and let me have a peep at them all first, so they don’t notice me. Where are they, in the blue room?”

<!-- B08-C06-P081 -->
Trifon Borisych looked at Mitya apprehensively, but immediately obeyed: he led him cautiously into the entrance hall, went himself into the large first room, next to the one where the guests were sitting, and carried out the candle. Then he quietly brought Mitya in and stationed him in a dark corner from which he could easily examine the company without being seen. But Mitya did not look long, nor could he study them: he saw her, and his heart began pounding, his vision clouded. She was sitting at the side of the table in an armchair, and beside her on the sofa was the handsome and still very young Kalganov. She held his hand and seemed to be laughing, while he, without looking at her, was saying something loudly, apparently in vexation, to Maximov, who sat across the table opposite Grushenka. Maximov was laughing very hard at something. He sat on a sofa, and beside it, on a chair against the wall, sat another stranger. The one lounging on the sofa was smoking a pipe, and Mitya caught only a fleeting impression of a rather stout, broad-faced little man, probably short, who seemed angry about something. His companion, the other stranger, struck Mitya as extraordinarily tall; but he could make out nothing more. His breath caught. He could not remain there even a minute. He put the case on a chest of drawers and, cold and faint, walked straight into the blue room toward the company.

<!-- B08-C06-P082 -->
“Ah!” Grushenka shrieked in fright, the first to notice him.
