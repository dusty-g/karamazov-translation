# Book XII: A Judicial Error
## I. The Fatal Day

<!-- B12-C01-P001 -->
At ten o’clock on the morning after the events I have described, our district court opened its session, and Dmitri Karamazov’s trial began.

<!-- B12-C01-P002 -->
Let me say at once, and say emphatically: I am far from considering myself capable of recounting everything that happened at the trial, not only with the proper fullness but even in the proper order. It seems to me that remembering everything and explaining it all as it should be explained would take a whole book, and a very large one at that. So please do not hold it against me if I relate only what struck me personally and stayed particularly in my memory. I may have taken something secondary for the most important thing, or even missed the most striking and essential features altogether… Still, I see that I had better stop apologizing. I shall do what I can, and readers will understand for themselves that I have done only what I could.

<!-- B12-C01-P003 -->
First of all, before we enter the courtroom, I must mention what particularly astonished me that day. Not just me, for that matter, but, as it later turned out, everyone. It was this: we all knew that the case had aroused the interest of a great many people, that everyone was burning with impatience for the trial to begin, that for two whole months our society had done nothing but talk, speculate, exclaim, and dream about it. We also knew that the case had become known throughout Russia, but none of us had imagined the burning, feverish excitement it had stirred in every single person, and not only in our town but everywhere, as became clear at the trial that day. Visitors had come for it not only from our provincial capital but from several other Russian towns, and finally from Moscow and Petersburg as well. Lawyers had come, and even several eminent personages, and ladies too. Every ticket had been snapped up. For the most distinguished and eminent male visitors, quite unprecedented seats had actually been provided behind the table where the court sat: a whole row of armchairs appeared there, occupied by various personages, something never permitted with us before. There were particularly large numbers of ladies, both local and visiting—at least half the entire audience, I should think. The lawyers alone, who had arrived from every direction, were so numerous that no one knew where to put them, since all the tickets had long since been given out, begged, and pleaded for. I saw for myself how a special enclosure was hastily improvised at the end of the hall, behind the platform, and all these visiting lawyers admitted to it; they counted themselves fortunate even to be able to stand there, for the chairs had all been removed to save space, and the entire crowd stood through the whole “case” in one densely packed mass, shoulder to shoulder. Some of the ladies, particularly the visitors, appeared in the galleries magnificently dressed, but most had forgotten even about their clothes. Their faces betrayed a hysterical, greedy, almost morbid curiosity. One of the most characteristic features of this gathering, and one that must be noted, was that almost all the ladies—at any rate, the overwhelming majority—were on Mitya’s side and wanted an acquittal, as numerous observations afterward confirmed. Perhaps this was chiefly because they had formed an image of him as a conqueror of women’s hearts. They knew that two women, rivals, would appear. One of them, Katerina Ivanovna, excited particular interest; a tremendous number of extraordinary things were said about her, and astonishing stories circulated about her passion for Mitya even despite his crime. Her pride was especially remarked on—she had paid calls on scarcely anyone in our town—as were her “aristocratic connections.” People said she intended to petition the government for permission to accompany the criminal to penal servitude and marry him somewhere underground in the mines. Grushenka’s appearance in court, as Katerina Ivanovna’s rival, was awaited with no less excitement. People looked forward with tormenting curiosity to the courtroom encounter between the two rivals: the proud aristocratic young lady and the “courtesan.” Our ladies knew Grushenka better, however, than they knew Katerina Ivanovna. They had seen this “destroyer of Fyodor Pavlovich and his unfortunate son” before, and almost every one of them wondered how both father and son could have fallen so desperately in love with “the most ordinary Russian tradeswoman, not even good-looking.” In short, there was plenty of talk. I know for a fact that several serious family quarrels took place in our town on Mitya’s account. Many ladies had bitter disputes with their husbands over their differing views of the whole dreadful affair, and it was only natural afterward that all these husbands should arrive in court not merely ill-disposed toward the defendant but positively embittered against him. Indeed, it could be stated with certainty that, in contrast to the ladies, the entire male contingent was against the defendant. Stern, frowning faces were visible everywhere, some of them downright hostile. Admittedly, Mitya had managed to offend many of these men personally during his time among us. Some of the spectators were almost cheerful, of course, and quite indifferent to Mitya’s fate itself, though again by no means indifferent to the case being heard. Everyone was concerned about its outcome, and most of the men decidedly wanted the criminal punished, except perhaps the lawyers, who cared about the case’s modern legal aspects, so to speak, rather than its moral ones. Everyone was excited by the arrival of the famous Fetyukovich. His talent was known everywhere, and this was not his first appearance in the provinces as counsel in a sensational criminal case. After his defense, such cases invariably became famous throughout Russia and were remembered for a long time. Several stories were also circulating about our prosecutor and the presiding judge. It was said that our prosecutor was trembling at the prospect of facing Fetyukovich, that they were old enemies from Petersburg, from the very beginning of their careers; that our proud Ippolit Kirillovich, who ever since his Petersburg days had felt someone was always slighting him by failing to appreciate his talents properly, had taken new heart over the Karamazov case and even dreamed of reviving his withered career through it, but that Fetyukovich alone frightened him. These judgments about his fear of Fetyukovich were not quite fair, however. Our prosecutor was not the sort of man to lose heart in the face of danger; on the contrary, his pride grew and took wing precisely as the danger increased. In general, though, it must be said that our prosecutor was excessively ardent and painfully sensitive. Into certain cases he put his whole soul, conducting them as though his entire fate and everything he possessed depended on their outcome. Legal circles laughed at this a little, for it was this very quality that had won our prosecutor a certain reputation, if far from universal, then much wider than his modest position in our court would have led one to expect. His passion for psychology was a particular object of amusement. In my view they were all mistaken: as a man and a character, our prosecutor seems to me to have been far more substantial than many people thought. But from his very first steps at the start of his career, and throughout his life afterward, this sickly man had never known how to establish himself in others’ eyes.

As for our presiding judge, all one can say is that he was an educated, humane man, with a practical knowledge of his work and the most modern ideas. He had considerable pride, but did not trouble himself much about his career. His chief aim in life was to be a progressive man. Besides, he had connections and money. As it emerged later, he took a fairly keen interest in the Karamazov case, but only in a general sense. What interested him was the phenomenon, its classification, its interpretation as a product of our social foundations, as an expression of the Russian element, and so on and so forth. Toward the personal side of the case, its tragedy, and the individual people involved, beginning with the defendant, his attitude was rather indifferent and abstract—as perhaps, indeed, it ought to have been.

<!-- B12-C01-P005 -->
Long before the court appeared, the hall was packed. Our courtroom is the finest hall in town: spacious, lofty, and resonant. To the right of the members of the court, who sat on a slightly raised platform, a table and two rows of armchairs had been arranged for the jury. To the left were the places for the defendant and his counsel. In the middle of the hall, near the court’s platform, stood a table holding the “material evidence.” On it lay Fyodor Pavlovich’s bloodstained white silk dressing gown; the fatal brass pestle with which the murder was presumed to have been committed; Mitya’s shirt with its bloodstained sleeve; his frock coat, covered in bloodstains at the back where the pocket was, into which he had thrust his handkerchief when it was soaked with blood; the handkerchief itself, stiff with blood and now quite yellow; the pistol Mitya had loaded at Perkhotin’s to kill himself, which Trifon Borisovich had quietly taken away from him at Mokroye; the inscribed envelope in which the three thousand had been set aside for Grushenka, and the thin pink ribbon tied around it; and many other objects I cannot remember. A little farther back in the hall the public seating began, but before the railing stood several armchairs for those witnesses who, after giving their testimony, would be allowed to remain. At ten o’clock the court appeared: the presiding judge, one other member, and an honorary justice of the peace. The prosecutor, of course, appeared at once as well. The presiding judge was a stout, stocky man of below average height, about fifty, with the complexion of a hemorrhoid sufferer, short dark hair streaked with gray, and the red sash of some order—I no longer remember which. The prosecutor, however, seemed to me, and not only to me but to everyone, extraordinarily pale, his face almost green, and somehow suddenly thinner, perhaps all in a single night, for I had seen him only the day before yesterday looking quite himself. The presiding judge began by asking the court bailiff whether all the jurors had arrived… But I see that I cannot go on like this, if only because there was much I failed to hear, other things I neglected to take in, and still others slipped my memory; and chiefly because, as I have already said, if I try to recall everything that was said and everything that happened, I shall quite literally have neither the time nor the space. I know only that not very many jurors were challenged by either side, that is, by the defense counsel or the prosecutor. I do remember the composition of the twelve: four of our officials, two merchants, and six peasants and townsmen. Long before the trial, I remember, people in our society, especially the ladies, had been asking in some surprise: “Can such a subtle, complicated, psychological case really be left to the fateful decision of a few officials and, finally, peasants? What could some official understand about it, let alone a peasant?” Indeed, the four officials selected for the jury were all insignificant men of low rank and gray hair—only one was a little younger—little known in our society, eking out a living on small salaries, presumably with aging wives they could never take anywhere, and a heap of children each, perhaps even barefoot ones; men whose leisure amusements amounted at most to a game of cards somewhere, and who had certainly never read a single book. The two merchants, though respectable in appearance, were strangely silent and motionless; one was clean-shaven and dressed in the German fashion, while the other, with a small gray beard, wore some medal around his neck on a red ribbon. As for the townsmen and peasants, there is nothing to say. Our Skotoprigonyevsk townsmen are practically peasants themselves; they even plow the land. Two of them also wore German dress, which perhaps made them look dirtier and more unsightly than the other four. So the thought might indeed occur to one, as it did to me, for instance, the moment I had looked them over: “What can people like these grasp in a case like this?” Nevertheless, their stern, frowning faces made a strangely imposing, almost threatening impression.

At last the presiding judge announced that the case concerning the murder of the retired titular councilor Fyodor Pavlovich Karamazov would be heard—I do not fully remember how he worded it. The bailiff was instructed to bring in the defendant, and Mitya appeared. The whole hall fell silent; you could have heard a fly. I do not know about anyone else, but Mitya’s appearance made a most unpleasant impression on me. Above all, he appeared dressed like an outrageous dandy, in a brand-new frock coat. I learned afterward that he had specially ordered a coat for this day from his former tailor in Moscow, who still had his measurements. He wore brand-new black kid gloves and elegant linen. He came forward with his long, yard-long strides, staring straight ahead without moving his eyes, and sat down in his place looking utterly fearless. His counsel, the celebrated Fetyukovich, appeared immediately afterward, and a sort of suppressed murmur swept through the hall. He was a tall, spare man with long, thin legs, extraordinarily long, pale, slender fingers, a clean-shaven face, fairly short hair modestly combed, and thin lips that occasionally twisted into something between a sneer and a smile. He looked about forty. His face would have been pleasant but for his eyes, small and inexpressive in themselves, but set so extraordinarily close together that only the thin bone of his long, narrow nose divided them. In short, there was something strikingly birdlike about his face. He wore a tailcoat and a white tie. I remember the presiding judge’s first questions to Mitya, about his name, rank, and so forth. Mitya answered abruptly and with such unexpected loudness that the judge actually shook his head and looked at him almost in surprise. Then the list of those summoned for the examination at trial, witnesses and experts, was read out. It was a long list. Four witnesses had failed to appear: Miusov, now in Paris, whose deposition from the preliminary investigation was available; Madame Khokhlakov and the landowner Maximov, owing to illness; and Smerdyakov, owing to his sudden death, for which a police certificate was produced. The news about Smerdyakov caused a great stir and much whispering in the hall. Many in the audience, of course, knew nothing as yet about this unexpected suicide. But what particularly astonished everyone was Mitya’s sudden outburst: the moment Smerdyakov’s death was reported, he cried out from his seat for the whole hall to hear:

<!-- B12-C01-P007 -->
“A dog’s death for a dog!”

<!-- B12-C01-P008 -->
I remember how his counsel rushed over to him, and how the presiding judge threatened to take severe measures if such an outburst occurred again. Mitya nodded and several times repeated to his counsel in a low, abrupt voice, though seemingly without the least remorse:

<!-- B12-C01-P009 -->
“I won’t, I won’t! It slipped out! I won’t do it again!”

<!-- B12-C01-P010 -->
Certainly this brief episode did him no favors in the eyes of either the jury or the audience. His character was making itself known and speaking for itself. With this impression still fresh, the clerk of the court read the indictment.

<!-- B12-C01-P011 -->
It was fairly brief but thorough. It set out only the principal reasons why such-and-such a person had been charged, why he had to be committed for trial, and so on. Nevertheless, it made a powerful impression on me. The clerk read clearly, sonorously, distinctly. It was as though the whole tragedy had appeared before everyone anew, in sharp relief, gathered into a single focus, lit by a fatal, merciless light. I remember how, immediately after the reading, the presiding judge asked Mitya in a loud, imposing voice:

<!-- B12-C01-P012 -->
“Defendant, do you plead guilty?”

<!-- B12-C01-P013 -->
Mitya suddenly rose from his seat.

<!-- B12-C01-P014 -->
“I admit I’m guilty of drunkenness and debauchery,” he cried, again in an unexpected, almost frenzied voice, “of idleness and brawling. I wanted to become an honest man forever at the very moment fate struck me down! But of the old man’s death, the death of my enemy and my father—I’m not guilty! And of robbing him—no, no, I’m not guilty, and I could never be guilty: Dmitri Karamazov is a scoundrel, but he’s no thief!”

<!-- B12-C01-P015 -->
Having shouted this, he sat down, visibly trembling all over. The presiding judge addressed another brief but instructive admonition to him: he was to answer only the questions asked, without launching into irrelevant, frenzied exclamations. Then he ordered the examination of the evidence to begin. All the witnesses were brought in to take the oath. Here I saw them all together. The defendant’s brothers, however, were permitted to testify without being sworn. After the priest and the presiding judge had exhorted them, the witnesses were led away and seated separately as far as possible. Then they began to be called in one by one.
