# Book XII: A Judicial Error

## XIII. An Adulterer of Thought

<!-- B12-C13-P001 -->
“It is not merely the accumulation of facts that is destroying my client, gentlemen of the jury,” he proclaimed. “No, in truth one fact alone is destroying my client: the old father’s corpse! Had this been an ordinary murder, given the insignificance, the lack of proof, the fanciful nature of the facts when considered individually rather than together, you would have rejected the accusation, or at least hesitated to destroy a man’s life solely through prejudice against him—a prejudice which, alas, he has earned! But this is no ordinary murder: it is parricide! That impresses us, and to such a degree that even the insignificance and lack of proof in the incriminating facts cease to seem so insignificant or so unproven, even to the most unprejudiced mind. How can such a defendant be acquitted? What if he killed him and goes unpunished—that is what everyone feels in his heart almost involuntarily, instinctively. Yes, it is a terrible thing to shed a father’s blood—the blood of the man who gave me life, who loved me, who would not spare his own life for me, who suffered through my illnesses from my childhood, who spent his whole life suffering for my happiness and lived only through my joys, my successes! Oh, to kill such a father—it is impossible even to imagine! Gentlemen of the jury, what is a father, a true father? What a great word this is, what an awesomely great idea lies in that name! We have just indicated, in part, what a true father is and should be. But in the present case, which occupies us all so deeply, which makes our souls ache—in the present case the father, the late Fyodor Pavlovich Karamazov, did not in the least fit that conception of a father which has just spoken to our hearts. That is a calamity. Yes, indeed, some fathers resemble a calamity. Let us examine this calamity more closely—we must fear nothing, gentlemen of the jury, in view of the importance of the decision before us. Now in particular we must not be afraid or, so to speak, wave away certain ideas like children or timid women, to borrow the highly talented prosecutor’s apt phrase. Yet in his heated speech my respected opponent—and my opponent even before I had spoken my first word—exclaimed several times: ‘No, I shall let no one defend the accused; I shall not surrender his defense to the counsel who has come from Petersburg—I am the prosecutor, and I am the defender too!’ That is what he exclaimed several times, yet he forgot to mention that if this terrible defendant remained so grateful for a full twenty-three years for a single pound of nuts given him by the one person who had shown him kindness as a child in his father’s house, then conversely such a man could hardly have failed to remember, through all those twenty-three years, how he ran barefoot at his father’s, ‘in the back yard, without little boots, and with his little trousers held up by one button,’ in the words of the humane Dr. Herzenstube. Oh, gentlemen of the jury, why examine this ‘calamity’ more closely, repeat what everyone already knows! What did my client encounter when he came here to his father? And why, why portray my client as unfeeling, an egoist, a monster? He is unrestrained, he is wild and violent, and now we judge him for it; but who is to blame for his fate, who is to blame that, despite his good inclinations, his noble and sensitive heart, he received such an absurd upbringing? Did anyone teach him sense, was he instructed in the sciences, did anyone love him at all in his childhood? My client grew up in God’s care—that is, like a wild beast. Perhaps he longed to see his father after their many years apart; perhaps a thousand times before, recalling his childhood as through a dream, he had driven away the loathsome phantoms that had haunted his childhood dreams and longed with all his soul to justify and embrace his father! And what happened? He was met with nothing but cynical mockery, suspicion, and legal trickery over disputed money; he heard only conversations and rules for living that turned his heart, daily ‘over a little cognac’; and finally he saw his father luring away his mistress from him, the son, with the son’s own money—oh, gentlemen of the jury, this is loathsome and cruel! And this same old man complains to everyone of his son’s disrespect and cruelty, blackens him in society, harms him, slanders him, buys up his promissory notes to put him in prison! Gentlemen of the jury, these souls, these seemingly hardhearted, violent, unrestrained people like my client, can be—and most often are—extraordinarily tenderhearted, only they do not show it. Do not laugh, do not laugh at my idea! The talented prosecutor laughed mercilessly at my client earlier, pointing out that he loves Schiller, loves ‘the beautiful and sublime.’ In his place, in the prosecutor’s place, I would not laugh at that! Yes, these hearts—oh, let me defend these hearts, so rarely and so unjustly understood—these hearts very often thirst for tenderness, beauty, and justice, precisely as though in opposition to themselves, to their violence, their cruelty; they thirst unconsciously, but thirst they do. Passionate and cruel on the outside, they are capable of loving, for example, a woman to the point of torment, and with a spiritual and exalted love. Again, do not laugh at me: this is precisely what most often happens in such natures! They merely cannot conceal their passion, at times very coarse—that is what strikes people, that is what people notice, while the person within remains unseen. On the contrary, all their passions are quickly satisfied, but beside a noble, beautiful being this seemingly coarse and cruel man seeks renewal, seeks the possibility of reforming himself, becoming better, becoming noble and honorable—‘sublime and beautiful,’ however much that phrase may have been mocked! Earlier I said I would not permit myself to touch my client’s romance with Madame Verkhovtsev. Yet half a word may be said: what we heard earlier was not testimony but merely the cry of a frenzied, vengeful woman, and it is not for her, oh, not for her to reproach him with betrayal, because she herself betrayed him! Had she had even a little time to think again, she would not have given such testimony! Oh, do not believe her; no, my client is not the ‘monster’ she called him! The crucified lover of mankind, preparing for his cross, said: ‘I am the good shepherd; the good shepherd giveth his life for the sheep, that not one should perish…’ Let us too not destroy a human soul! I asked just now: what is a father? And I exclaimed that it is a great word, a precious name. But words, gentlemen of the jury, must be treated honestly, and I shall take the liberty of calling a thing by its own word, its proper name: a father like the murdered old Karamazov cannot and does not deserve to be called a father. Love for a father which the father has not justified is an absurdity, an impossibility. Love cannot be created out of nothing; only God creates out of nothing. ‘Fathers, provoke not your children,’ the apostle writes from his heart blazing with love. I do not cite these holy words now for my client’s sake; I recall them for all fathers. Who gave me this authority to instruct fathers? No one. But as a human being and a citizen I cry out—vivos voco![^b12-c13-n001] We are on this earth only briefly; we do many bad deeds and speak many bad words. So let us all seize the favorable moment when we are together to say a good word to one another as well. That is what I am doing: while I stand here, I make use of my moment. This rostrum was not granted us by the supreme will for nothing—from it all Russia hears us. I speak not only to the fathers here, but cry out to all fathers: ‘Fathers, provoke not your children!’ Let us first fulfill Christ’s commandment ourselves, and only then permit ourselves to make demands of our children. Otherwise we are not fathers but enemies to our children, and they are not our children but our enemies, and we ourselves have made them our enemies! ‘With what measure ye mete, it shall be measured to you again’—that is not I speaking now, it is the Gospel that commands us to measure by the measure with which others measure to us. How, then, can we blame children if they measure to us by our own measure? Recently in Finland a young woman, a servant, was suspected of secretly having given birth. They began watching her, and in the attic of the house, in a corner behind some bricks, found a chest of hers that no one knew about; they unlocked it and took out the little body of a newborn infant she had killed. In the same chest they found two skeletons of infants she had previously borne and likewise killed at the moment of birth, as she confessed. Gentlemen of the jury, is this a mother to her children? Yes, she bore them, but is she their mother? Would any of us dare pronounce over her the sacred name of mother? Let us be bold, gentlemen of the jury, let us even be daring; indeed, at the present moment we are obliged to be so, and not fear certain words and ideas like Moscow merchants’ wives who are afraid of ‘metal’ and ‘brimstone.’[^b12-c13-n002] No, let us prove instead that the progress of recent years has touched our development too, and say plainly: a man who begets is not yet a father; a father is one who begets and earns the name. Oh, of course there is another meaning, another interpretation of the word ‘father,’ requiring that my father, even if he is a monster, even if he is a villain toward his children, should nevertheless remain my father solely because he begot me. But that meaning is, so to speak, mystical; I do not understand it with my reason and can accept it only through faith, or more exactly, on faith, like much else that I do not understand but that religion nevertheless commands me to believe. In that case, however, let it remain outside the sphere of actual life. In the sphere of actual life, which not only has its own rights but itself imposes great duties—in this sphere, if we wish to be humane, Christians finally, we must and are obliged to put into practice only convictions justified by reason and experience, passed through the crucible of analysis; in a word, to act rationally, not irrationally as in a dream or delirium, so as not to harm a human being, not to torment or destroy a human being. Then, then it will be a truly Christian deed, not merely mystical, but rational and truly humane…”

At this point loud applause began breaking out from many parts of the room, but Fetyukovich even waved his hands, as though begging them not to interrupt and to let him finish. Everyone immediately fell silent. The orator continued:

<!-- B12-C13-P003 -->
“Do you think, gentlemen of the jury, that such questions can pass our children by—let us say, once they are young men, once they are beginning to reason? No, they cannot, and let us not demand impossible restraint from them! The sight of an unworthy father, particularly beside the worthy fathers of other children his own age, involuntarily prompts painful questions in a young man. These questions are answered with an official formula: ‘He begot you, and you are his blood, and therefore you must love him.’ The young man involuntarily begins to think: ‘But did he love me when he begot me?’ he asks, more and more astonished. ‘Did he beget me for my sake? He knew neither me nor even my sex at that moment, that moment of passion perhaps inflamed by wine, and perhaps merely passed on to me a tendency to drunkenness—that is the sum of his benefactions… Why must I love him merely because he begot me, and then did not love me all my life?’ Oh, perhaps these questions seem coarse and cruel to you, but do not demand impossible restraint from a young mind: ‘Drive nature out the door, and it will fly in through the window’; and above all, above all, let us not fear ‘metal’ and ‘brimstone,’ but settle the question as reason and humanity prescribe, not as mystical notions prescribe. How are we to settle it? In this way: let the son stand before his father and ask him directly, with understanding: ‘Father, tell me: why must I love you? Father, prove to me that I must love you.’ And if this father has the strength and ability to answer and prove it to him, then here is a true, normal family, founded not merely on mystical prejudice but on rational, conscious, and strictly humane principles. Otherwise, if the father cannot prove it, that family is at an end at once: he is not his father, and the son gains the freedom and right henceforth to regard his father as a stranger, even as his enemy. Our rostrum, gentlemen of the jury, must be a school of truth and sound ideas!”

<!-- B12-C13-P004 -->
Here the speaker was interrupted by irrepressible, almost frenzied applause. Of course, not the whole courtroom applauded, but half of it certainly did. Fathers and mothers applauded. From above, where the ladies sat, came squeals and shouts. Handkerchiefs waved. The presiding judge began ringing his bell with all his strength. He was visibly irritated by the courtroom’s behavior, but simply did not dare “clear” it, as he had recently threatened: even the dignitaries sitting behind on special chairs, old gentlemen with stars on their tailcoats, were applauding and waving their handkerchiefs at the orator. So when the noise subsided, the presiding judge contented himself with repeating his most stern promise to “clear” the courtroom, and the triumphant, excited Fetyukovich resumed his speech.

“Gentlemen of the jury, you remember that terrible night of which so much has been said even today, when the son climbed over the fence, entered his father’s house, and at last stood face to face with the enemy and tormentor who had begotten him. I insist with all my strength: he did not run there for money at that moment; the charge of robbery is absurd, as I have already explained. Nor did he force his way in to kill him—oh no! Had he had that premeditated intention, he would at least have seen to a weapon beforehand, whereas he seized the brass pestle instinctively, without himself knowing why. Let us grant that he deceived his father with the signals, that he gained admission—I have already said I do not believe this legend for a single moment, but very well, let us suppose it for one moment! Gentlemen of the jury, I swear to you by everything holy: had this been not his father but some unrelated man who had wronged him, he would, after running through the rooms and satisfying himself that the woman was not in the house, have fled headlong without doing his rival any harm; perhaps he would have struck him, pushed him, but that would have been all, because his mind was elsewhere, he had no time, he had to know where she was. But his father, his father—oh, everything was done by the mere sight of his father, the man who had hated him since childhood, his enemy, his tormentor, and now his monstrous rival! Hatred seized him involuntarily, irresistibly; reasoning was impossible: everything rose up in a single instant! It was a fit of madness and derangement, but also a fit of nature, avenging her eternal laws uncontrollably and unconsciously, like everything in nature. Yet even here the murderer did not murder—I affirm it, I proclaim it—no, he merely swung the pestle in disgust and indignation, without wishing to kill, without knowing he would kill. Had that fatal pestle not been in his hands, perhaps he would merely have beaten his father, but would not have killed him. When he ran away he did not know whether the old man he had struck down was dead. Such a killing is not murder. Such a killing is not parricide either. No, the killing of such a father cannot be called parricide. Can such a killing be counted as parricide except through prejudice? But was there, was there really a murder here? I cry out to you again and again from the depths of my soul! Gentlemen of the jury, suppose we condemn him, and he says to himself: ‘These people did nothing for my fate, for my upbringing, for my education, to make me better, to make me a human being. These people did not feed me or give me drink, did not visit me naked in prison, and now these same people have sent me to penal servitude. We are quits; I owe them nothing now, and owe nothing to anyone for ever and ever. They are wicked, and I shall be wicked. They are cruel, and I shall be cruel.’ That is what he will say, gentlemen of the jury! And I swear: by convicting him you will merely relieve him, relieve his conscience; he will curse the blood he has shed, not regret it. At the same time you will destroy the human being still possible within him, for he will remain wicked and blind all his life. But do you wish to punish him terribly, dreadfully, with the most horrible punishment imaginable, yet in such a way as to save and regenerate his soul forever? Then overwhelm him with your mercy! You will see, you will hear his soul shudder in horror: ‘Can I bear this mercy, can so much love be meant for me, am I worthy of it?’ That is what he will exclaim! Oh, I know, I know this heart, this wild but noble heart, gentlemen of the jury. It will bow before your great deed, it thirsts for a great act of love, it will catch fire and rise again forever. There are souls which, in their narrowness, accuse the whole world. But overwhelm this soul with mercy, show it love, and it will curse its deed, for it holds so many seeds of goodness. The soul will expand and see how merciful God is, how beautiful and just human beings are. Remorse and the immeasurable debt that lies before him from that moment will horrify and overwhelm him. And then he will not say: ‘We are quits,’ but: ‘I am guilty before all people and less worthy than anyone.’ In tears of repentance and burning, anguished tenderness he will exclaim: ‘People are better than I am, for they wished not to destroy but to save me!’ Oh, it is so easy for you to perform this act of mercy, since in the absence of any evidence with even the slightest semblance of truth it will be all too hard for you to pronounce: ‘Yes, guilty.’ Better to release ten guilty men than punish one innocent man—do you hear, do you hear that majestic voice from the previous century of our glorious history? Is it for me, insignificant as I am, to remind you that Russian justice is not merely punishment but the salvation of a lost human being? Let other nations have the letter and punishment; we have the spirit and meaning, the salvation and regeneration of the lost. And if so, if Russia and her justice truly are like this, then—forward, Russia! And do not frighten us, oh, do not frighten us with your mad troikas from which all nations turn aside in disgust! Not a mad troika, but the majestic Russian chariot will arrive at its goal solemnly and calmly. My client’s fate is in your hands; in your hands, too, is the fate of our Russian truth. You will save it, you will defend it, you will prove that there are those who can preserve it, that it is in good hands!”

[^b12-c13-n001]: Latin for “I call the living,” the opening words of the epigraph to Schiller’s *Song of the Bell*.
[^b12-c13-n002]: The odd pairing echoes Ostrovsky’s play *Hard Days*, where Moscow merchant women are frightened by unfamiliar words. “Brimstone” renders an archaic word for burning sulfur or pitch.
