# Book V · Pro and Contra
## V. The Grand Inquisitor

<!-- B05-C05-P001 -->
“Even here we can't do without a preface—a literary preface, that is. Pah!” Ivan laughed. “And what sort of author am I! You see, my story takes place in the sixteenth century, and then—you must know this from school—it was precisely the custom to bring the powers of heaven down to earth in poetic works. I'm not even speaking of Dante. In France, law clerks, and monks in monasteries too, put on whole performances in which they brought the Madonna, angels, saints, Christ, and God himself onto the stage. It was all very innocent then. In Victor Hugo's *Notre-Dame de Paris*, under Louis XI, a free, edifying performance is given for the people in the town-hall chamber to celebrate the birth of the French dauphin. It's called *Le bon jugement de la très sainte et gracieuse Vierge Marie*, and she appears in person and pronounces her *bon jugement*.[^b05-c05-n001] In Moscow too, in the old days before Peter, they occasionally staged almost the same sort of dramatic performances, particularly from the Old Testament; but besides the dramatic performances, many tales and ‘verses’ circulated throughout the world, in which saints, angels, and all the heavenly host appeared as needed. In our monasteries too they translated, copied out, and even composed such poems—and when, at that: under the Tatars. There's a little monastic poem, for instance, from the Greek, of course: ‘The Mother of God's Journey Through the Torments,’ with scenes and a daring equal to Dante's. The Mother of God visits hell, and the archangel Michael guides her ‘through the torments.’ She sees the sinners and their sufferings. Among them is one very interesting category of sinners in a lake of fire: those who sink into the lake so deep they can never rise to the surface again—‘these God now forgets,’ an expression of extraordinary depth and force. And so, appalled and weeping, the Mother of God falls before God's throne and begs mercy for everyone in hell, everyone she saw there, without distinction. Her conversation with God is immensely interesting. She pleads, she won't leave, and when God points to her son's hands and feet pierced by nails and asks, ‘How can I forgive his torturers?’ she bids all the saints, all the martyrs, all the angels and archangels fall down with her and plead for mercy for everyone without distinction. It ends with her winning from God a suspension of the torments every year from Good Friday to Pentecost, and the sinners in hell at once thank the Lord and cry out to him: ‘Just are you, Lord, to have judged so.’ Well, my little poem would be of that same kind if it had appeared then. He appears on the stage in mine; admittedly he says nothing in the poem, he only appears and passes through. Fifteen centuries have gone by since he promised to come in his kingdom, fifteen centuries since his prophet wrote: ‘Behold, I come soon.’ ‘But of that day and hour not even the Son knows, only my Father in heaven,’ as he himself said while still on earth. But humanity waits for him with the same faith and the same tender devotion. Oh, with even greater faith, because fifteen centuries have already gone by since heaven stopped sending man its pledges:

> Trust what your heart will tell you.  
> There are no pledges from the heavens.

<!-- B05-C05-P002 -->
“And only faith in what the heart has said! True, there were many miracles then too. There were saints who worked miraculous cures; the Queen of Heaven herself came down to certain righteous people, according to their lives. But the devil doesn't sleep, and humanity had already begun to doubt the truth of these miracles. Just then, in the north, in Germany, a terrible new heresy appeared. A great star ‘like a lamp’—that is, a church—‘fell upon the springs of water, and they became bitter.’ These heresies began blasphemously denying miracles. But those who remained faithful believed all the more ardently. Humanity's tears rise to him as before; they wait for him, love him, hope in him, yearn to suffer and die for him as before. And so, for so many centuries humanity had prayed with faith and fervor, ‘For, Lord, appear to us,’ had called on him for so many centuries, that in his immeasurable compassion he wished to descend to those who prayed. He had descended before, visiting certain righteous people, martyrs, and holy hermits while they were still on earth, as recorded in their ‘lives.’ Our Tyutchev, profoundly believing his own words to be true, proclaimed that

> Bowed beneath the burden of the cross,  
> Through all of you, my native land,  
> In a slave's form the King of Heaven  
> Walked, giving his blessing.

“And that is certainly how it was, I tell you. And so he wished to appear, if only for a moment, to the people—the tormented, suffering, foully sinful people who nevertheless loved him like little children. My story is set in Spain, in Seville, at the most terrible time of the Inquisition, when every day pyres burned throughout the land to the glory of God, and

> In magnificent autos-da-fé  
> They burned the wicked heretics.

“Oh, of course this was not the descent in which, according to his promise, he will appear at the end of time in all his heavenly glory, suddenly, ‘like lightning flashing from east to west.’ No, he wished to visit his children, if only for a moment, and precisely where the heretics' pyres had just begun to crackle. In his boundless mercy he walks among people once again in the very same human form in which he walked among them for three years fifteen centuries before. He descends into the ‘burning streets’ of the southern city where, only the previous day, in a ‘magnificent auto-da-fé,’ in the presence of the king, the court, knights, cardinals, and the loveliest ladies of the court, before the great populace of all Seville, the cardinal Grand Inquisitor had burned nearly a hundred heretics at once, *ad majorem gloriam Dei*.[^b05-c05-n003] He appeared quietly, unnoticed, and yet everyone—strange as it is—recognizes him. That could be one of the best passages in the poem: why exactly they recognize him. Drawn by an irresistible force, the people stream toward him, surround him, gather ever more thickly around him, follow him. He walks silently among them with a quiet smile of infinite compassion. The sun of love burns in his heart, rays of Light, Enlightenment, and Power stream from his eyes and, pouring over the people, move their hearts to answer with love. He stretches out his hands to them, blesses them, and from contact with him, even with his garments alone, healing power flows. An old man, blind from childhood, cries out from the crowd: ‘Lord, heal me, so that I too may see you,’ and something like scales falls from his eyes, and the blind man sees him. The people weep and kiss the ground he walks on. Children throw flowers before him, sing, and cry out to him: ‘Hosanna!’ ‘It's him, it's him himself,’ everyone repeats, ‘it must be him, it can be no one else.’ He stops at the entrance of Seville Cathedral at the very moment when, amid weeping, a little open white coffin is being carried into the church: in it lies a seven-year-old girl, the only daughter of a prominent citizen. The dead child lies covered in flowers. ‘He'll raise your child,’ they shout from the crowd to the weeping mother. The cathedral priest who has come out to meet the coffin looks on in bewilderment and frowns. But then the dead child's mother cries out. She throws herself at his feet: ‘If it is you, raise my child!’ she cries, stretching her arms toward him. The procession stops; the little coffin is lowered onto the steps at his feet. He looks with compassion, and once again his lips quietly say, ‘Talitha cumi’—‘and the maiden arose.’ The girl rises in the coffin, sits up, and looks around, smiling, with her little eyes wide in wonder. In her hands is the bouquet of white roses with which she had lain in the coffin. There is confusion among the people, cries, sobbing, and at that very moment the cardinal Grand Inquisitor himself suddenly passes across the square in front of the cathedral. He is an old man of nearly ninety, tall and straight, with a withered face and sunken eyes in which a gleam still shines like a fiery spark. Oh, he isn't wearing the splendid cardinal's robes in which he displayed himself before the people yesterday when the enemies of the Roman faith were burned; no, at this moment he wears only his old, coarse monk's habit. At a certain distance behind him follow his grim assistants and servants, and the ‘holy’ guard. He stops before the crowd and watches from a distance. He has seen everything, seen the coffin set at his feet, seen the girl rise from the dead, and his face has darkened. He draws his thick gray eyebrows together, and his eyes flash with ominous fire. He points a finger and orders the guards to take him. And such is his power, so thoroughly trained, submissive, and tremblingly obedient are the people, that the crowd immediately parts before the guards, who lay hands on him and lead him away amid the deathly silence that has suddenly fallen. Instantly, the entire crowd, as one, bows its head to the ground before the aged Inquisitor, who silently blesses the people and passes on. The guards bring the prisoner to a cramped, dark, vaulted cell in the ancient building of the Holy Tribunal and lock him in. The day passes; the dark, hot, ‘breathless’ Seville night comes on. The air ‘smells of laurel and lemon.’ In the deep darkness the iron door of the cell suddenly opens, and the old Grand Inquisitor himself slowly enters, a lamp in his hand. He's alone; the door is locked behind him at once. He stops at the entrance and looks intently into his face for a long time, a minute or two. At last he approaches quietly, sets the lamp on the table, and says to him: ‘Is it you? You?’ But receiving no answer, he quickly adds: ‘Don't answer. Be silent. And what could you say? I know only too well what you'll say. Besides, you have no right to add anything to what you said before. Why have you come to interfere with us? For you have come to interfere with us, and you know it yourself. But do you know what will happen tomorrow? I don't know who you are, and I don't want to know: whether it's you or only someone who resembles him, tomorrow I will condemn you and burn you at the stake as the worst of heretics, and the very people who kissed your feet today will rush tomorrow, at a mere sign from me, to heap the coals on your pyre. Do you know that? Yes, perhaps you do,’ he added in deep, searching reflection, never taking his eyes from his prisoner for an instant.”

“I don't quite understand, Ivan. What is this?” Alyosha smiled, having listened in silence all this time. “Is it simply boundless fantasy, or some mistake by the old man, some impossible *qui pro quo*?[^b05-c05-n004]”

<!-- B05-C05-P004 -->
“Take it as the latter if you like,” Ivan laughed, “if modern realism has spoiled you so badly that you can't stand anything fantastic. If you want a *qui pro quo*, let it be that. It's true,” he laughed again, “the old man's ninety and could long ago have gone mad over his idea. The prisoner's appearance might have struck him. In the end it could simply be delirium, a vision of a ninety-year-old man on the verge of death, still feverish from yesterday's auto-da-fé with its hundred burned heretics. But what difference does it make to you and me whether it's a *qui pro quo* or boundless fantasy? All that matters here is that the old man needs to speak his mind, that at last, after all his ninety years, he speaks out and says aloud what he's kept silent about for all those ninety years.”

<!-- B05-C05-P005 -->
“And the prisoner stays silent too? Looks at him and doesn't say a word?”

<!-- B05-C05-P006 -->
“That's just how it must be, whichever the case,” Ivan laughed again. “The old man himself tells him that he has no right to add anything to what has already been said. If you like, that's the most fundamental feature of Roman Catholicism, in my opinion at least: ‘You've handed everything over to the pope, so everything is now with the pope, and you needn't come at all anymore; don't interfere, at least before the time comes.’ They not only speak in that sense, they write it too—the Jesuits, at least. I've read it myself in their theologians. ‘Have you the right to reveal to us even one of the mysteries of the world you've come from?’ my old man asks, and answers for him: ‘No, you haven't, so that you add nothing to what has already been said, and don't take away the freedom you defended so strongly while you were on earth. Anything new you reveal will encroach on people's freedom of faith, for it will appear as a miracle, and their freedom of faith was dearer to you than anything even then, fifteen hundred years ago. Didn't you so often say then, “I want to make you free”? But now you've seen these “free” people,’ the old man suddenly adds with a thoughtful smile. ‘Yes, this business has cost us dearly,’ he continues, looking sternly at him, ‘but at last we've finished it in your name. For fifteen centuries we struggled with this freedom, but now it's over, and settled for good. You don't believe it's settled for good? You look at me gently and don't even deign to be indignant with me? But know that now, at this very moment, these people are more certain than ever that they're completely free, and yet they've brought their freedom to us themselves and laid it obediently at our feet. But that was our doing. Was that what you wanted, that kind of freedom?’”

<!-- B05-C05-P007 -->
“Again I don't understand,” Alyosha interrupted. “Is he being ironic, is he joking?”

<!-- B05-C05-P008 -->
“Not at all. He takes credit for himself and his people precisely because they've finally conquered freedom, and done it to make people happy. ‘For only now’—he's speaking of the Inquisition, of course—‘has it become possible for the first time to think of human happiness. Man was made a rebel; can rebels be happy? You were warned,’ he says to him, ‘you had no lack of warnings and guidance, but you didn't heed the warnings, you rejected the only way people could be made happy. Fortunately, when you left, you handed the work over to us. You promised, you confirmed it with your word, you gave us the right to bind and loose, and surely you can't even think of taking that right away from us now. Why, then, have you come to interfere with us?’”

<!-- B05-C05-P009 -->
“What does he mean, ‘no lack of warnings and guidance’?” Alyosha asked.

<!-- B05-C05-P010 -->
“That's the main thing the old man needs to say.”

“‘The terrible and intelligent spirit, the spirit of self-destruction and nonbeing,’ the old man goes on, ‘the great spirit spoke to you in the wilderness, and the books have handed down to us that he supposedly “tempted” you. Is that so? And could anything be said more true than what he revealed to you in those three questions, which you rejected, and which the books call “temptations”? And yet, if ever a true, thunderous miracle was performed on earth, it was that day, the day of those three temptations. The miracle lay precisely in the appearance of those three questions. If it were possible to imagine, merely as an experiment and an example, that those three questions of the terrible spirit had been lost from the books without a trace, and had to be restored, invented and composed anew to be put back into the books, and for that purpose all the wise men of the earth were assembled—rulers, high priests, scholars, philosophers, poets—and given this task: invent, compose three questions, but such questions as would not only match the magnitude of the event, but would also express in three words, in just three human sentences, the entire future history of the world and humanity—do you think all the wisdom of the earth combined could invent anything equal in power and depth to those three questions the mighty and intelligent spirit actually put to you then in the wilderness? From those questions alone, from the mere miracle of their appearance, one can see that one is dealing not with a passing human intelligence but with an eternal and absolute one. For in these three questions the whole subsequent history of humanity is, as it were, brought together and foretold, and three images are revealed in which all the insoluble historical contradictions of human nature throughout the earth will converge. It couldn't yet have been so clear then, for the future was unknown, but now, after fifteen centuries, we see that everything in those three questions was so thoroughly divined and foretold, and has been so thoroughly borne out, that nothing more can be added to them or taken away.

<!-- B05-C05-P012 -->
“‘Decide for yourself, then, who was right: you, or the one who questioned you then? Remember the first question; not literally, but this was its meaning: “You want to go into the world, and you go empty-handed, with some promise of freedom that, in their simplicity and their inborn unruliness, they cannot even comprehend, that they fear and dread—for nothing has ever been more unbearable to man and human society than freedom! But do you see these stones in this bare, scorching wilderness? Turn them into bread, and humanity will run after you like a flock, grateful and obedient, though always trembling lest you withdraw your hand and your bread cease to come to them.” But you didn't want to deprive man of freedom and rejected the offer, for what freedom could there be, you reasoned, if obedience were bought with bread? You replied that man does not live by bread alone, but do you know that in the name of that very earthly bread the spirit of the earth will rise against you, do battle with you, and defeat you, and everyone will follow him, crying, “Who is like this beast? He has given us fire from heaven!” Do you know that centuries will pass and humanity will proclaim through the mouth of its wisdom and science that there is no crime, and therefore no sin, but only hungry people? “Feed them, then ask virtue of them!”—that is what they will write on the banner they raise against you, the banner by which your temple will be destroyed. In place of your temple a new building will rise, the terrible Tower of Babel will rise again, and although this one too will remain unfinished, like the earlier one, you could still have prevented this new tower and shortened human suffering by a thousand years, for they will come to us, after tormenting themselves for a thousand years with their tower! Then they will find us again, hiding underground in the catacombs—for we will once more be persecuted and tortured—they will find us and cry out to us: “Feed us, for those who promised us fire from heaven have not given it to us.” And then we will finish building their tower, for whoever feeds them will complete it, and we alone will feed them, in your name, and we will lie that it is in your name. Oh, never, never will they feed themselves without us! No science will give them bread so long as they remain free, but it will end with their bringing their freedom to our feet and saying, “Enslave us instead, only feed us.” At last they themselves will understand that freedom and earthly bread enough for everyone are inconceivable together, because never, never will they know how to share among themselves! They will also become convinced that they can never be free, because they are weak, vicious, worthless, and rebellious. You promised them heavenly bread, but I repeat again, can it compare with earthly bread in the eyes of the weak, eternally vicious and eternally ignoble human race? And if thousands and tens of thousands follow you for the sake of heavenly bread, what will become of the millions and tens of thousands of millions of beings who will lack the strength to scorn earthly bread for heavenly? Or are only the tens of thousands of great and strong ones dear to you, while the remaining millions, as numerous as the sand of the sea, weak but loving you, must serve only as material for the great and strong? No, the weak are dear to us too. They are vicious and rebellious, but in the end they will become obedient. They will marvel at us and regard us as gods because, placing ourselves at their head, we agreed to endure freedom and rule over them—so terrible will being free have become to them at last! But we will say that we obey you and rule in your name. We will deceive them again, for we will no longer let you come among us. That deception will be our suffering, for we will have to lie. That was the meaning of the first question in the wilderness, and that is what you rejected in the name of freedom, which you placed above all else. And yet that question contained the great secret of this world. Had you accepted “the bread,” you would have answered the universal and eternal longing of man, both as an individual and as the whole of humanity together: “Before whom shall I bow?” No concern is more constant and tormenting for man than, once left free, to find as quickly as possible someone to bow down before. But man seeks to bow before something already beyond dispute, so indisputable that all people will agree at once to bow before it together. For the concern of these miserable creatures is not merely to find something before which I or someone else can bow, but to find something everyone will believe in and bow before, and that everyone should do so together, without fail. This need for a shared worship is the chief torment of every individual person and of humanity as a whole from the beginning of time. For the sake of universal worship they have slaughtered one another with the sword. They created gods and called to one another: “Abandon your gods and come worship ours, or death to you and your gods!” And so it will be to the end of the world, even when gods themselves have disappeared from the world: they will fall before idols all the same. You knew, you could not have failed to know, this fundamental secret of human nature, but you rejected the one absolute banner offered you to make everyone bow before you without dispute—the banner of earthly bread—and rejected it in the name of freedom and heavenly bread. Look, then, at what you did next. Again, all in the name of freedom! I tell you, man has no more tormenting concern than to find someone to whom he can quickly hand over the gift of freedom with which this unhappy creature is born. But only someone who sets their conscience at rest can take possession of people's freedom. With bread you were given an indisputable banner: give bread, and man will bow down, for nothing is more indisputable than bread; but if at the same time someone else, apart from you, takes possession of his conscience—oh, then he'll even abandon your bread and follow the one who seduces his conscience. In that you were right. For the secret of human existence is not merely to live, but to have something to live for. Without a firm idea of what he is to live for, man will not consent to live and will destroy himself rather than remain on earth, even if bread is all around him. That's true, but what came of it? Instead of taking possession of people's freedom, you increased it still further! Or had you forgotten that peace, and even death, are dearer to man than free choice in the knowledge of good and evil? Nothing is more seductive to man than freedom of conscience, but nothing is more tormenting either. And so, instead of firm foundations to set the human conscience at rest once and for all, you chose everything extraordinary, conjectural, and indefinite, you chose everything beyond people's powers, and thus acted as though you did not love them at all—and who was it? The very one who had come to give his life for them! Instead of taking possession of human freedom, you multiplied it and burdened the spiritual kingdom of man with its torments forever. You desired man's freely given love, that he should freely follow you, drawn and captivated by you. Instead of the firm ancient law, man was henceforth to decide for himself with a free heart what was good and what was evil, with only your image before him as a guide—but did you really not think that he would eventually reject and even dispute your image and your truth, if he were crushed by so terrible a burden as freedom of choice? They will cry out in the end that the truth is not in you, for it was impossible to leave them in greater confusion and torment than you did, leaving them so many cares and insoluble problems. Thus you yourself laid the foundation for the destruction of your own kingdom, and you must blame no one else for it. And yet, was that what was offered you? There are three powers, only three powers on earth, capable of conquering and holding captive forever the conscience of these feeble rebels, for their happiness. These powers are miracle, mystery, and authority. You rejected all three and set the example yourself. When the terrible and all-wise spirit placed you on the pinnacle of the temple and said, “If you want to know whether you are the Son of God, throw yourself down, for it is said of him that the angels will catch him and carry him, and he will not fall or hurt himself, and then you will know whether you are the Son of God and prove your faith in your Father”—you listened, but rejected the offer, did not yield, and did not throw yourself down. Oh, of course you acted proudly and magnificently there, like God, but people, this weak, rebellious breed—are they gods? Oh, you understood then that by taking just one step, just making the movement to throw yourself down, you would at once have tempted the Lord and lost all faith in him, and would have shattered yourself against the earth you came to save, and the intelligent spirit tempting you would have rejoiced. But I repeat, how many are there like you? And could you really have supposed, even for a moment, that people too would have the strength to withstand such a temptation? Is human nature made to reject miracles and, at such terrible moments in life, moments of its most terrible, fundamental, tormenting spiritual questions, to be left with only the free decision of the heart? Oh, you knew that your deed would be preserved in the books, would reach into the depths of time and to the farthest ends of the earth, and you hoped that, following you, man too would remain with God without needing a miracle. But you did not know that as soon as man rejects miracles, he rejects God too, for man seeks not so much God as miracles. And since man cannot do without miracles, he'll make up new miracles of his own and worship the healer's marvels, women's sorcery, even if he's a rebel, heretic, and atheist a hundred times over. You did not come down from the cross when they shouted at you, mocking and taunting you, “Come down from the cross, and we will believe it is you.” You did not come down because, again, you did not want to enslave man through a miracle and longed for faith freely given, not faith born of miracles. You longed for freely given love, not a slave's servile rapture before a power that had terrified him once and for all. But here too you thought too highly of people, for of course they are slaves, though they were created rebels. Look around and judge: fifteen centuries have passed, go and look at them. Whom have you raised to your level? I swear, man was created weaker and lower than you thought! Can he, can he accomplish what you can? In respecting him so much, you acted as though you had ceased to feel compassion for him, because you demanded far too much of him—and who was it? The very one who loved him more than himself! Had you respected him less, you would have demanded less of him, and that would have been closer to love, for his burden would have been lighter. He is weak and base. What of it if he now rebels against our power everywhere, and is proud of rebelling? It is a child's, a schoolboy's pride. They are little children who have rebelled in the classroom and driven out the teacher. But the children's delight will come to an end too, and cost them dearly. They will tear down temples and flood the earth with blood. But at last the foolish children will realize that although they are rebels, they are feeble rebels, unable to endure their own rebellion. Drenched in their foolish tears, they will finally admit that whoever created them rebels undoubtedly meant to mock them. They will say this in despair, and what they say will be blasphemy, which will make them unhappier still, for human nature cannot endure blasphemy and in the end always avenges it itself. So unrest, confusion, and unhappiness—that is people's present lot after you suffered so much for their freedom! Your great prophet says in vision and allegory that he saw all those who shared in the first resurrection, and that there were twelve thousand of them from each tribe. But if there were that many, then they too were, as it were, gods rather than people. They endured your cross, they endured decades in the wilderness, hungry and naked, living on locusts and roots—and of course you can proudly point to these children of freedom, of freely given love, of their free and magnificent sacrifice in your name. But remember, there were only a few thousand of them, and those were gods; what of the rest? And how are the other, weak people to blame for being unable to endure what the mighty could? How is a weak soul to blame for being unable to hold such terrible gifts? Can you really have come only to the chosen and for the chosen? But if so, there is a mystery here, and we cannot understand it. And if there is a mystery, then we too had the right to preach mystery and teach them that what matters is not the free decision of their hearts, nor love, but mystery, which they must obey blindly, even against their conscience. That is what we did. We corrected your deed and founded it on miracle, mystery, and authority. And people rejoiced that they were once again led like a flock, and that their hearts were finally relieved of that terrible gift which had brought them so much torment. Were we right to teach and act that way, tell me? Did we not love humanity, so humbly acknowledging its powerlessness, lovingly easing its burden, and permitting its weak nature even sin, provided it was with our permission? Why, then, have you come now to interfere with us? And why do you silently look so deeply into me with your gentle eyes? Be angry; I don't want your love, because I don't love you myself. And what have I to hide from you? Don't I know whom I'm speaking to? Everything I have to tell you is already known to you, I read it in your eyes. Could I conceal our secret from you? Perhaps you want to hear it from my own lips. Listen, then: we are not with you, but with him—that is our secret! We have long been with him and not with you, for eight centuries now. Exactly eight centuries ago we took from him what you indignantly rejected, that last gift he offered you when he showed you all the kingdoms of the earth: we took Rome and Caesar's sword from him, and proclaimed ourselves alone the kings of the earth, its sole kings, though to this day we have not yet managed to bring our work to full completion. But whose fault is that? Oh, this work is still only beginning, but it has begun. Its completion is still a long way off, and the earth will suffer much more, but we will achieve it and become Caesars, and then we will think about the universal happiness of mankind. Yet you could have taken Caesar's sword even then. Why did you reject that last gift? By accepting the mighty spirit's third counsel, you would have fulfilled everything man seeks on earth: someone to bow before, someone to hand his conscience over to, and a way for everyone to unite at last in an indisputably common and harmonious anthill, for the need for universal union is people's third and final torment. Humanity as a whole has always striven to organize itself on a universal scale. There have been many great nations with great histories, but the greater those nations, the unhappier they were, for they felt more keenly than others the need for a worldwide union of people. The great conquerors, the Timurs and Genghis Khans, swept across the earth like a whirlwind, seeking to conquer the universe, but they too, though unconsciously, expressed that same great human need for worldwide and universal unity. Had you accepted the world and Caesar's purple, you would have founded a universal kingdom and given universal peace. For who should rule people if not those who rule their conscience and hold their bread in their hands? We took Caesar's sword, and in taking it, of course, rejected you and followed him. Oh, centuries of the unrestrained free mind, of their science and cannibalism, will pass, for having begun to build their Tower of Babel without us, they will end in cannibalism. But then the beast will crawl to us, lick our feet, and sprinkle them with bloody tears from its eyes. And we will mount the beast and raise the cup, and on it will be written “Mystery!” Then and only then will the kingdom of peace and happiness begin for mankind. You are proud of your chosen ones, but you have only the chosen, while we will give everyone rest. And even that—is it so? How many of these chosen ones, of the mighty who could have become chosen ones, have finally tired of waiting for you, and have carried, and will yet carry, the strength of their spirits and the ardor of their hearts to another field, and will end by raising their free banner against you yourself. But you yourself raised that banner. Under us, everyone will be happy, and they will no longer rebel or slaughter one another everywhere, as they do in your freedom. Oh, we will convince them that they will become free only when they surrender their freedom to us and submit to us. And will we be right, or will we be lying? They themselves will become convinced we're right, for they will remember the horrors of slavery and confusion to which your freedom brought them. Freedom, a free mind, and science will lead them into such wildernesses and confront them with such marvels and insoluble mysteries that some, the unruly and ferocious, will destroy themselves; others, unruly but weak, will destroy one another; and the remaining ones, feeble and unhappy, will crawl to our feet and cry out: “Yes, you were right, you alone possessed his mystery, and we're coming back to you. Save us from ourselves.” Of course, when they receive bread from us, they'll see clearly that we take their own bread, earned by their own hands, to give it back to them without any miracle; they'll see that we haven't turned stones into bread. But truly, more than the bread itself, they'll rejoice that they receive it from our hands! For they'll remember only too well that before, without us, the very bread they earned turned into stones in their hands, while when they returned to us, the very stones turned into bread in their hands. They will appreciate only too well, only too well, what it means to submit once and for all! And until people understand this, they will be unhappy. Who has contributed most to this failure to understand, tell me? Who broke up the flock and scattered it along unknown paths? But the flock will gather again and submit again, and this time once and for all. Then we will give them a quiet, humble happiness, the happiness of the feeble creatures they were made to be. Oh, we will finally convince them not to be proud, for you raised them up and thereby taught them pride; we will prove to them that they are feeble, that they are only pitiful children, but that a child's happiness is sweeter than any other. They will become timid and look to us and huddle against us in fear, like chicks around a hen. They will marvel at us and be terrified of us, and be proud that we are so powerful and so intelligent that we could tame such a turbulent flock of a thousand million. They will tremble helplessly before our anger, their minds will grow timid, their eyes will become tearful like children's and women's, but just as easily, at a sign from us, they will turn to merriment and laughter, bright joy and a happy little children's song. Yes, we will make them work, but in the hours free from labor we will arrange their lives like a children's game, with children's songs sung in chorus, with innocent dances. Oh, we will allow them sin too; they are weak and powerless, and they will love us like children for letting them sin. We will tell them that every sin will be redeemed if committed with our permission, and that we allow them to sin because we love them, while the punishment for these sins—so be it—we will take upon ourselves. And we will take it upon ourselves, and they will adore us as benefactors who have borne their sins before God. And they will have no secrets from us. We will permit or forbid them to live with their wives and mistresses, to have or not have children, all according to their obedience, and they will submit to us with gladness and joy. The most agonizing secrets of their conscience—everything, everything they will bring to us, and we will resolve everything, and they will believe our decision with joy, because it will relieve them of their great care and of the terrible torments they now suffer in deciding personally and freely. And everyone will be happy, all the millions of beings, except the hundred thousand who govern them. For only we, we who guard the secret, only we will be unhappy. There will be thousands of millions of happy infants and a hundred thousand sufferers who have taken upon themselves the curse of the knowledge of good and evil. They will die quietly, quietly fade away in your name, and beyond the grave find only death. But we will keep the secret and, for their own happiness, entice them with the promise of a heavenly and eternal reward. For even if there were anything in the next world, it certainly would not be for people like them. They say and prophesy that you will come and triumph again, that you will come with your chosen ones, your proud and mighty ones, but we will say that they saved only themselves, while we saved everyone. They say the harlot sitting on the beast and holding the mystery in her hands will be disgraced, that the weak will rebel again, tear off her purple robe and expose her “foul” body. But then I will rise and point out to you the thousands of millions of happy infants who have known no sin. And we who took their sins upon ourselves for their happiness, we will stand before you and say: “Judge us, if you can and dare.” Know that I am not afraid of you. Know that I too was in the wilderness, that I too fed on locusts and roots, that I too blessed the freedom with which you blessed mankind, and was preparing to join the ranks of your chosen, the mighty and the strong, thirsting to “make up the number.” But I came to my senses and would not serve madness. I returned and joined the ranks of those who corrected your deed. I left the proud and returned to the humble, for the happiness of those humble ones. What I tell you will come to pass, and our kingdom will be built. I repeat: tomorrow you will see this obedient flock rush at my first signal to heap hot coals on your pyre, where I will burn you for coming to interfere with us. For if anyone has ever deserved our pyre more than all the rest, it is you. Tomorrow I will burn you. *Dixi*.[^b05-c05-n005]’”

<!-- B05-C05-P013 -->
Ivan stopped. He had grown heated as he spoke and had spoken with enthusiasm; when he finished, he suddenly smiled.

<!-- B05-C05-P014 -->
Alyosha, who had listened in silence all along and toward the end, in extreme agitation, had tried many times to interrupt his brother but visibly restrained himself, suddenly burst out, as though springing loose.

<!-- B05-C05-P015 -->
“But... it's absurd!” he cried, blushing. “Your poem is praise of Jesus, not an attack on him... as you meant it to be. And who will believe you about freedom? Is that, is that how it should be understood? Is that the Orthodox understanding?... That's Rome, and not even all of Rome, it's untrue—it's the worst of Catholicism, the inquisitors, the Jesuits!.. Besides, such a fantastic person as your Inquisitor simply couldn't exist. What are these human sins they've taken on themselves? Who are these keepers of a mystery who've taken some curse upon themselves for people's happiness? When has anyone seen them? We know the Jesuits, people speak badly of them, but are they what you make them? They aren't that at all, nothing like it... They're simply the Roman army for a future universal earthly kingdom, with an emperor—the Roman pontiff—at its head... that's their ideal, but without any mysteries or lofty sorrow... The plainest desire for power, for filthy earthly goods, for enslavement... a sort of future serfdom, with themselves as the landowners... that's all they have. Perhaps they don't even believe in God. Your suffering Inquisitor is pure fantasy...”

<!-- B05-C05-P016 -->
“Wait, wait,” Ivan laughed, “how heated you've become. Fantasy, you say—very well! Of course it's fantasy. But allow me this: do you really think that this whole Catholic movement of recent centuries is in fact nothing but a desire for power merely for filthy gain? Is that what Father Paisius teaches you?”

<!-- B05-C05-P017 -->
“No, no, on the contrary, Father Paisius once said something even like what you... but of course not that, nothing like that,” Alyosha suddenly caught himself.

<!-- B05-C05-P018 -->
“Valuable information all the same, despite your ‘nothing like that.’ What I'm asking you is why your Jesuits and inquisitors united solely for vile material gain. Why couldn't there be even one sufferer among them, tormented by a great sorrow and loving humanity? Look: suppose that among all those who desire only material and filthy gain there were even one—just one like my old Inquisitor, who himself ate roots in the wilderness and raged as he conquered his flesh to make himself free and perfect, but who had nevertheless loved humanity all his life, and suddenly saw clearly and realized that there was little moral blessedness in attaining perfection of will, only to become convinced at the same time that millions of God's other creatures had been made merely as a mockery, that they would never have the strength to manage their freedom, that these pitiful rebels would never produce giants to complete the tower, that it was not for geese like these that the great idealist had dreamed of his harmony. Understanding all this, he returned and joined... the intelligent people. Could that really not have happened?”

<!-- B05-C05-P019 -->
“Joined whom, what intelligent people?” Alyosha cried, almost fiercely. “They have no intelligence of that sort, no mysteries or secrets like that... Only godlessness, perhaps, that's their whole secret. Your Inquisitor doesn't believe in God, that's his whole secret!”

<!-- B05-C05-P020 -->
“Suppose that's so! You've finally guessed. And it really is so, that really is the whole secret, but isn't that suffering, at least for a man like him, who spent his whole life on his feat of asceticism in the wilderness and never cured himself of his love for humanity? In the twilight of his days he becomes clearly convinced that only the counsels of the great and terrible spirit could bring the feeble rebels, those ‘unfinished trial creatures made as a mockery,’ into any kind of tolerable order. And so, convinced of this, he sees that he must follow the guidance of the intelligent spirit, the terrible spirit of death and destruction, and to do that accept lies and deceit, and knowingly lead people toward death and destruction, deceiving them the whole way so that they don't somehow notice where they're being led, so that at least on the journey these miserable blind people will think themselves happy. And notice, it's deception in the name of the one in whose ideal the old man believed so passionately all his life! Isn't that unhappiness? And if even one such man were at the head of that whole army ‘thirsting for power solely for filthy gain,’ wouldn't even one be enough to make a tragedy? More than that: one such man at the head would be enough to discover at last the true guiding idea of the whole Roman enterprise with all its armies and Jesuits, the higher idea of that enterprise. I'll tell you directly that I firmly believe this one man has never been lacking among those at the head of the movement. Who knows, perhaps such solitary men have appeared among the Roman pontiffs too. Who knows, perhaps this accursed old man, who so persistently and in his own way loves humanity, exists even now in the form of a whole host of such solitary old men, and not at all by chance, but as an alliance, a secret union long ago established to preserve the mystery, to keep it from unhappy, feeble people in order to make them happy. It certainly exists, indeed it must be so. I have a notion that even the Freemasons have something like this same mystery at their foundation, and that the reason Catholics hate the Freemasons so much is that they see them as competitors, a fragmentation of the unity of the idea, when there should be one flock and one shepherd... But defending my idea, I look like an author who couldn't stand your criticism. Enough of that.”

<!-- B05-C05-P021 -->
“Perhaps you're a Freemason yourself!” Alyosha suddenly blurted out. “You don't believe in God,” he added, but now with immense sorrow. Besides, it seemed to him that his brother was looking at him mockingly. “How does your poem end?” he asked suddenly, looking at the ground. “Or is it finished already?”

<!-- B05-C05-P022 -->
“I meant to end it like this: when the Inquisitor falls silent, he waits a while for his prisoner to answer. His silence weighs on him. He has seen how the prisoner listened all along, quietly and with deep attention, looking straight into his eyes and apparently not wishing to make any objection. The old man would like him to say something, even something bitter, terrible. But suddenly he silently approaches the old man and gently kisses his bloodless ninety-year-old lips. That's the whole answer. The old man shudders. Something moves at the corners of his mouth; he goes to the door, opens it, and says to him: ‘Go, and don't come again... don't come at all... never, never!’ And he lets him out into ‘the dark streets of the city.’ The prisoner leaves.”

<!-- B05-C05-P023 -->
“And the old man?”

<!-- B05-C05-P024 -->
“The kiss burns in his heart, but the old man holds to his former idea.”

<!-- B05-C05-P025 -->
“And you along with him, you too?” Alyosha cried sorrowfully. Ivan laughed.

<!-- B05-C05-P026 -->
“But it's nonsense, Alyosha, only a muddled poem by a muddled student who's never written two lines of verse. Why take it so seriously? Surely you don't think I'm going straight off to the Jesuits now, to join the ranks of the people correcting his deed? Good Lord, what do I care! I told you: if I can only last till thirty, then—the cup to the floor!”

<!-- B05-C05-P027 -->
“And the sticky little leaves, and the beloved graves, and the blue sky, and the woman you love! How will you live, what will you love them with?” Alyosha cried sorrowfully. “With such hell in your heart and your head, is that possible? No, you are going to join them... and if you aren't, you'll kill yourself, you won't endure it!”

<!-- B05-C05-P028 -->
“There's a force that will endure anything!” Ivan said, his smile cold now.

<!-- B05-C05-P029 -->
“What force?”

<!-- B05-C05-P030 -->
“The Karamazov force... the force of Karamazov baseness.”

<!-- B05-C05-P031 -->
“Drowning in debauchery, crushing your soul in corruption—is that it, is that it?”

<!-- B05-C05-P032 -->
“Perhaps that too... only I may avoid it till I'm thirty, and then...”

<!-- B05-C05-P033 -->
“How will you avoid it? By what means? It's impossible with your ideas.”

<!-- B05-C05-P034 -->
“Again, in the Karamazov way.”

<!-- B05-C05-P035 -->
“You mean ‘everything is permitted’? Everything is permitted, is that it, is that it?”

<!-- B05-C05-P036 -->
Ivan frowned and suddenly turned strangely pale.

<!-- B05-C05-P037 -->
“Ah, you've picked up yesterday's little phrase, the one that offended Miusov so much... and that brother Dmitri jumped in so innocently and restated?” He smiled crookedly. “Yes, perhaps: ‘everything is permitted,’ now that the words have been spoken. I don't renounce it. And Mitenka's version isn't bad.”

<!-- B05-C05-P038 -->
Alyosha looked at him silently.

<!-- B05-C05-P039 -->
“Brother, as I was leaving, I thought that in all the world I had at least you,” Ivan suddenly said with unexpected feeling, “but now I see there's no place for me even in your heart, my dear hermit. I won't renounce the formula ‘everything is permitted.’ Well then, will you renounce me for that, will you, will you?”

<!-- B05-C05-P040 -->
Alyosha stood up, went over to him, and silently, gently kissed him on the lips.

<!-- B05-C05-P041 -->
“Literary theft!” Ivan cried, suddenly passing into a kind of rapture. “You stole that from my poem! Thank you all the same. Get up, Alyosha, let's go, it's time for both of us.”

<!-- B05-C05-P042 -->
They went out, but stopped at the tavern steps.

<!-- B05-C05-P043 -->
“Listen, Alyosha,” Ivan said firmly. “If I really do have enough left in me for the sticky little leaves, I'll love them only by remembering you. It's enough for me that you exist somewhere here, and I won't lose the desire to live yet. Is that enough for you? If you like, take it as a declaration of love. And now you go right, I go left—and enough, do you hear, enough. I mean, if I don't leave tomorrow after all—I think I certainly will—and we happen to meet again somehow, not another word to me about any of these subjects. I insist. And about brother Dmitri too, I particularly ask you never even to bring him up with me again,” he suddenly added irritably. “It's all exhausted, it's all been said, hasn't it? And for my part, in return, I'll make you a promise too: when, toward thirty, I want to ‘throw the cup to the floor,’ then wherever you are, I'll come to talk things over with you once more... even from America, know that. I'll come expressly for it. It will be very interesting to see you by then too: what will you be like then? You see, that's a fairly solemn promise. And really, we may be saying goodbye for seven years, for ten. Well, go now to your *Pater Seraphicus*,[^b05-c05-n006] he's dying, after all; if he dies without you, you may even be angry with me for keeping you. Goodbye, kiss me once more, like that, and go...”

<!-- B05-C05-P044 -->
Ivan suddenly turned and went his way without looking back. It was like the way brother Dmitri had left Alyosha yesterday, although yesterday had been quite a different sort of thing. This strange little observation darted like an arrow through Alyosha's sad mind, sad and sorrowful at that moment. He waited a little, watching his brother go. For some reason he suddenly noticed that brother Ivan walked with a kind of swaying motion, and that his right shoulder, seen from behind, seemed lower than his left. He had never noticed it before. But suddenly he too turned and almost ran toward the monastery. It was already growing quite dark, and he was almost afraid; something new was rising in him that he couldn't have accounted for. The wind had risen again, as yesterday, and the ancient pines murmured darkly around him as he entered the little wood of the hermitage. He was almost running. “‘Pater Seraphicus’—he got that name from somewhere—where?” flashed through Alyosha's mind. “Ivan, poor Ivan, when will I see you now?... Here's the hermitage, Lord! Yes, yes, it's him, he's *Pater Seraphicus*, he'll save me... from him, and forever!”

<!-- B05-C05-P045 -->
Later, several times in his life, he would recall with great bewilderment how, after parting from Ivan, he could suddenly have forgotten brother Dmitri so completely, when that morning, only a few hours before, he had resolved to find him without fail and not leave until he did, even if it meant not returning to the monastery that night.
