# Book VIII — Mitya
## IV. In the Dark

<!-- B08-C04-P001 -->
Where had he run? We know: “Where could she be but at Fyodor Pavlovich’s? She ran straight to him from Samsonov’s; that’s clear now. The whole plot, the whole deception is obvious now…” All this whirled through his head. He did not go into Marya Kondratyevna’s yard. “No need to go there, absolutely not… mustn’t raise the slightest alarm… they’ll tell on me at once, betray me… Marya Kondratyevna’s plainly in the plot, and Smerdyakov too, too, they’ve all been bought!” He formed another plan: making a wide detour down a lane, he circled Fyodor Pavlovich’s house, ran along Dmitrovskaya Street, crossed the little bridge, and came straight into the secluded lane at the back, empty and uninhabited, bounded on one side by the wattle fence of a neighbor’s vegetable garden and on the other by the high, solid fence enclosing Fyodor Pavlovich’s garden. Here he chose a spot—apparently the very spot where, according to the story he knew, Stinking Lizaveta had once climbed over. “If she could get over,” flashed through his mind, God knows why, “why shouldn’t I?” And indeed, he jumped, deftly caught the top of the fence with one hand, hauled himself up with a vigorous pull, and in a moment was sitting astride it. There was a small bathhouse nearby in the garden, but from the fence he could also see the lighted windows of the house. “Just as I thought: the old man’s bedroom’s lit up. She’s there!” He jumped down into the garden. Although he knew Grigory was ill, and perhaps Smerdyakov really was ill too, so that there was no one to hear him, instinctively he hid, stood motionless, and listened. But everything was deathly silent and, as if on purpose, utterly still, without the slightest breath of wind.

<!-- B08-C04-P002 -->
“‘And only silence whispers,’” for some reason that scrap of verse flashed through his mind. “If only no one heard me jump. I don’t think they did.” After standing there a minute, he moved quietly through the garden, over the grass. Skirting the trees and bushes, he took a long time, muffling each footstep, listening to each step himself. It took him about five minutes to reach the lighted window. He remembered several large, tall, thick elderberry and guelder-rose bushes directly beneath the windows. The door leading from the house into the garden, on the left side of the façade, was locked: he had deliberately made sure of that as he passed. At last he reached the bushes and hid behind them. He held his breath. “Now I must wait,” he thought. “If they heard my footsteps and are listening now, they must have time to think they were mistaken… if only I don’t cough or sneeze…”

<!-- B08-C04-P003 -->
He waited about two minutes, but his heart was pounding terribly, and at moments he could hardly breathe. “No, my heart won’t settle down,” he thought. “I can’t wait any longer.” He stood in the shadow behind a bush; its front half was lit by the window. “Guelder rose—berries—how red they are!” he whispered, without knowing why. Quietly, taking separate, inaudible steps, he went up to the window and rose on tiptoe. Fyodor Pavlovich’s entire little bedroom lay plainly before him. It was a small room, divided right across by red screens, “Chinese,” as Fyodor Pavlovich called them. “Chinese,” passed through Mitya’s mind, “and Grushenka’s behind them.” He began examining Fyodor Pavlovich. The old man was wearing a new striped silk dressing gown Mitya had never seen before, tied with a silk cord and tassels. Clean, elegant linen showed beneath its collar: a fine Holland shirt with gold studs. On his head Fyodor Pavlovich wore the same red bandage Alyosha had seen. “Dressed himself up,” thought Mitya. Fyodor Pavlovich stood near the window, apparently lost in thought; suddenly he jerked up his head, listened a little, and, hearing nothing, went to the table, poured half a small glass of cognac from the decanter, and drank it. Then he gave a deep sigh, stood still again, and absentmindedly approached the mirror on the wall between the windows. With his right hand he raised the red bandage a little from his forehead and began inspecting his bruises and sores, which had not yet healed. “He’s alone,” thought Mitya. “In all probability, alone.” Fyodor Pavlovich moved away from the mirror, suddenly turned toward the window, and looked out. Mitya instantly sprang back into the shadows.

<!-- B08-C04-P004 -->
“Perhaps she’s behind his screens, perhaps she’s asleep already.” The thought stabbed him in the heart. Fyodor Pavlovich moved away from the window. “He was looking out for her, so she isn’t here. Why else look into the dark?… Impatience must be eating him up…” Mitya immediately darted forward and looked through the window again. The old man was now sitting at the little table, visibly dejected. At last he rested his elbow on it and propped his cheek in his right palm. Mitya watched avidly.

<!-- B08-C04-P005 -->
“Alone, alone!” he repeated again. “If she were here, his face would be different.” Strangely, a senseless, peculiar vexation suddenly boiled up in his heart because she was not there. “Not because she isn’t here,” Mitya immediately reasoned and answered himself, “but because I can’t find out for certain whether she is or not.” Mitya himself remembered afterward that his mind had been extraordinarily clear at that moment: it had taken in everything down to the last detail, caught every tiny sign. But anguish, the anguish of uncertainty and indecision, was mounting in his heart at a monstrous speed. “Is she here or isn’t she?” seethed angrily within him. And suddenly he made up his mind, reached out, and tapped softly on the window frame. He tapped out the old man’s agreed signal with Smerdyakov: the first two taps more softly, then three in quicker succession—tap, tap, tap—the signal that meant “Grushenka’s here.” The old man started, jerked up his head, leaped to his feet, and rushed to the window. Mitya sprang back into the shadows. Fyodor Pavlovich unlatched the window and thrust his whole head out.

<!-- B08-C04-P006 -->
“Grushenka, is it you? Is that you?” he said in a strange, trembling half whisper. “Where are you, my darling, my little angel, where are you?” He was terribly agitated, gasping for breath.

<!-- B08-C04-P007 -->
“Alone!” Mitya decided.

<!-- B08-C04-P008 -->
“Where are you?” the old man called again, sticking his head out farther still, his shoulders too, looking all around, to right and left. “Come here. I’ve got a little present ready. Come, I’ll show you!…”

<!-- B08-C04-P009 -->
“He means the envelope with the three thousand,” flashed through Mitya’s mind.

<!-- B08-C04-P010 -->
“But where?… Are you at the door? I’ll open it at once…”

<!-- B08-C04-P011 -->
And the old man almost climbed out of the window, looking to the right, toward the door into the garden, trying to see into the dark. In another second he would certainly have run to unlock the door without waiting for Grushenka’s answer. Mitya watched from the side, motionless. The old man’s whole profile, so hateful to him—his pendulous Adam’s apple, his hooked nose, his lips smiling in sweet anticipation—was brightly lit by the slanting lamplight from the room on the left. A terrible, savage rage suddenly boiled up in Mitya’s heart. “There he is, his rival, his tormentor, the tormentor of his life!” It was a surge of that same sudden, vengeful, savage rage he had foretold to Alyosha, as if sensing it in advance, during their talk in the summerhouse four days earlier, when he answered Alyosha’s question, “How can you say you’ll kill our father?”

<!-- B08-C04-P012 -->
“I don’t know, I don’t know,” he had said then. “Perhaps I won’t kill him, perhaps I will. I’m afraid his face may suddenly become hateful to me at that very moment. I hate his Adam’s apple, his nose, his eyes, his shameless sneer. I feel a personal revulsion. That’s what I’m afraid of—that I won’t hold myself back…”

<!-- B08-C04-P013 -->
The personal revulsion grew unbearable. Mitya no longer knew what he was doing and suddenly snatched the brass pestle from his pocket…

“God,” as Mitya himself said afterward, “was watching over me then”: at that very moment the sick Grigory Vasilievich woke in his bed. Toward evening that day he had undergone the familiar treatment Smerdyakov had described to Ivan Fyodorovich: with his wife’s help he had rubbed himself all over with vodka containing some secret, extraordinarily strong infusion, then drunk the rest with “a certain prayer” his wife whispered over it, and gone to bed. Marfa Ignatyevna had taken some too and, being no drinker, was sleeping like the dead beside her husband. But now, quite unexpectedly, Grigory suddenly woke in the night, collected his thoughts for a moment, and, though immediately feeling the burning pain in his lower back again, sat up in bed. Then he thought something over once more, got up, and hurriedly dressed. Perhaps his conscience pricked him because he was asleep while the house had no one to watch it “at such a dangerous time.” Smerdyakov, struck down by epilepsy, lay motionless in another little room. Marfa Ignatyevna did not stir. “The woman’s worn out,” Grigory Vasilievich thought, looking at her, and went out onto the steps with a groan. Of course, he meant only to look out from the steps, for he was in no condition to walk: the pain in his lower back and right leg was unbearable. But suddenly he remembered that he had not locked the garden gate that evening. He was an exceptionally orderly and precise man, a man of settled routine and habits of many years. Limping and writhing with pain, he came down the steps and went toward the garden. Just as he thought: the gate stood wide open. He stepped mechanically into the garden. Perhaps he imagined something, perhaps he heard some sound, but when he looked to the left he saw his master’s window open—an empty window now, with no one looking out. “Why is it open? It isn’t summer!” Grigory thought, and suddenly, at that very moment, something extraordinary flickered through the garden directly ahead of him. Some forty paces away a man seemed to be running through the dark; a shadow was moving very fast. “Lord!” Grigory said, and, beside himself, forgetting the pain in his back, he ran to cut off the fugitive. He took a shorter route; evidently he knew the garden better than the fleeing man, who was making for the bathhouse, running behind it, rushing toward the wall… Grigory followed him without losing sight of him, running headlong. He reached the fence at the very moment the fugitive was climbing over. With a frantic yell Grigory lunged and seized his leg in both hands.

<!-- B08-C04-P016 -->
Yes, his foreboding had been right: he recognized him, it was he, the “monster, the parricide”!

<!-- B08-C04-P017 -->
“Parricide!” the old man shouted for the whole neighborhood to hear, but that was all he managed to shout; suddenly he fell as though struck by lightning. Mitya jumped back into the garden and bent over the fallen man. He had the brass pestle in his hands and mechanically tossed it into the grass. It fell two paces from Grigory, but not in the grass: it landed on the path, in the most conspicuous place. For several seconds he examined the man lying before him. The old man’s head was covered in blood; Mitya reached out and began feeling it. He remembered clearly afterward that at that moment he had desperately wanted to “make absolutely certain” whether he had fractured the old man’s skull or merely “stunned” him with the pestle on the crown of his head. But the blood was flowing, flowing terribly, and instantly covered Mitya’s trembling fingers in a hot stream. He remembered snatching from his pocket the new white handkerchief he had brought for his visit to Khokhlakov, and pressing it to the old man’s head, senselessly trying to wipe the blood from his forehead and face. But the handkerchief too was instantly soaked through with blood. “Lord, why am I doing this?” Mitya suddenly came to himself. “If I’ve cracked it, how can I tell now?… And what difference does it make now anyway!” he suddenly added in despair. “If I’ve killed him, I’ve killed him… You got in the way, old man, so lie there!” he said aloud, and suddenly rushed at the fence, vaulted into the lane, and ran. The blood-soaked handkerchief was crumpled in his right fist, and as he ran he thrust it into the back pocket of his frock coat. He ran headlong, and the few scattered pedestrians he encountered in the dark in the town’s streets remembered afterward meeting a man running furiously that night. Once more he was flying toward Morozov’s house. Earlier, the moment he left, Fenya had rushed to the senior yard porter, Nazar Ivanovich, and begun imploring him “for Christ’s sake” not to “let the captain in again, either today or tomorrow.” Nazar Ivanovich heard her out and agreed, but unluckily he went upstairs to the mistress, who had suddenly sent for him. On his way, meeting his nephew, a lad of about twenty who had only recently arrived from the village, he told him to stay in the yard, but forgot to give him the order about the captain. Reaching the gates, Mitya knocked. The lad recognized him at once: Mitya had already tipped him more than once. He immediately opened the wicket, let him in, and, smiling cheerfully, hastened obligingly to inform him that “Agrafena Alexandrovna isn’t at home just now, sir.”

<!-- B08-C04-P018 -->
“Where is she, Prokhor?” Mitya suddenly stopped.

<!-- B08-C04-P019 -->
“She left earlier, about two hours ago, with Timofey, for Mokroye.”

<!-- B08-C04-P020 -->
“What for?” Mitya shouted.

<!-- B08-C04-P021 -->
“I couldn’t say, sir. To see some officer. Somebody sent for her from there, and they sent horses…”

<!-- B08-C04-P022 -->
Mitya left him and ran in to Fenya like a madman.
