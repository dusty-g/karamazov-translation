# Book XI — Brother Ivan Fyodorovich
## VI. The First Meeting with Smerdyakov

<!-- B11-C06-P001 -->
This was the third time Ivan Fyodorovich had gone to speak to Smerdyakov since returning from Moscow. He had first seen him and spoken to him after the catastrophe on the very day of his arrival, then visited him again two weeks later. After that second visit, however, he had stopped seeing Smerdyakov, so that it was now more than a month since he had seen him or heard almost anything about him. Ivan Fyodorovich had returned from Moscow only on the fifth day after his father's death, and had not even seen him in his coffin: the funeral had taken place the day before he arrived. The reason for Ivan Fyodorovich's delay was that Alyosha, who did not know his exact address in Moscow, had turned to Katerina Ivanovna to send a telegram, and she, equally ignorant of his present address, had telegraphed her sister and aunt, expecting Ivan Fyodorovich to visit them as soon as he reached Moscow. But he went to see them only on the fourth day after his arrival, and on reading the telegram had, of course, rushed headlong back to us. The first person he met here was Alyosha, but after talking to him he was greatly astonished to find that he would not even entertain a suspicion of Mitya, and pointed straight to Smerdyakov as the murderer, contrary to every other opinion in our town. Having then seen the district police chief and the prosecutor and learned the details of the charge and the arrest, he was still more astonished at Alyosha, and put his opinion down entirely to his brotherly feeling, stirred to its utmost, and his compassion for Mitya, whom, as Ivan knew, Alyosha loved very much. Incidentally, let us say a word or two, once and for all, about Ivan's feelings toward his brother Dmitri Fyodorovich: he decidedly did not love him, and the very most he ever felt for him was an occasional compassion, itself mixed with great contempt that verged on disgust. Everything about Mitya, even his whole physical presence, was intensely distasteful to him. He regarded Katerina Ivanovna's love for him with indignation. Nevertheless, he also saw Mitya, now the accused, on the first day of his arrival, and this meeting, far from weakening his conviction of his guilt, strengthened it. He found his brother restless and painfully agitated. Mitya talked a great deal, but was distracted and all over the place, spoke very sharply, accused Smerdyakov, and got himself terribly confused. Above all he kept talking about those same three thousand the dead man had “stolen” from him. “It was my money, it belonged to me,” Mitya kept repeating. “Even if I'd stolen it, I'd have been in the right.” He scarcely contested any of the evidence against him, and when he did interpret the facts in his own favor, it was again in a thoroughly confused and absurd fashion—as though, on the whole, he had no wish to justify himself to Ivan or anyone else. On the contrary, he grew angry, dismissed the accusations with proud contempt, swore, and flew into a rage. He merely laughed scornfully at Grigory's testimony about the open door, insisting that “the devil opened it.” But he could provide no coherent explanation of the fact. In this first meeting he even managed to insult Ivan Fyodorovich, telling him sharply that people who themselves maintained that “everything is permitted” had no business suspecting and questioning him. Altogether, he was very unfriendly to Ivan Fyodorovich on this occasion. Immediately after that meeting with Mitya, Ivan Fyodorovich went to Smerdyakov.

Even on the train, racing back from Moscow, he had kept thinking about Smerdyakov and his last conversation with him, on the evening before his departure. Much of it troubled him; much seemed suspicious. But when giving his evidence to the judicial investigator, Ivan Fyodorovich had said nothing about that conversation for the time being. He had put everything off until he had seen Smerdyakov. The latter was then in the town hospital. Doctor Herzenstube and Doctor Varvinsky, whom Ivan Fyodorovich met at the hospital, answered his insistent questions firmly: there was no doubt about Smerdyakov's epilepsy. They were even surprised at the question, “Could he have been pretending on the day of the catastrophe?” They gave him to understand that the seizure had been an extraordinary one, persisting and recurring over several days, so that the patient's life had been in serious danger, and that only now, after the treatment administered, could they positively say that he would survive, although it was very possible, Doctor Herzenstube added, that his reason would remain partially impaired, “if not for life, then for a considerable time.” To Ivan Fyodorovich's impatient question, “So he's mad now?” they replied that “it has not yet reached that point in the full sense, but certain abnormalities are observable.” Ivan Fyodorovich decided to discover for himself what these abnormalities were. He was admitted at once. Smerdyakov was in a separate room, lying on a cot. Beside him was another cot, occupied by a paralyzed townsman, swollen all over with dropsy and apparently about to die tomorrow or the day after; he could not interfere with their conversation. At the sight of Ivan Fyodorovich, Smerdyakov gave a distrustful grin and for the first instant even seemed frightened. At least, that was the impression that flashed through Ivan Fyodorovich's mind. But it lasted only an instant; throughout the rest of the visit, on the contrary, Smerdyakov almost astonished him with his composure. His first glance convinced Ivan Fyodorovich beyond doubt that the man was genuinely and extremely ill: he was very weak, spoke slowly, as though his tongue was difficult to move, and had grown very thin and yellow. Throughout their twenty-minute meeting he complained of a headache and aching in every limb. His dry, eunuchlike face seemed to have become quite small; the hair at his temples was tousled, and instead of his tuft only one fine little strand stuck up. But the narrowed left eye, seemingly hinting at something, betrayed the old Smerdyakov. “It's always interesting talking to an intelligent man,” Ivan Fyodorovich immediately recalled. He sat down on a stool at the foot of the bed. Smerdyakov shifted his whole body painfully on the bed, but did not speak first. He remained silent and now looked at him without much apparent curiosity.

“Can you talk to me?” Ivan Fyodorovich asked. “I won't tire you too much.”

“Only today... To clear up this mess of yours here.”

“What are you sighing for? You knew, didn't you?” Ivan Fyodorovich blurted out.

Smerdyakov maintained a weighty silence.

“How could I not have known, sir? It was plain beforehand. Only how could I have known they'd go about it that way?”

“Go about what? Don't wriggle out of it! You predicted yourself that you'd have a fit the moment you went down into the cellar. You specifically said the cellar.”

“Have you already stated that when they questioned you?” Smerdyakov inquired calmly.

Ivan Fyodorovich suddenly grew angry.

<!-- B11-C06-P013 -->
“No, I haven't yet, but I certainly will. There are a great many things you have to explain to me now, my friend, and understand this, my dear fellow: I won't let you play games with me!”

“Why would I play such games, sir, when all my hope rests in you, in you alone, as in the Lord God himself?” Smerdyakov said, still perfectly calmly, merely closing his eyes for a moment.

“First of all,” Ivan Fyodorovich began, “I know you can't predict an epileptic fit. I've made inquiries, so don't wriggle. You can't predict the day and hour. How was it, then, that you predicted both the day and the hour to me, and the cellar into the bargain? How could you know in advance that you'd fall into that particular cellar in a fit, unless you deliberately faked the fit?”

“I had to go down into the cellar in any case, sir, several times a day, even,” Smerdyakov drawled unhurriedly. “Just a year ago I fell out of the attic in exactly the same way. Certainly you can't predict a fit beforehand to the day and hour, but you can always have a foreboding.”

“But you predicted the day and hour!”

<!-- B11-C06-P018 -->
“As for my epilepsy, sir, you'd best ask the doctors here whether it was genuine or not. I have nothing more to tell you on the subject.”

<!-- B11-C06-P019 -->
“And the cellar? How did you foresee the cellar?”

“You do keep on about that cellar! So I'm going down into that very cellar, thinking, ‘Now it'll come, now it'll strike—will I fall or won't I?’ And that very doubt suddenly brought that inevitable spasm clutching at my throat, sir... and down I went. All of that, and our whole conversation the evening before, at the gate, when I told you about my fear and about the cellar—I told it all in detail to Doctor Herzenstube and to the investigator, Nikolai Parfenovich, and they put it all in the record, sir. And Doctor Varvinsky here particularly insisted in front of them all that it had happened precisely from thinking about it—from that very apprehension, that is: ‘Will I fall or won't I?’ And that was when it took hold of me. They put it down that way, sir: that it was bound to happen just as it did, solely from my fear.”

“So you've already stated all this in your evidence?” asked Ivan Fyodorovich, somewhat taken aback. He had meant to frighten him precisely by threatening to disclose their conversation, and it turned out that the other man had already disclosed it all himself.

“What have I to fear? Let them write down the whole true truth,” Smerdyakov said firmly.

<!-- B11-C06-P024 -->
“And you told them our whole conversation at the gate, word for word?”

“No, not exactly every word, sir.”

<!-- B11-C06-P026 -->
“Did you tell them you know how to fake a fit, as you boasted to me then?”

<!-- B11-C06-P027 -->
“No, I didn't tell them that either, sir.”

<!-- B11-C06-P028 -->
“Now tell me why you were sending me off to Chermashnya.”

<!-- B11-C06-P029 -->
“I was afraid you'd go to Moscow. Chermashnya is closer, after all, sir.”

What happened was that Ivan Fyodorovich abruptly walked out, and only after he had gone ten paces along the corridor did he suddenly feel there was something offensive in Smerdyakov's last sentence. He was about to go back, but the impulse merely flickered through his mind, and with a “Nonsense!” he hurried out of the hospital. Above all, he felt that his mind really had been set at rest, precisely by the fact that it was not Smerdyakov who was guilty but his brother Mitya, although one would have expected the opposite. Why this was so he did not care to examine then; he even felt a revulsion at probing his own feelings. He wanted, as it were, to forget something as quickly as possible. Then, over the next few days, he became completely convinced of Mitya's guilt as he acquired a closer and more thorough knowledge of all the evidence weighing against him. There was testimony from the most insignificant people that was almost shattering—for example, from Fenya and her mother. Perkhotin, the tavern, Plotnikov's shop, the witnesses at Mokroye went without saying. Above all, it was the details that weighed against him. The news of the secret “knocks” struck the investigator and the prosecutor almost as forcibly as Grigory's testimony about the open door. When Ivan Fyodorovich questioned Grigory's wife, Marfa Ignatyevna, she stated outright that Smerdyakov had lain behind their partition all night, “not three paces from our bed,” and that although she herself had slept soundly, she had woken many times on hearing him groan there: “He groaned the whole time, groaned without stopping.” When he spoke to Herzenstube and gave his opinion that Smerdyakov seemed to him not at all deranged, merely weak, he only elicited a thin little smile from the old man. “Do you know what he's particularly occupied with now?” he asked Ivan Fyodorovich. “Learning French vocabulary by heart. He has a notebook under his pillow, with French words written in Russian letters by someone, heh-heh-heh!” Ivan Fyodorovich at last abandoned all doubt. He could no longer even think of his brother Dmitri without loathing. One thing remained strange: Alyosha stubbornly maintained that it was not Dmitri who had killed him, but “in all probability” Smerdyakov. Ivan had always valued Alyosha's opinion highly and was therefore now greatly puzzled by him. It was strange, too, that Alyosha did not seek conversations with him about Mitya and never began one himself, but merely answered Ivan's questions. Ivan Fyodorovich noticed this particularly as well. At that time, however, his attention was greatly distracted by a wholly unrelated circumstance: in the very first days after his return from Moscow, he had given himself wholly and irrevocably to his burning, mad passion for Katerina Ivanovna. This is not the place to begin the story of that new passion of Ivan Fyodorovich's, which was to affect his whole subsequent life: all of it might form the basis of another story, another novel, which I do not know whether I shall ever undertake. Still, I cannot leave unmentioned even now that when Ivan Fyodorovich, walking with Alyosha from Katerina Ivanovna's that night, as I have already described, said, “I'm not after her,” he was lying terribly at that moment: he loved her madly, although it is also true that at times he hated her so much that he might even have killed her. Many causes came together here. Shattered by what had happened to Mitya, she had flung herself upon Ivan Fyodorovich when he returned to her, as upon some savior of her own. She had been wounded, insulted, humiliated in her feelings. And now here was a man once again who had loved her so much before—oh, she knew it only too well—and whose mind and heart she had always placed so far above her own. But the stern young woman did not give herself up entirely as a sacrifice, despite all the Karamazov abandon of her lover's desires and all the charm he held for her. At the same time she was constantly tormented by remorse for betraying Mitya, and in stormy moments of quarrelling with Ivan—and there were many—she told him so outright. This was what he had called “lie upon lie” when speaking to Alyosha. There certainly were many lies here, and that was what irritated Ivan Fyodorovich most of all... but all that later. In short, for a time he almost forgot about Smerdyakov. And yet, two weeks after his first visit, those same strange thoughts began tormenting him again as before. Suffice it to say that he began asking himself incessantly why, on his last night in Fyodor Pavlovich's house, before his departure, he had crept out onto the stairs like a thief and listened to what his father was doing below. Why did he remember it afterward with revulsion? Why had he suddenly felt so miserable on the road the next morning, and why had he said to himself as he entered Moscow, “I'm a scoundrel”? And now, one day, it occurred to him that because of all these tormenting thoughts he might even be ready to forget Katerina Ivanovna, so powerfully had they suddenly taken hold of him again! Just as he thought this, he met Alyosha in the street. He stopped him at once and suddenly asked:

But at that moment, immediately after their encounter, Ivan Fyodorovich, without going home, suddenly set off to see Smerdyakov again.
