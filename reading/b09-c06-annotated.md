# Book IX: The Preliminary Investigation
## VI. The Prosecutor Catches Mitya

<!-- B09-C06-P001 -->
Something began that was entirely unexpected and astonishing to Mitya. He could never have imagined beforehand, even a minute earlier, that anyone could treat him like this—him, Mitya Karamazov! Above all, there was something humiliating about it, and on their side something “arrogant and contemptuous toward him.” Taking off his frock coat would have been all right, but they asked him to go on undressing. And they did not so much ask as order him, in effect; he understood that perfectly. Out of pride and contempt he complied completely, without a word. Besides Nikolai Parfenovich, the prosecutor also went behind the curtain, and several peasants were present, “to provide force, of course,” Mitya thought, “or perhaps for something else as well.”

<!-- B09-C06-P002 -->
“What, must I take off my shirt too?” he asked sharply, but Nikolai Parfenovich did not answer. He and the prosecutor were absorbed in examining the frock coat, trousers, waistcoat, and cap, and both were plainly very interested in the inspection. “They don't stand on ceremony at all,” flashed through Mitya's mind. “They don't even observe the necessary courtesies.”

<!-- B09-C06-P003 -->
“I'm asking you for the second time: do I have to take off my shirt or not?” he said still more sharply and irritably.

<!-- B09-C06-P004 -->
“Don't worry, we shall let you know,” Nikolai Parfenovich replied, with something of a superior's authority. At least, that was how it seemed to Mitya.

<!-- B09-C06-P005 -->
Meanwhile, the investigator and prosecutor were conferring anxiously in undertones. There were enormous bloodstains on the frock coat, especially on the left skirt at the back, dried and crusted, and scarcely yet rubbed down. There were stains on the trousers too. In addition, Nikolai Parfenovich himself, in the presence of the official witnesses, ran his fingers over the collar, cuffs, and every seam of the coat and trousers, evidently searching for something—money, of course. Above all, they made no attempt to hide from Mitya their suspicion that he could have, and was capable of having, sewn money into his clothes. “This is treating me outright as a thief, not an officer,” he grumbled to himself. They communicated their thoughts to one another with extraordinary frankness in his presence. For example, the clerk, who had also appeared behind the curtain and was bustling about helping, drew Nikolai Parfenovich's attention to the cap, which they also felt over. “Remember Gridyenko the clerk, sir,” he observed. “This summer he went to collect the salaries for the whole office, and when he came back said he'd lost the money while drunk. And where did they find it? In this very piping, sir, in his cap—the hundred-ruble notes were rolled into little tubes and sewn into the piping.” Both the investigator and prosecutor remembered the affair with Gridyenko very well, and so Mitya's cap was set aside too, and they decided that all of it would have to be examined thoroughly later, along with the rest of his clothes.

<!-- B09-C06-P006 -->
“One moment!” Nikolai Parfenovich suddenly cried, noticing the cuff on Mitya's right shirtsleeve, turned inward and completely soaked with blood. “One moment, sir, what is this—blood?”

<!-- B09-C06-P007 -->
“Blood,” Mitya snapped.

<!-- B09-C06-P008 -->
“That is, what blood is this, sir... and why is it turned inside the sleeve?”

<!-- B09-C06-P009 -->
Mitya explained how he had stained the cuff while dealing with Grigory and had turned it inward at Perkhotin's, when he washed his hands there.

<!-- B09-C06-P010 -->
“We shall have to take your shirt too. This is very important... as material evidence.” Mitya flushed and grew furious.

<!-- B09-C06-P011 -->
“What, am I to stay naked?” he shouted.

<!-- B09-C06-P012 -->
“Don't worry... We shall arrange something. For now, please remove your socks as well.”

<!-- B09-C06-P013 -->
“Are you joking? Is that really so necessary?” Mitya's eyes flashed.

<!-- B09-C06-P014 -->
“We are in no position to joke,” Nikolai Parfenovich retorted sternly.

<!-- B09-C06-P015 -->
“Well, if it's necessary... I...” Mitya muttered, and, sitting on the bed, began taking off his socks. He felt unbearably embarrassed: everyone else was dressed, and he was undressed; and, strangely, undressed, he himself seemed to feel guilty before them, and, above all, he almost agreed that he had indeed suddenly become inferior to all of them and that now they had every right to despise him. “If everyone's undressed, there's no shame, but one man undressed and everyone looking—disgraceful!” flashed through his mind again and again. “Just like a dream. I've sometimes dreamed of humiliations like this happening to me.” But taking off his socks was positively agonizing: they were very dirty, as was his underwear, and now everyone could see it. Above all, he disliked his own feet; for some reason, all his life he had thought the big toes on both feet ugly, especially one coarse, flat nail on his right foot that curved downward somehow, and now they would all see it. His unbearable shame suddenly made him even ruder, and now deliberately so. He tore off his shirt himself.

<!-- B09-C06-P016 -->
“Would you like to search anywhere else, if you're not ashamed to?”

<!-- B09-C06-P017 -->
“No, sir, not necessary for the moment.”

<!-- B09-C06-P018 -->
“Well, am I to stay naked like this?” he added furiously.

<!-- B09-C06-P019 -->
“Yes, that is necessary for now... Please sit down here for the moment. You can take the blanket from the bed and wrap yourself in it, and I... I shall arrange everything.”

<!-- B09-C06-P020 -->
All the articles were shown to the official witnesses, a record of the examination was drawn up, and finally Nikolai Parfenovich went out, with the clothes carried out after him. Ippolit Kirillovich left too. Only the peasants remained with Mitya, standing silently, never taking their eyes off him. Mitya wrapped himself in the blanket; he had begun to feel cold. His bare feet stuck out, and however he pulled the blanket over them, he could not cover them. Nikolai Parfenovich was a long time returning, “a torturously long time.” “He thinks I'm a puppy,” Mitya ground his teeth. “That vermin of a prosecutor has gone too, surely out of contempt: he found the sight of a naked man disgusting.” Mitya still supposed they would examine his clothes somewhere out there and bring them back. Imagine his indignation, then, when Nikolai Parfenovich suddenly returned with an entirely different set of clothes, carried behind him by a peasant.

<!-- B09-C06-P021 -->
“Well, here are some clothes for you,” he said breezily, apparently very pleased with the success of his errand. “Mr. Kalganov is contributing them for this curious occasion, along with a clean shirt for you. Fortunately, he happened to have all this in his suitcase. You may keep your own underwear and socks.”

<!-- B09-C06-P022 -->
Mitya flew into a terrible rage.

<!-- B09-C06-P023 -->
“I don't want someone else's clothes!” he shouted menacingly. “Give me mine!”

<!-- B09-C06-P024 -->
“Impossible.”

<!-- B09-C06-P025 -->
“Give me mine! To hell with Kalganov, his clothes and him too!”

<!-- B09-C06-P026 -->
They spent a long time persuading him. Somehow, however, they calmed him. They explained that his clothes, being stained with blood, must “be added to the collection of material evidence,” and that they now “had no right even” to leave them on him, “in view of the possible outcome of the case.” At last Mitya more or less understood. He fell gloomily silent and began dressing hurriedly. As he put on the clothes, he remarked only that they were finer than his old ones and that he did not want to “take advantage.” Besides, they were “humiliatingly tight. Am I supposed to play the buffoon in them... for your enjoyment?”

<!-- B09-C06-P027 -->
They again explained that he was exaggerating here too, that although Mr. Kalganov was taller than he was, it was only by a little, and perhaps the trousers alone would be slightly too long. But the coat did prove tight across the shoulders.

<!-- B09-C06-P028 -->
“Damn it, I can hardly button it,” Mitya began grumbling again. “Do me a favor and tell Mr. Kalganov from me this instant that I didn't ask him for his clothes and that it was you who dressed me up as a clown.”

<!-- B09-C06-P029 -->
“He understands that very well and is sorry... that is, not sorry about his clothes, but about this whole affair...” Nikolai Parfenovich began mumbling.

<!-- B09-C06-P030 -->
“To hell with his sympathy! Well, where now? Or am I to keep sitting here?”

<!-- B09-C06-P031 -->
They asked him to return to “that room.” Mitya went out, scowling with anger and trying not to look at anyone. In another man's clothes he felt utterly disgraced, even before these peasants and Trifon Borisovich, whose face suddenly appeared in the doorway for some reason and disappeared again. “Came to take a look at the man in fancy dress,” Mitya thought. He sat down on his former chair. Something nightmarish and absurd seemed to surround him; he felt as though he were out of his mind.

<!-- B09-C06-P032 -->
“Well, what now? Will you start flogging me with rods? There's nothing else left,” he said through grinding teeth, addressing the prosecutor. He no longer even wanted to turn toward Nikolai Parfenovich, as though disdaining to speak to him. “He examined my socks far too closely, and then the scoundrel told me to turn them inside out. He did that on purpose, to show everyone how dirty my underclothes were!”

<!-- B09-C06-P033 -->
“Well, we must now proceed to question the witnesses,” Nikolai Parfenovich said, as though answering Dmitri Fyodorovich's question.

<!-- B09-C06-P034 -->
“Yes, sir,” the prosecutor said thoughtfully, as though he too were considering something.

<!-- B09-C06-P035 -->
“We have done everything we could in your own interests, Dmitri Fyodorovich,” Nikolai Parfenovich continued, “but having received so categorical a refusal on your part to explain the origin of the sum found in your possession, we are at this moment...”

<!-- B09-C06-P036 -->
“What is your ring made of?” Mitya suddenly interrupted, as though emerging from a reverie, pointing at one of the three large rings adorning Nikolai Parfenovich's little right hand.

<!-- B09-C06-P037 -->
“My ring?” Nikolai Parfenovich repeated in surprise.

<!-- B09-C06-P038 -->
“Yes, that one... on your middle finger, with the veins in it. What stone is that?” Mitya persisted irritably, like a stubborn child.

<!-- B09-C06-P039 -->
“It's a smoky topaz,” Nikolai Parfenovich smiled. “Would you like to see it? I'll take it off...”

<!-- B09-C06-P040 -->
“No, no, don't take it off!” Mitya shouted furiously, suddenly coming to himself and growing angry with himself. “Don't take it off, there's no need... The devil... Gentlemen, you've befouled my soul! Do you really think I'd have concealed it from you if I had actually killed my father, that I'd have dodged and lied and hidden? No, Dmitri Karamazov isn't like that; he couldn't have borne it, and if I were guilty, I swear I wouldn't have waited for you to arrive here or for sunrise, as I originally intended, but would have destroyed myself earlier, without waiting for dawn! I feel it now, I know myself. I couldn't have learned in twenty years of life as much as I've learned in this accursed night!.. And would I have been like this, like this, tonight and at this very moment, sitting here with you—would I speak like this, move like this, look at you and the world like this, if I really were a parricide, when even that accidental killing of Grigory gave me no peace all night—and not from fear, oh, not merely from fear of your punishment! Shameful! And you want me to reveal and tell yet another vile deed of mine, another disgrace, to scoffers like you, who see nothing and believe nothing, blind moles and scoffers, even if it would save me from your accusation? I'd sooner go to penal servitude! The man who opened the door to my father and went in by that door is the man who killed him, the man who robbed him. Who he is—I am lost and tormented by it, but he is not Dmitri Karamazov, know that—and that's all I can tell you, and enough, stop pestering me... Exile me, execute me, but don't provoke me anymore. I am silent. Call your witnesses!”

<!-- B09-C06-P041 -->
Mitya delivered his sudden monologue as though he had now firmly resolved to say nothing more, once and for all. The prosecutor watched him throughout, and as soon as he fell silent, suddenly said with the coldest and calmest expression, as though it were the most ordinary thing:

<!-- B09-C06-P042 -->
“Precisely concerning that open door you have just mentioned, we can, very appropriately, inform you at this very moment of an extraordinarily interesting and highly important piece of evidence, for both you and us, given by the old man you wounded, Grigory Vasilyev. When he regained consciousness, he told us clearly and insistently, in answer to our questions, that when he came out onto the porch, heard a noise in the garden, and decided to enter it through the little gate, which stood unlocked, he noticed, on entering the garden—even before he saw you in the darkness running away, as you have already told us, from the open window where you had seen your father—that is, he, Grigory, looked to his left and did indeed notice the open window, and at the same time, much nearer to him, the door standing wide open, which you stated remained locked the entire time you were in the garden. I shall not conceal from you that Vasilyev himself firmly concludes and testifies that you must have run out of the door, although, of course, he did not actually see you run out, first noticing you already some distance from him, in the middle of the garden, running toward the fence...”

<!-- B09-C06-P043 -->
Halfway through this speech Mitya had already sprung from his chair.

<!-- B09-C06-P044 -->
“Nonsense!” he suddenly howled in a frenzy. “A brazen lie! He couldn't have seen the door open, because it was locked then... He's lying!..”

<!-- B09-C06-P045 -->
“I consider it my duty to repeat that his testimony is firm. He does not waver. He stands by it. We questioned him several times.”

<!-- B09-C06-P046 -->
“Indeed, I questioned him several times!” Nikolai Parfenovich warmly confirmed.

<!-- B09-C06-P047 -->
“Not true, not true! It's either slander against me or a madman's hallucination,” Mitya continued shouting. “It's simply that he imagined it when he came to, delirious, covered in blood, from his wound... So he's raving.”

<!-- B09-C06-P048 -->
“Yes, sir, but he noticed the open door not when he recovered consciousness after his injury, but before that, when he was just entering the garden from the wing.”

<!-- B09-C06-P049 -->
“But it's not true, not true, it can't be! He's slandering me out of spite... He couldn't have seen it... I didn't run out of the door,” Mitya gasped.

<!-- B09-C06-P050 -->
The prosecutor turned to Nikolai Parfenovich and said emphatically:

<!-- B09-C06-P051 -->
“Produce it.”

<!-- B09-C06-P052 -->
“Do you recognize this object?” Nikolai Parfenovich suddenly placed on the table a large official-size envelope of thick paper, on which three seals still remained visible. The envelope itself was empty and torn open along one side. Mitya stared at it, his eyes bulging.

<!-- B09-C06-P053 -->
“That... that must be my father's envelope,” he murmured. “The very one with the three thousand in it... and if there's an inscription, let me see: ‘to my little chick’... there: three thousand!” he cried. “Three thousand, you see?”

<!-- B09-C06-P054 -->
“Yes, sir, we see, but we found no money left in it. It was empty, lying on the floor beside the bed, behind the screens.”

<!-- B09-C06-P055 -->
For several seconds Mitya stood as though stunned.

<!-- B09-C06-P056 -->
“Gentlemen, it's Smerdyakov!” he suddenly shouted with all his strength. “He killed him, he robbed him! Only he knew where the old man had hidden the envelope... It's him, now it's clear!”

<!-- B09-C06-P057 -->
“But you too knew about the envelope and that it lay under the pillow.”

<!-- B09-C06-P058 -->
“I never knew. I'd never even seen it at all; this is the first time I've seen it, before this I'd only heard of it from Smerdyakov... He alone knew where the old man had hidden it, and I didn't know...” Mitya was almost choking.

<!-- B09-C06-P059 -->
“And yet you yourself testified earlier that the envelope lay under your late father's pillow. You specifically said under the pillow, so you did know where it was.”

<!-- B09-C06-P060 -->
“That is what we recorded!” Nikolai Parfenovich confirmed.

<!-- B09-C06-P061 -->
“Nonsense, absurd! I didn't know at all that it was under the pillow. Perhaps it wasn't under the pillow at all... I said under the pillow at random... What does Smerdyakov say? Did you ask him where it was? What does Smerdyakov say? That's the main thing... And I deliberately told a lie against myself... I lied to you without thinking when I said it lay under the pillow, and now you... Well, you know, something slips off your tongue, and you tell a lie. But only Smerdyakov knew, only Smerdyakov, no one else!.. He didn't even tell me where it was! But it's him, it's him; he undoubtedly killed him, now it's as clear as daylight to me,” Mitya kept exclaiming, more and more frenziedly, repeating himself incoherently, growing heated and bitter. “Understand that and arrest him quickly, quickly... He killed him when I'd run away and Grigory was lying unconscious, it's clear now... He gave the signals and Father opened to him... Because only he knew the signals, and without them Father wouldn't have opened to anyone...”

<!-- B09-C06-P062 -->
“But once again you forget the fact,” the prosecutor observed, still with the same restraint, but now with something like triumph, “that there was no need to give any signals if the door was already unlocked while you were there, while you were still in the garden...”

<!-- B09-C06-P063 -->
“The door, the door,” Mitya murmured, staring silently at the prosecutor. Powerless, he sank back into his chair. Everyone fell silent.

<!-- B09-C06-P064 -->
“Yes, the door!.. It's a phantom! God is against me!” he exclaimed, staring in front of him now without a thought.

<!-- B09-C06-P065 -->
“There, you see,” the prosecutor said gravely. “Now judge for yourself, Dmitri Fyodorovich: on the one hand, this evidence about the open door from which you ran, overwhelming both you and us. On the other, your incomprehensible, stubborn, almost embittered silence concerning the origin of the money that suddenly appeared in your hands, when, only three hours before you had that sum, you pawned your pistols, by your own testimony, to obtain a mere ten rubles! In view of all this, decide for yourself: what are we to believe, and what conclusion are we to reach? And don't resent us as ‘cold cynics and scoffers’ incapable of believing in the noble impulses of your soul... Try, on the contrary, to appreciate our position too...”

<!-- B09-C06-P066 -->
Mitya was in unimaginable agitation; he had turned pale.

<!-- B09-C06-P067 -->
“Very well!” he suddenly cried. “I'll reveal my secret, tell you where I got the money!.. I'll reveal my disgrace, so I won't blame either you or myself afterward...”

<!-- B09-C06-P068 -->
“And believe me, Dmitri Fyodorovich,” Nikolai Parfenovich took up in a tender, joyful little voice, “any sincere and full confession you make at this very moment may subsequently bring about an immeasurable mitigation of your fate, and even, besides...”

<!-- B09-C06-P069 -->
But the prosecutor nudged him gently under the table, and he managed to stop in time. Mitya, however, was not even listening to him.
