# Book XII: A Judicial Error
## IV. Fortune Smiles on Mitya

<!-- B12-C04-P001 -->
It happened quite unexpectedly, even for Alyosha himself. He was called without being sworn, and I remember that from the very first questions everyone on both sides treated him with extraordinary gentleness and sympathy. Clearly his good reputation had preceded him. Alyosha testified modestly and with restraint, but his ardent sympathy for his unfortunate brother plainly broke through his evidence. In answer to one question, he described his brother as a man who might be violent and carried away by his passions, but who was also noble, proud, and generous, ready even to sacrifice himself if it were asked of him. He admitted, however, that in the days before the catastrophe his brother had been in an unbearable situation because of his passion for Grushenka and his rivalry with his father. But he indignantly rejected even the suggestion that his brother could have killed in order to rob, though he acknowledged that the three thousand had become almost a mania in Mitya’s mind, that he regarded them as inheritance withheld through his father’s deceit, and that, although wholly free of greed, he could not even speak of those three thousand without frenzy and rage. About the rivalry between the two “persons,” as the prosecutor put it—Grushenka and Katya—he answered evasively, and one or two questions he refused to answer at all.

<!-- B12-C04-P002 -->
“Did your brother at least tell you that he intended to kill his father?” the prosecutor asked. “You need not answer if you consider it necessary not to,” he added.

<!-- B12-C04-P003 -->
“He didn’t say so directly,” Alyosha replied.

<!-- B12-C04-P004 -->
“How, then? Indirectly?”

<!-- B12-C04-P005 -->
“He once spoke to me of his personal hatred of our father, and said he was afraid that… at the last extremity… in a moment of revulsion… perhaps he might kill him.”

<!-- B12-C04-P006 -->
“And when you heard this, did you believe it?”

<!-- B12-C04-P007 -->
“I’m afraid to say that I did. But I was always convinced that some higher feeling would save him at the fatal moment, as indeed it did, because he did not kill my father,” Alyosha concluded firmly, in a loud voice that carried through the hall. The prosecutor started like a warhorse hearing a trumpet call.

<!-- B12-C04-P008 -->
“Be assured that I entirely believe in the complete sincerity of your conviction, without in any way qualifying it by, or identifying it with, your love for your unfortunate brother. Your particular view of the whole tragic episode that has played out in your family is already known to us from the preliminary investigation. I shall not conceal from you that it is highly unusual and contradicts all the other evidence received by the prosecution. I therefore consider it necessary to ask you, now with some insistence: precisely what facts guided your thinking and led you to a settled conviction of your brother’s innocence and, on the contrary, of the guilt of another person, whom you directly identified at the preliminary investigation?”

<!-- B12-C04-P009 -->
“At the preliminary investigation I only answered questions,” Alyosha said quietly and calmly. “I didn’t come forward myself to accuse Smerdyakov.”

<!-- B12-C04-P010 -->
“And yet you did identify him?”

<!-- B12-C04-P011 -->
“I named him on the strength of my brother Dmitri’s words. Before I was questioned, I had been told what happened when he was arrested, and how he himself named Smerdyakov then. I believe absolutely that my brother is innocent. And if he wasn’t the one who killed him, then…”

<!-- B12-C04-P012 -->
“Then Smerdyakov? Why Smerdyakov in particular? And why, precisely, are you so thoroughly convinced of your brother’s innocence?”

<!-- B12-C04-P013 -->
“I couldn’t help believing my brother. I know he wouldn’t lie to me. I could see in his face that he wasn’t lying to me.”

<!-- B12-C04-P014 -->
“Only in his face? Is that all your evidence?”

<!-- B12-C04-P015 -->
“I have no other evidence.”

<!-- B12-C04-P016 -->
“And as to Smerdyakov’s guilt, you likewise rely on not the slightest other evidence beyond your brother’s words and the expression on his face?”

<!-- B12-C04-P017 -->
“Yes, I have no other evidence.”

<!-- B12-C04-P018 -->
At this the prosecutor ended his questions. Alyosha’s answers had made a deeply disappointing impression on the audience. There had already been talk of Smerdyakov before the trial; someone had heard something, someone had pointed to something else, and people said Alyosha had amassed extraordinary evidence in his brother’s favor and of the servant’s guilt. And now—nothing, no evidence beyond a few moral convictions, so natural in the defendant’s own brother.

<!-- B12-C04-P019 -->
But then Fetyukovich began his questions. Asked exactly when the defendant had spoken to him, Alyosha, about his hatred of his father and the possibility that he might kill him, and whether he had heard this from him, for instance, at their last meeting before the catastrophe, Alyosha, as he answered, suddenly seemed to start, as though only now remembering and understanding something.

<!-- B12-C04-P020 -->
“I remember something now that I had quite forgotten myself. It was so unclear to me then, but now…”

<!-- B12-C04-P021 -->
And with excitement, plainly struck by the idea only at that instant, Alyosha recalled how, at their last meeting, in the evening by the tree on the road to the monastery, Mitya had struck his chest, “the upper part of his chest,” and repeatedly told him he had the means to restore his honor, that those means were here, right here on his chest… “At the time I thought, when he struck his chest, that he meant his heart,” Alyosha continued, “that in his heart he could find the strength to escape some terrible disgrace that lay ahead of him and that he didn’t dare admit even to me. I confess I thought at the time that he meant our father, and that the thought of going to him and committing some act of violence made him shudder as something shameful. But he seemed to be pointing to something on his chest just then, so that I remember a thought flashing through my mind at that very moment: the heart wasn’t in that part of the chest at all, but lower down, while he was striking much higher, just here, right below his neck, and kept pointing to that spot. The thought seemed foolish to me then, but perhaps what he was pointing to was precisely that pouch, with the fifteen hundred sewn inside!…”

<!-- B12-C04-P022 -->
“Exactly!” Mitya suddenly cried from his seat. “That’s right, Alyosha, that’s right! I was pounding on it with my fist!”

<!-- B12-C04-P023 -->
Fetyukovich rushed over to him, imploring him to keep calm, and at that same moment fastened on Alyosha. Carried away himself by his recollection, Alyosha ardently offered his supposition that the disgrace most probably consisted precisely in this: although he had those fifteen hundred rubles on him and could have returned them to Katerina Ivanovna as half his debt to her, he had nevertheless decided not to return that half, but to use it for something else—to take Grushenka away, if she would agree…

<!-- B12-C04-P024 -->
“That’s right, that’s exactly right!” Alyosha exclaimed in his sudden excitement. “My brother cried out to me then that half, half his disgrace—he said ‘half’ several times!—he could rid himself of at once, but that he was so wretchedly weak in character that he wouldn’t do it… He knew beforehand that he couldn’t, that he hadn’t the strength to do it!”

<!-- B12-C04-P025 -->
“And you remember firmly and clearly that it was precisely this part of his chest he struck?” Fetyukovich questioned him eagerly.

<!-- B12-C04-P026 -->
“Clearly and firmly, because I remember wondering why he was striking so high when the heart was lower down, and thinking at the time that it was a foolish thought… I remember that it seemed foolish… It flashed through my mind. That’s why I’ve remembered now. How could I have forgotten it until this moment! He was pointing precisely to that pouch to show that he had the means, but that he wouldn’t give back the fifteen hundred! And when he was arrested at Mokroye, he cried out—I know this, I was told—that he considered it the most shameful act of his entire life: that although he had the means to repay half—precisely half!—of his debt to Katerina Ivanovna, and stand before her as something other than a thief, he still couldn’t bring himself to return it, and preferred to remain a thief in her eyes rather than part with the money! And how that debt tormented him, how it tormented him!” Alyosha finished with a cry.

<!-- B12-C04-P027 -->
The prosecutor naturally intervened. He asked Alyosha to describe once more how it had all happened, and repeatedly pressed him: had the defendant really seemed to be pointing to something when he beat his chest? Perhaps he was merely beating his chest with his fist?

<!-- B12-C04-P028 -->
“It wasn’t even his fist!” Alyosha exclaimed. “He was pointing with his fingers, pointing here, very high up… But how could I have forgotten it so completely until this very minute!”

<!-- B12-C04-P029 -->
The presiding judge asked Mitya what he could say about this testimony. Mitya confirmed that everything had happened exactly so: he had indeed been pointing to his fifteen hundred, which were on his chest just below his neck, and of course it had been a disgrace, “a disgrace I don’t deny, the most shameful act of my whole life!” Mitya cried. “I could have given it back and I didn’t. I preferred to remain a thief in her eyes, but I wouldn’t give it back; and the greatest disgrace was that I knew beforehand I wouldn’t give it back! Alyosha’s right! Thank you, Alyosha!”

<!-- B12-C04-P030 -->
This ended Alyosha’s examination. The important and distinctive point was that at least one fact had been found, one piece of evidence, however tiny, almost no more than a hint of evidence, yet something that testified, if only a little, that the pouch really had existed, that there had been fifteen hundred inside it, and that the defendant had not lied during the preliminary investigation when he declared at Mokroye that those fifteen hundred “were mine.” Alyosha was delighted. Flushed all over, he went to the place assigned him. For a long time he kept repeating to himself: “How did I forget it! How could I have forgotten it! And how is it that I suddenly remembered only now!”

<!-- B12-C04-P031 -->
Katerina Ivanovna’s examination began. The moment she appeared, something extraordinary passed through the hall. The ladies seized their lorgnettes and opera glasses, the men stirred, and some rose from their seats to see better. Everyone said afterward that as soon as she entered Mitya suddenly turned “white as a handkerchief.” All in black, she approached her assigned place modestly, almost timidly. One could not have guessed from her face that she was agitated, but resolve flashed in her dark, somber gaze. I should mention that afterward a great many people said she had looked astonishingly beautiful at that moment. She began speaking quietly but clearly, so that the whole courtroom could hear. She expressed herself with extraordinary calm, or at least made an effort to be calm. The presiding judge began his questions cautiously and with the greatest respect, as though afraid of touching “certain chords,” and in deference to a great misfortune. But from the very first words Katerina Ivanovna herself, in answer to one of the questions, firmly declared that she had been the defendant’s betrothed bride “until he himself left me…” she added quietly. Asked about the three thousand entrusted to Mitya to send by post to her relatives, she said firmly: “I didn’t give it to him to post straightaway. I had a feeling then that he badly needed money… at that moment. I gave him those three thousand on the understanding that he could send them, if he wished, within a month. He needlessly tormented himself so afterward over that debt…”

<!-- B12-C04-P032 -->
I am not reproducing all the questions or her every answer exactly; I am giving only the essential meaning of her testimony.

<!-- B12-C04-P033 -->
“I was firmly convinced that he would have time to send those three thousand as soon as he received the money from his father,” she continued in answer to the questions. “I have always been convinced of his disinterestedness and his honesty… his great honesty… in money matters. He was firmly convinced he would receive three thousand rubles from his father and told me so several times. I knew he and his father were in dispute, and I always believed, and still believe, that his father had wronged him. I remember no threats against his father on his part. At least he said nothing of the kind in my presence, no threats. If he had come to me then, I would have immediately relieved his anxiety over those wretched three thousand he owed me, but he no longer came to see me… and I myself… I had been placed in such a position… that I couldn’t ask him to come… Besides, I had no right to be demanding toward him over that debt,” she suddenly added, and a note of determination rang in her voice. “I myself once received financial assistance from him amounting to more than three thousand, and accepted it despite being unable even to foresee whether I would ever be in a position to repay my debt.”

<!-- B12-C04-P034 -->
There was something like a challenge in her tone. It was just then that the questioning passed to Fetyukovich.

<!-- B12-C04-P035 -->
“This happened elsewhere, at the beginning of your acquaintance?” Fetyukovich took it up cautiously, instantly sensing something favorable ahead. (I shall note in parentheses that, although Katerina Ivanovna herself had been partly responsible for summoning him from Petersburg, he knew nothing of the episode of the five thousand Mitya had given her in that other town, or of the “bow to the ground.” She had not told him; she had concealed it! And this was astonishing. One may safely suppose that until the very last moment she herself did not know whether she would tell this story in court, and was waiting for some inspiration.)

<!-- B12-C04-P036 -->
No, I can never forget those moments! She began to tell it; she told everything, the whole episode Mitya had related to Alyosha, the “bow to the ground,” the reasons for it, about her father, about her visit to Mitya, and not by a word, not by the faintest hint did she mention that through her sister Mitya himself had proposed that they “send Katerina Ivanovna to him for the money.” This she generously concealed, and did not shrink from revealing that she, she herself, had run to the young officer of her own impulse, hoping for something… to beg money from him. It was overwhelming. I grew cold and trembled as I listened; the hall held its breath, catching every word. There was something unprecedented in it, so that even from such a self-willed and scornfully proud young woman, such supremely candid testimony, such a sacrifice, such an immolation of herself, could scarcely have been expected. And for what, for whom? To save the man who had betrayed and wronged her, to do something, however small, toward his salvation by making a favorable impression on his behalf! And indeed, the image of an officer giving away his last five thousand rubles—everything he had left in the world—and bowing respectfully before an innocent girl was profoundly sympathetic and attractive, but… my heart contracted painfully! I felt that slander might follow later—and it did, it did! Throughout the town people afterward said with malicious little laughs that perhaps the story was not entirely accurate, precisely at the point where the officer had sent the young lady away “supposedly with no more than a respectful bow.” They hinted that something had been “left out.” “And even if nothing was left out, even if it was all true,” even our most respectable ladies said, “it is still not clear whether it was a very noble thing for a young lady to do, even to save her father.” Could Katerina Ivanovna, with her intelligence and her painfully acute perception, really not have foreseen that people would talk like this? She certainly had foreseen it, and yet she had resolved to tell everything! Of course, all these grubby doubts about the story’s truth began only later; in the first moment everything and everyone was shaken. The members of the court listened to Katerina Ivanovna in reverent, one might even say abashed, silence. The prosecutor did not permit himself a single further question on the subject. Fetyukovich bowed deeply to her. Oh, he was almost triumphant! Much had been gained: a man giving away his last five thousand in a noble impulse, and then that same man murdering his father at night to rob him of three thousand—these were things somewhat difficult to reconcile. At least Fetyukovich might now be able to eliminate the robbery. A new kind of light suddenly flooded the “case.” A wave of sympathy for Mitya swept through the hall. And he… people said that once or twice during Katerina Ivanovna’s testimony he had started up from his seat, then fallen back onto the bench and covered his face with both hands. But when she finished, he suddenly stretched out his arms to her and exclaimed in a sobbing voice:

<!-- B12-C04-P037 -->
“Katya, why have you destroyed me!”

<!-- B12-C04-P038 -->
He began sobbing loudly for the whole hall to hear. But he mastered himself at once and shouted again:

<!-- B12-C04-P039 -->
“Now I’m condemned!”

<!-- B12-C04-P040 -->
Then he seemed to stiffen in his place, his teeth clenched and his arms tightly crossed over his chest. Katerina Ivanovna remained in the hall and sat down on the chair assigned to her. She was pale and sat with her eyes lowered. Those near her said that for a long time she trembled all over as though in a fever. Grushenka appeared to be examined.

<!-- B12-C04-P041 -->
I am nearing the catastrophe that, bursting out suddenly, may truly have destroyed Mitya. For I am certain, and everyone else agreed—all the lawyers said so afterward—that without this episode the criminal would at least have been granted mercy. But more of that in a moment. First, just a few words about Grushenka.

<!-- B12-C04-P042 -->
She too entered the hall dressed entirely in black, with her beautiful black shawl around her shoulders. Smoothly, with her noiseless step and the slight sway sometimes seen in full-figured women, she approached the railing, looking fixedly at the presiding judge and never once glancing right or left. To my mind she was very beautiful at that moment, and not at all pale, as the ladies afterward maintained. They also maintained that there was a peculiar intensity and malice in her expression. I think only that she was irritated and painfully conscious of the contemptuous, curious looks of our scandal-hungry audience. Hers was a proud nature that could not endure contempt, one of those that, at the mere suspicion of anyone’s contempt, immediately flare up in anger and a longing to strike back. There was timidity too, of course, and an inward shame at that timidity, so it is hardly surprising that her speech was uneven: now angry, now contemptuous and deliberately coarse, then suddenly sounding a sincere, heartfelt note of self-condemnation and self-accusation. Sometimes she spoke as though plunging into an abyss: “Whatever happens, it’s all the same; I’ll say it anyway…” Of her acquaintance with Fyodor Pavlovich she remarked sharply: “It’s all nonsense. Is it my fault he attached himself to me?” Then a minute later she added: “It’s all my fault. I made fun of both of them, the old man and this one, and drove them both to it. It all happened because of me.” Somehow the matter of Samsonov came up. “What business is it of anyone’s?” she immediately snapped with a kind of insolent defiance. “He was my benefactor. He took me in barefoot after my own family threw me out of the house.” The presiding judge reminded her, very politely, however, that she must answer the questions directly without going into unnecessary detail. Grushenka blushed, and her eyes flashed.

<!-- B12-C04-P043 -->
She had not seen the packet of money, but had only heard from “the villain” that Fyodor Pavlovich had some packet with three thousand inside. “But it was all foolishness. I was laughing, and I would never have gone there for anything…”

<!-- B12-C04-P044 -->
“Whom have you just referred to as ‘the villain’?” the prosecutor inquired.

<!-- B12-C04-P045 -->
“The servant, Smerdyakov, who killed his master and hanged himself yesterday.”

<!-- B12-C04-P046 -->
Naturally, she was asked at once what grounds she had for so categorical an accusation, but she too turned out to have none.

<!-- B12-C04-P047 -->
“Dmitri Fyodorovich told me himself; believe him. That woman who came between us destroyed him, that’s what. She alone is the cause of everything, that’s what,” Grushenka added, seeming to shudder all over with hatred, and a malicious note rang in her voice.

<!-- B12-C04-P048 -->
She was asked whom she was alluding to now.

<!-- B12-C04-P049 -->
“That young lady, Katerina Ivanovna over there. She invited me to her house that time, treated me to chocolate, wanted to lure me in. She hasn’t much real shame, that’s what…”

<!-- B12-C04-P050 -->
Here the presiding judge stopped her sternly, asking her to moderate her language. But the jealous woman’s heart was already aflame; she was ready to plunge into any abyss…

<!-- B12-C04-P051 -->
“At the arrest in the village of Mokroye,” the prosecutor asked, recalling the incident, “everyone saw and heard you run out of another room and cry: ‘It’s all my fault! We’ll go to penal servitude together!’ So at that moment you too were convinced he had murdered his father?”

<!-- B12-C04-P052 -->
“I don’t remember what I felt then,” Grushenka replied. “Everyone was shouting that he’d killed his father, and I felt that I was to blame and he’d killed him because of me. But when he said he was innocent, I believed him at once, and I believe him now, and always will. He isn’t the sort of man to lie.”

<!-- B12-C04-P053 -->
The questioning passed to Fetyukovich. Among other things, I remember, he asked about Rakitin and the twenty-five rubles “for bringing Alexei Fyodorovich Karamazov to you.”

<!-- B12-C04-P054 -->
“What’s so surprising about his taking money?” Grushenka said with a spiteful, contemptuous laugh. “He was always coming to pester me for money, sometimes wheedling thirty rubles a month out of me, mostly to amuse himself. He had enough to eat and drink without my money.”

<!-- B12-C04-P055 -->
“On what grounds were you so generous to Mr. Rakitin?” Fetyukovich seized on it, despite the presiding judge’s marked restlessness.

<!-- B12-C04-P056 -->
“Why, he’s my first cousin. My mother and his mother are sisters. Only he kept begging me not to tell anyone here; he was so terribly ashamed of me.”

<!-- B12-C04-P057 -->
This new fact came as a complete surprise to everyone. No one in the whole town had known it until now, not even at the monastery; even Mitya had not known. People said Rakitin turned crimson with shame in his chair. Before entering the courtroom, Grushenka had somehow learned that he had testified against Mitya, and that was why she was so angry. Mr. Rakitin’s entire earlier speech, all its nobility, all its attacks on serfdom and Russia’s civic disorder—this time all of it was utterly erased and destroyed in the public mind. Fetyukovich was pleased; once again God had dropped a gift into his lap. In general, Grushenka was not questioned for very long, nor could she, of course, contribute anything particularly new. She left the audience with a very unpleasant impression. Hundreds of contemptuous looks were directed at her when, having finished her testimony, she sat down in the hall at some distance from Katerina Ivanovna. Throughout her examination Mitya had remained silent, as though turned to stone, his eyes lowered to the ground.

Ivan Fyodorovich appeared as a witness.
