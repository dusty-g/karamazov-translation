# Book XI — Brother Ivan Fyodorovich
## VIII. The Third and Last Meeting with Smerdyakov

<!-- B11-C08-P001 -->
Before he was halfway there, a sharp, dry wind rose, like the wind early that morning, and fine, dry snow began to fall thickly. It fell on the ground without sticking; the wind whirled it about, and soon a full blizzard was blowing. In the part of our town where Smerdyakov lived there were hardly any streetlamps. Ivan Fyodorovich strode through the darkness, oblivious to the blizzard, finding his way by instinct. His head ached, his temples throbbed painfully. He could feel his hands cramping. A little short of Marya Kondratyevna's house, Ivan Fyodorovich suddenly met a solitary drunken peasant, a small fellow in a patched coat, zigzagging along, grumbling and swearing, then abruptly abandoning his curses to sing in a hoarse, drunken voice:

Oh, Vanka's gone to Petersburg,  
I won't wait for him!

<!-- B11-C08-P002 -->
But he kept breaking off at this second line and starting to abuse someone again, then suddenly launching into the same song. Ivan Fyodorovich had long felt a terrible hatred for him without yet thinking about him at all; suddenly he became conscious of him. At once an irresistible desire seized him to bring his fist down on the little peasant. At that very moment they drew level, and the peasant, swaying violently, lurched into Ivan with all his weight. Ivan shoved him furiously away. The peasant flew back and dropped like a log onto the frozen ground, uttered just one groan of pain—“Oh-oh!”—and fell silent. Ivan stepped toward him. He lay on his back, quite motionless and unconscious. “He'll freeze!” Ivan thought, and strode on toward Smerdyakov.

<!-- B11-C08-P003 -->
In the entrance passage Marya Kondratyevna, who had hurried out to open the door with a candle in her hand, whispered that Pavel Fyodorovich—that is, Smerdyakov—was very ill, sir; not that he was bedridden, but almost out of his mind, sir, and had even ordered the tea taken away, refusing to drink it.

<!-- B11-C08-P004 -->
“What, is he running wild?” Ivan Fyodorovich asked roughly.

<!-- B11-C08-P005 -->
“Oh no, quite the contrary, he's perfectly quiet, sir. Only don't talk to him too long...” Marya Kondratyevna pleaded.

<!-- B11-C08-P006 -->
Ivan Fyodorovich opened the door and stepped into the room.

<!-- B11-C08-P007 -->
It was heated as fiercely as before, but some changes were noticeable in the room: one of the side benches had been removed, replaced by a large old leather sofa finished to look like mahogany. A bed had been made up on it, with fairly clean white pillows. Smerdyakov sat on the bed in the same dressing gown. The table had been moved in front of the sofa, making the room very cramped. A thick book in a yellow cover lay on the table, but Smerdyakov was not reading it; apparently he had been sitting doing nothing. He greeted Ivan Fyodorovich with a long, silent look and seemed not at all surprised by his arrival. His face had changed greatly; he had grown very thin and yellow. His eyes were sunken, his lower eyelids blue.

<!-- B11-C08-P008 -->
“So you really are ill?” Ivan Fyodorovich stopped. “I won't keep you long; I won't even take off my coat. Where am I to sit?”

<!-- B11-C08-P009 -->
He went round the other end of the table, pulled up a chair, and sat down.

<!-- B11-C08-P010 -->
“Why are you staring without speaking? I've only one question, and I swear I won't leave you without an answer: has the lady, Katerina Ivanovna, been here?”

<!-- B11-C08-P011 -->
Smerdyakov remained silent a long time, still looking quietly at Ivan, then suddenly waved his hand and turned his face away.

<!-- B11-C08-P012 -->
“What is it?” Ivan exclaimed.

<!-- B11-C08-P013 -->
“Nothing.”

<!-- B11-C08-P014 -->
“What do you mean, nothing?”

<!-- B11-C08-P015 -->
“Well, she came, and it's all the same to you. Leave me alone, sir.”

<!-- B11-C08-P016 -->
“No, I won't! Tell me when she came.”

<!-- B11-C08-P017 -->
“I've forgotten she ever existed,” Smerdyakov said with a contemptuous smile, and suddenly, turning back to Ivan, fixed him with a look of frenzied hatred, the very look he had given him at their meeting a month before.

<!-- B11-C08-P018 -->
“You seem ill yourself. Look how drawn you are—you haven't a face left,” he said to Ivan.

<!-- B11-C08-P019 -->
“Leave my health alone and answer what you're asked.”

<!-- B11-C08-P020 -->
“Why have your eyes gone yellow? The whites are quite yellow. Are you suffering badly?”

<!-- B11-C08-P021 -->
He smiled contemptuously and suddenly broke into outright laughter.

<!-- B11-C08-P022 -->
“Listen, I told you I won't leave without an answer!” Ivan shouted in terrible irritation.

<!-- B11-C08-P023 -->
“Why are you pestering me, sir? Why torment me?” Smerdyakov said in anguish.

<!-- B11-C08-P024 -->
“Oh, damn it! I don't care about you. Answer the question and I'll leave at once.”

<!-- B11-C08-P025 -->
“I have nothing to answer you!” Smerdyakov lowered his eyes again.

<!-- B11-C08-P026 -->
“I assure you I'll make you answer!”

<!-- B11-C08-P027 -->
“What are you so worried about?” Smerdyakov suddenly stared at him, now not so much with contempt as with something almost like disgust. “Is it because the trial begins tomorrow? Nothing will happen to you—believe it at last! Go home, go quietly to bed, and don't be afraid of anything.”

<!-- B11-C08-P028 -->
“I don't understand you... What have I to fear tomorrow?” Ivan said in surprise, and suddenly a real chill of some strange fear passed over his soul. Smerdyakov looked him up and down.

<!-- B11-C08-P029 -->
“You don't un-der-stand?” he drawled reproachfully. “For an intelligent man to want to put on such a performance!”

<!-- B11-C08-P030 -->
Ivan looked at him in silence. The unexpected tone alone, a tone of quite unprecedented arrogance in which his former servant now addressed him, was extraordinary. There had been nothing quite like it even last time.

<!-- B11-C08-P031 -->
“I tell you, you've nothing to fear. I won't testify against you. There's no evidence. Look at your hands shaking. Why are your fingers twitching? Go home. You didn't kill him.”

<!-- B11-C08-P032 -->
Ivan shuddered; he remembered Alyosha.

<!-- B11-C08-P033 -->
“I know it wasn't me...” he began falteringly.

<!-- B11-C08-P034 -->
“You kno-ow?” Smerdyakov picked him up again.

<!-- B11-C08-P035 -->
Ivan jumped up and seized him by the shoulder.

<!-- B11-C08-P036 -->
“Tell me everything, you reptile! Everything!”

<!-- B11-C08-P037 -->
Smerdyakov was not frightened in the least. He merely fastened his eyes on him with mad hatred.

<!-- B11-C08-P038 -->
“Well then, it was you who killed him,” he whispered furiously.

<!-- B11-C08-P039 -->
Ivan sank onto his chair, as though he had thought something through. He gave a malicious smile.

<!-- B11-C08-P040 -->
“Is it still the same old thing? What you said last time?”

<!-- B11-C08-P041 -->
“Last time too, you stood in front of me and understood everything. You understand now as well.”

<!-- B11-C08-P042 -->
“All I understand is that you're mad.”

<!-- B11-C08-P043 -->
“Don't you ever get tired of it! Here we sit face to face—what need is there to fool each other and play a comedy? Or do you still want to put it all on me alone, to my very face? You killed him. You're the chief murderer, and I was only your accomplice, your faithful servant Licharda, and I did this deed at your word.”

<!-- B11-C08-P044 -->
“Did it? You mean you killed him?” Ivan went cold.

<!-- B11-C08-P045 -->
Something seemed to shake in his brain, and a fine, cold trembling ran all through him. Now Smerdyakov himself looked at him in surprise: probably the sincerity of Ivan's fear had struck him at last.

<!-- B11-C08-P046 -->
“Can you really have known nothing?” he faltered distrustfully, smiling crookedly into his face.

<!-- B11-C08-P047 -->
Ivan kept staring at him; he seemed to have lost the power of speech.

Oh, Vanka's gone to Petersburg,  
I won't wait for him!

suddenly rang through his head.

<!-- B11-C08-P048 -->
“Do you know what? I'm afraid you're a dream—that it's a ghost sitting here in front of me,” he faltered.

<!-- B11-C08-P049 -->
“There are no ghosts here, sir, only the two of us, and a certain third. Without a doubt he's here now, that third one, between us two.”

<!-- B11-C08-P050 -->
“Who? Who's here? Who's the third?” Ivan Fyodorovich said in fright, looking around and hurriedly searching every corner with his eyes.

<!-- B11-C08-P051 -->
“That third one is God, sir, Providence itself. It's here beside us now, sir. Only don't look for it; you won't find it.”

<!-- B11-C08-P052 -->
“You lied when you said you killed him!” Ivan yelled furiously. “You're either mad or taunting me, just as you did last time!”

<!-- B11-C08-P053 -->
As before, Smerdyakov, entirely unafraid, watched him searchingly. He still could not overcome his disbelief; he still thought Ivan “knew everything” and was merely pretending in order to “put it all on him alone, to his very face.”

<!-- B11-C08-P054 -->
“Wait, sir,” he said at last in a weak voice, and suddenly drew his left foot out from under the table and began rolling up his trouser leg. His leg was covered by a long white stocking, his foot in a slipper. Unhurriedly, Smerdyakov removed the garter and thrust his fingers deep inside the stocking. Ivan Fyodorovich watched him and suddenly began shaking with convulsive terror.

<!-- B11-C08-P055 -->
“Madman!” he yelled, leaping up and recoiling so quickly that his back struck the wall, where he seemed to stick, stretched rigidly to his full height. He stared at Smerdyakov in mad horror. The latter, quite unperturbed by his fright, kept rummaging in the stocking, apparently struggling to grasp something with his fingers and pull it out. At last he caught hold of it and began pulling. Ivan Fyodorovich saw that it was some papers, or a packet of papers. Smerdyakov drew it out and laid it on the table.

<!-- B11-C08-P056 -->
“There, sir!” he said quietly.

<!-- B11-C08-P057 -->
“What?” Ivan answered, shaking.

<!-- B11-C08-P058 -->
“Have a look, sir,” Smerdyakov said just as quietly.

<!-- B11-C08-P059 -->
Ivan stepped up to the table, took hold of the packet, and began unwrapping it, but suddenly snatched his fingers away as though he had touched some loathsome, terrifying reptile.

<!-- B11-C08-P060 -->
“Your fingers keep trembling, sir, in a spasm,” Smerdyakov observed, and slowly unwrapped the paper himself. Inside were three bundles of rainbow-colored hundred-ruble banknotes.

<!-- B11-C08-P061 -->
“It's all here, sir, all three thousand. You needn't even count it. Take it, sir,” he invited Ivan, nodding at the money. Ivan sank onto the chair. He was as pale as a handkerchief.

<!-- B11-C08-P062 -->
“You frightened me... with that stocking...” he said with a strange smirk.

<!-- B11-C08-P063 -->
“Did you really, really not know until now?” Smerdyakov asked once more.

<!-- B11-C08-P064 -->
“No, I didn't know. I thought it was Dmitri all along. Brother! Brother! Ah!” He suddenly clutched his head in both hands. “Listen: did you kill him alone? Without my brother, or with him?”

<!-- B11-C08-P065 -->
“Only together with you, sir. I killed him together with you; Dmitri Fyodorovich is entirely innocent, sir.”

<!-- B11-C08-P066 -->
“Very well, very well... About me later. Why do I keep shaking... I can't get a word out.”

<!-- B11-C08-P067 -->
“You were so bold then, sir, saying ‘everything is permitted,’ and now you're so frightened!” Smerdyakov murmured in wonder. “Would you like some lemonade? I'll order it at once. It can be very refreshing. Only we ought to cover this up first.”

<!-- B11-C08-P068 -->
Again he nodded toward the bundles. He was about to get up and call through the door to Marya Kondratyevna to make and bring some lemonade, but, looking for something to cover the money so that she would not see it, he first took out his handkerchief. As this again proved thoroughly soiled with mucus, he took the only book on the table, the thick yellow one Ivan had noticed on entering, and weighted the money down with it. The title of the book was *The Discourses of Our Holy Father Isaac the Syrian*. Ivan Fyodorovich managed to read the title mechanically.

<!-- B11-C08-P069 -->
“I don't want lemonade,” he said. “About me later. Sit down and tell me: how did you do it? Tell everything...”

<!-- B11-C08-P070 -->
“You might at least take your coat off, sir, or you'll be soaked in sweat.”

<!-- B11-C08-P071 -->
As though it had only now occurred to him, Ivan Fyodorovich tore off his coat and, without leaving his chair, flung it onto the bench.

<!-- B11-C08-P072 -->
“Speak, please, speak!”

<!-- B11-C08-P073 -->
He seemed to have quieted down. He waited confidently for Smerdyakov to tell everything now.

<!-- B11-C08-P074 -->
“How it was done, sir?” Smerdyakov sighed. “It was done in the most natural way, following those very words of yours...”

<!-- B11-C08-P075 -->
“My words later,” Ivan interrupted again, but without shouting as before, pronouncing his words firmly and seemingly quite in control of himself. “Just tell me in detail how you did it. Everything in order. Don't forget anything. Details—the details above all. Please.”

“You left, and then I fell into the cellar, sir...”

“In a fit, or were you pretending?”

“Wait! And the whole time afterward, in the hospital too, you were pretending?”

“Very well, very well. Go on.”

<!-- B11-C08-P082 -->

<!-- B11-C08-P083 -->
“Waiting for him to come to you?”

<!-- B11-C08-P084 -->
“Why to me? I was waiting for him to come to the house, because I no longer had any doubt he'd come that very night. Without me and without any information, he was bound to have to get into the house himself, over the fence, as he knew how, sir, and do something or other.”

“And if he hadn't come?”

“Then nothing would have happened, sir. I wouldn't have dared without him.”

“Very well, very well... Speak more clearly, take your time; above all, leave nothing out!”

“Until tomorrow!” Ivan cried again, and left the room. The blizzard was still blowing. He walked the first few steps briskly, but suddenly seemed to begin staggering. “It's something physical,” he thought with a smile. Something like joy had now descended into his soul. He felt an infinite firmness within him: an end to the vacillation that had tormented him so horribly of late! His decision was made, “and it won't change now,” he thought happily. At that moment he suddenly stumbled over something and nearly fell. Stopping, he made out at his feet the peasant he had knocked down, still lying in the very same place, unconscious and motionless. The blizzard had already covered almost his whole face with snow. Ivan suddenly seized him and began dragging him, supporting his weight. Seeing a light in a little house on the right, he went up, knocked on the shutters, and asked the townsman who answered, the owner of the house to help him carry the peasant to the police station, promising him three rubles on the spot for doing so. The townsman got ready and came out. I shall not describe in detail how Ivan Fyodorovich managed to accomplish his purpose and get the peasant taken in at the station, with instructions that a doctor examine him immediately; here too he gave money generously “for expenses.” I shall only say that the business took almost a full hour. But Ivan Fyodorovich was very pleased. His thoughts ranged and worked busily. “If I hadn't taken such a firm decision for tomorrow,” he suddenly thought with pleasure, “I wouldn't have stopped for a whole hour to find help for the peasant. I'd have walked past him and spat at the thought of his freezing... Yet how well I can observe myself,” he thought at that same instant with still greater pleasure, “and they've decided over there that I'm going mad!” When he reached his house, he suddenly stopped, struck by a question: “Shouldn't I go to the prosecutor right now, this very moment, and tell everything?” He settled the question by turning toward the house again. “Tomorrow, all together!” he whispered to himself, and, strangely, almost all his joy and self-satisfaction vanished in an instant.
