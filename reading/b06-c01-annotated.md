# Book VI: The Russian Monk
## I. The Elder Zosima and His Guests

<!-- B06-C01-P001 -->
When Alyosha entered the elder's cell, anxious and heartsick, he stopped in something close to amazement. Instead of the dying man, perhaps already unconscious, whom he had feared he would find, he suddenly saw him sitting in his armchair, his face wasted by weakness but cheerful and alert, surrounded by guests and talking with them quietly and joyfully. He had, however, risen from his bed no more than a quarter of an hour before Alyosha arrived. The guests had gathered in his cell earlier and waited for him to wake, firmly assured by Father Paisius that “our teacher will certainly rise to speak once more with those dear to his heart, as he himself declared and promised this morning.” Father Paisius believed so firmly in this promise, and indeed in every word of the dying elder, that even if he had seen him wholly unconscious, even no longer breathing, but had his promise that he would rise once more and take leave of him, he might not have believed in death itself, still expecting the dying man to come to and fulfill his promise. That morning, as he went to sleep, the elder Zosima had told him quite definitely: “I will not die before I have drunk my fill of conversation with you once more, beloved of my heart, looked upon your dear faces and poured out my soul to you again.” Those who had gathered for what was probably the elder's last conversation were his most devoted friends of many years. There were four of them: the priest-monks Father Joseph and Father Paisius; the priest-monk Father Mikhail, superior of the hermitage, a man not yet very old, far less learned, of humble origins but firm in spirit, whose faith was simple and unshakable, stern in appearance but with a heart full of tender feeling, which he plainly concealed almost as though ashamed of it. The fourth guest was a very old, simple little monk of the poorest peasant class, Brother Anfim, who may even have been barely literate, silent and quiet, seldom speaking to anyone, the humblest of the humble, with the look of a man forever frightened by something great and terrible beyond the grasp of his mind. The elder Zosima loved this seemingly trembling man very much and had treated him with extraordinary respect all his life, though perhaps he had exchanged fewer words with him than with anyone else in all that time, despite the many years they had once spent wandering together across holy Russia. That was very long ago, some forty years before, when the elder Zosima had first begun his monastic labors in a poor, little-known monastery in the Kostroma region and soon afterward joined Father Anfim on his travels to collect donations for their poor little monastery there. They all, host and guests, sat in the elder's second room, where his bed stood—a very cramped room, as I have mentioned before, so that the four of them barely found space around the elder's armchair on chairs brought in from the first room; Porfiry the novice remained standing. It was already growing dark, and the room was lit by the lamps and wax candles before the icons. When the elder saw Alyosha, who had faltered on entering and stopped in the doorway, he smiled joyfully and held out his hand.

“Welcome, quiet one, welcome, my dear. Here you are. I knew you would come.”

<!-- B06-C01-P003 -->
Alyosha went up to him, bowed to the ground before him and began to cry. Something was struggling to burst from his heart; his soul trembled, and he wanted to sob.

<!-- B06-C01-P004 -->
“Why, wait a little before you mourn me,” the elder said with a smile, laying his right hand on Alyosha's head. “You see, I'm sitting here talking. Perhaps I'll live another twenty years, as that good, dear woman from Vyshegorye wished me yesterday, the one with the little girl Lizaveta in her arms. Remember, O Lord, both the mother and the little girl Lizaveta!” (He crossed himself.) “Porfiry, did you take her gift where I told you?”

<!-- B06-C01-P005 -->
He had remembered the sixty kopecks donated yesterday by the cheerful pilgrim to be given to “someone poorer than I am.” Such offerings are made as penances, voluntarily undertaken for one reason or another, and must come from money earned by the giver's own labor. The elder had sent Porfiry that very evening to a townswoman, a widow with children who had recently lost her home in a fire and had since taken to begging. Porfiry hastened to report that it was done and that, as instructed, he had given it “from an unknown benefactress.”

<!-- B06-C01-P006 -->
“Get up, my dear,” the elder went on to Alyosha. “Let me look at you. Have you been to see your family, and have you seen your brother?”

<!-- B06-C01-P007 -->
Alyosha thought it strange that he should ask so firmly and specifically about just one of his brothers—but which one? Perhaps, then, it was for this brother's sake that he had sent him away both yesterday and today.

<!-- B06-C01-P008 -->
“I've seen one of my brothers,” Alyosha replied.

<!-- B06-C01-P009 -->
“I mean the one who was here yesterday, the eldest, the one I bowed to the ground before.”

<!-- B06-C01-P010 -->
“I only saw him yesterday. Today I couldn't find him anywhere,” said Alyosha.

<!-- B06-C01-P011 -->
“Hurry and find him. Go again tomorrow, and hurry. Leave everything else and hurry. Perhaps you still have time to prevent something terrible. Yesterday I bowed before the great suffering that lies ahead of him.”

<!-- B06-C01-P012 -->
He suddenly fell silent and seemed to sink into thought. These were strange words. Father Joseph, who had witnessed the elder's bow to the ground the day before, exchanged a glance with Father Paisius. Alyosha could not contain himself.

<!-- B06-C01-P013 -->
“Father and teacher,” he said in great agitation, “your words are so unclear... What suffering awaits him?”

<!-- B06-C01-P014 -->
“Do not pry. I seemed to see something terrible yesterday... as though his whole fate were expressed in his eyes. There was one look he gave... and in an instant my heart recoiled in horror at what this man is preparing for himself. Once or twice in my life I have seen the same expression on other faces... as though it portrayed those people's whole fate, and that fate, alas, came to pass. I sent you to him, Alexei, because I thought the sight of your brotherly face would help him. But everything comes from the Lord, and all our destinies. ‘Unless a grain of wheat falls into the earth and dies, it remains alone; but if it dies, it bears much fruit.’ Remember that. And you, Alexei—many times in my life I have blessed you in my thoughts for that face of yours. Know that,” the elder said with a quiet smile. “This is what I think of you: you will leave these walls, yet in the world you will remain as a monk. You will have many adversaries, but even your enemies will love you. Life will bring you many misfortunes, yet through them you will be happy, and you will bless life and make others bless it—which matters most of all. There, that is what you are like. My fathers and teachers,” he turned to his guests with a tender smile, “until today I have never said, even to him, why this young man's face has been so dear to my soul. Only now will I say it: his face was to me something like a reminder and a prophecy. At the dawn of my days, when I was still a little child, I had an older brother who died before my eyes while still a youth, only seventeen years old. And later, as I went through life, I gradually became convinced that this brother was, as it were, a sign and a calling from above in my life; for had he not appeared in my life, had he never existed at all, perhaps, I think, I would never have become a monk or set out upon this precious path. That first appearance came in my childhood, and now, as my journey draws to its close, something like a repetition of it has appeared before my eyes. It is strange, fathers and teachers: although Alexei did not look very much like him, only a little, he seemed so like him in spirit that I often took him almost for that very youth, my brother, mysteriously come to me at the end of my journey to bring some remembrance and understanding, so that I even marveled at myself and this strange dream of mine. Do you hear this, Porfiry?” he said, turning to the novice who attended him. “I have often seen what looked like hurt on your face because I love Alexei more than you. Now you know why it was so, but I love you too, know that, and have often grieved to see you hurt. And to you, my dear guests, I want to tell of this youth, my brother, for nothing in my life has been more precious, more prophetic or more moving than his appearance in it. My heart has softened, and at this moment I behold my whole life as though I were living it all over again...”

Here I must mention that this last conversation of the elder's with the guests who visited him on the last day of his life has in part been preserved in writing. Alexei Fyodorovich Karamazov wrote it down from memory some time after the elder's death. Whether it was entirely the conversation of that day, or whether he added passages from earlier conversations with his teacher, I can no longer determine. Besides, in this account the elder speaks almost without interruption, as though relating the story of his life to his friends, whereas from what was told afterward there is no doubt that things actually happened somewhat differently. The conversation that evening was a general one, and although the guests seldom interrupted their host, they too spoke and joined in, perhaps even contributing accounts and stories of their own. Nor could the narrative have been so continuous, since the elder sometimes became breathless, lost his voice and even lay down on his bed to rest, though he did not fall asleep and his guests remained in their places. Once or twice the conversation was interrupted by a reading from the Gospel; Father Paisius read. It is remarkable, too, that none of them expected him to die that very night, particularly since, on this last evening of his life, after his deep sleep during the day, he seemed suddenly to have found new strength that sustained him throughout this long conversation with his friends. It was like a last surge of tender feeling, sustaining in him an extraordinary animation, but only for a little while, for his life broke off suddenly... But more of that later. For now I wish to explain that, instead of giving every detail of the conversation, I have chosen to confine myself to the elder's story as it appears in Alexei Fyodorovich Karamazov's manuscript. It will be shorter that way, and less tiring, though of course, I repeat, Alyosha took a good deal from earlier conversations as well and brought it all together.
