# Book IX: The Preliminary Investigation
## IV. The Second Ordeal

<!-- B09-C04-P001 -->
“You wouldn't believe how much encouragement your willingness gives us, Dmitri Fyodorovich...” Nikolai Parfenovich began animatedly, with visible pleasure shining in his large, protuberant light-gray eyes, which were, however, very nearsighted; a minute earlier he had taken off his glasses. “And your remark just now about our mutual trust was quite right. Without it, in matters of such importance, it is sometimes impossible to proceed—that is, where the suspected person truly wishes, hopes, and is able to clear himself. For our part, we shall do everything in our power, and even now you have had an opportunity to see how we are conducting this case... Do you agree, Ippolit Kirillovich?” he suddenly asked the prosecutor.

<!-- B09-C04-P002 -->
“Oh, certainly,” the prosecutor assented, though somewhat dryly compared with Nikolai Parfenovich's enthusiasm.

<!-- B09-C04-P003 -->
Let me remark here once and for all: from the very beginning of his career among us, the newly arrived Nikolai Parfenovich had felt an extraordinary respect for our prosecutor, Ippolit Kirillovich, and had almost come to love him. He was practically the only person who believed unreservedly in the exceptional psychological and oratorical talents of our Ippolit Kirillovich, so “unfairly treated in the service,” and fully believed, too, that his treatment had been unfair. He had heard of him back in Petersburg. In return, the youthful Nikolai Parfenovich proved to be the only person in the entire world whom our “unfairly treated” prosecutor sincerely loved. On their way here they had managed to discuss and agree on certain points concerning the coming case, and now, at the table, Nikolai Parfenovich's sharp mind caught on the wing and understood every hint, every movement in his senior colleague's face, from half a word, a glance, a wink.

<!-- B09-C04-P004 -->
“Gentlemen, just let me tell it myself and don't interrupt me over trifles, and I'll lay it all before you in a moment,” Mitya said heatedly.

<!-- B09-C04-P005 -->
“Excellent, sir. Thank you. But before we proceed to hear your account, perhaps you would allow me to establish one more small fact of considerable interest to us: namely, the ten rubles you borrowed yesterday, at about five o'clock, from your acquaintance Pyotr Ilyich Perkhotin, giving your pistols as security.”

<!-- B09-C04-P006 -->
“I pawned them, gentlemen, pawned them for ten rubles, and what of it? That's all there is to it. As soon as I got back to town from my journey, I pawned them.”

<!-- B09-C04-P007 -->
“You had returned from a journey? You had been out of town?”

<!-- B09-C04-P008 -->
“Yes, gentlemen, forty versts out of town. You didn't know?”

<!-- B09-C04-P009 -->
The prosecutor and Nikolai Parfenovich exchanged glances.

<!-- B09-C04-P010 -->
“Perhaps, then, you might begin your account with a systematic description of your entire day yesterday, starting in the morning? Allow us to learn, for example, why you left town, and precisely when you went and returned... and all those facts...”

<!-- B09-C04-P011 -->
“Then you should have asked that at the beginning,” Mitya laughed loudly. “And if you like, we need to begin not with yesterday but with the day before yesterday, first thing in the morning; then you'll understand where I went, on foot and by carriage, and how and why. The morning before yesterday, gentlemen, I went to that big merchant here, Samsonov, to borrow three thousand from him on the surest possible security—I suddenly had to have it, gentlemen, suddenly had to...”

<!-- B09-C04-P012 -->
“Allow me to interrupt,” the prosecutor broke in politely. “Why did you so suddenly need precisely that sum, that is, three thousand rubles?”

<!-- B09-C04-P013 -->
“Oh, gentlemen, let's leave out the details: how, when, and why, and why this amount and not that, and all that fiddle-faddle... You couldn't fit it into three volumes that way, and you'd need an epilogue besides!”

<!-- B09-C04-P014 -->
Mitya said all this with the good-natured but impatient familiarity of a man who wishes to tell the whole truth and is filled with the best intentions.

<!-- B09-C04-P015 -->
“Gentlemen,” he said, as though suddenly recollecting himself, “don't hold my kicking up against me. I ask you again: believe once more that I feel the fullest respect and understand the present state of affairs. Don't suppose I'm drunk, either. I've sobered up by now. And being drunk wouldn't interfere at all. With me it's like this:

> Sobered up, grew clever—and became a fool;  
> Got drunk, grew foolish—and became wise.

Ha-ha! Still, I see, gentlemen, that it isn't proper for me to make jokes before you yet, until we've cleared things up. Allow me to preserve my own dignity too. I understand the difference between us at present: after all, I'm sitting before you as a criminal, which puts me a very long way below you, while you have been charged with keeping watch on me. You aren't going to pat me on the head for Grigory; one can't go around cracking old men's skulls with impunity. You'll have me put away for him by order of the court, for six months, say, or a year in a house of correction—I don't know how you sentence people—though without deprivation of rights. It will be without deprivation of rights, won't it, Mr. Prosecutor? So there you are, gentlemen, I do understand the distinction... But you must admit, too, that you could confuse the Lord God himself with such questions: where did you step, how did you step, when did you step, and what did you step in? I'll get muddled that way, and you'll seize on every last scrap and write it down, and what will come of it? Nothing will come of it! Anyway, now that I've begun lying, I'll finish, and you, gentlemen, as men of higher education and the greatest nobility of spirit, must forgive me. I shall end with a request, to be precise: gentlemen, unlearn this official style of interrogation. You know, first you start with something paltry, insignificant: how did you get up, what did you eat, how did you spit, and once you've ‘lulled the criminal's vigilance,’ you suddenly spring a crushing question on him: ‘Whom did you murder? Whom did you rob?’ Ha-ha! That's your official method, that's your rule, that's the foundation of all your cunning! Well, lull peasants with tricks like that, not me. I understand how things work; I've served myself, ha-ha-ha! Don't be angry, gentlemen. Will you forgive my insolence?” he cried, looking at them with what was almost astonishing good nature. “After all, it was Mitka Karamazov who said it, so you can forgive him, because you couldn't forgive a clever man, but you can forgive Mitka! Ha-ha!”

<!-- B09-C04-P016 -->
Nikolai Parfenovich listened and laughed too. The prosecutor did not laugh, but watched Mitya keenly, never taking his eyes off him, as though unwilling to miss the smallest word, the smallest movement, the slightest tremor in the slightest feature of his face.

<!-- B09-C04-P017 -->
“But that is how we began with you,” Nikolai Parfenovich answered, still laughing. “We didn't confuse you with questions about how you got up in the morning or what you ate, but began with something rather too substantial.”

<!-- B09-C04-P018 -->
“I understand, I understood and appreciated it, and I appreciate still more your present kindness to me, which is beyond compare and worthy of the noblest souls. Three honorable men have come together here, and let everything between us rest on the mutual trust of educated men of the world, bound by their noble birth and their honor. In any event, allow me to regard you as my best friends at this moment of my life, at this moment when my honor is laid low! That doesn't offend you, gentlemen, does it?”

<!-- B09-C04-P019 -->
“On the contrary, you have expressed all that so beautifully, Dmitri Fyodorovich,” Nikolai Parfenovich agreed with grave approval.

<!-- B09-C04-P020 -->
“And away with the details, gentlemen, all these quibbling details!” Mitya exclaimed in delight. “Otherwise we'll simply end up with the devil knows what, won't we?”

<!-- B09-C04-P021 -->
“I shall gladly follow your sensible advice,” the prosecutor suddenly interposed, addressing Mitya, “but I shall not withdraw my question. It is absolutely essential for us to know exactly why you needed that sum—precisely three thousand.”

<!-- B09-C04-P022 -->
“Why I needed it? Well, for this and that... well, to pay a debt.”

<!-- B09-C04-P023 -->
“To whom, precisely?”

<!-- B09-C04-P024 -->
“I absolutely refuse to say, gentlemen! You see, it's not because I can't say, or don't dare, or am afraid, because all that is nothing, sheer nonsense; I won't say because there's a principle involved: this is my private life, and I won't allow any intrusion into my private life. That's my principle. Your question has no bearing on the case, and everything that has no bearing on the case is my private life! I wanted to pay a debt, a debt of honor, and I won't tell you to whom.”

<!-- B09-C04-P025 -->
“Allow us to record that,” the prosecutor said.

<!-- B09-C04-P026 -->
“Please do. Write it down just like that: I won't say, and I won't say. Write down, gentlemen, that I even consider it dishonorable to say. What a lot of time you have for writing things down!”

<!-- B09-C04-P027 -->
“Allow me, sir, to caution you and remind you once again, in case you did not know,” the prosecutor said with particular and very stern emphasis, “that you have every right not to answer the questions being put to you now, and that we, conversely, have no right whatever to force answers from you if you choose not to answer for one reason or another. That is a matter for your own judgment. But our duty, in a case such as the present one, is also to draw your attention to, and explain, the full extent of the harm you do yourself by refusing to give this or that piece of evidence. Now, please continue.”

<!-- B09-C04-P028 -->
“Gentlemen, I'm not angry... I...” Mitya began to mutter, somewhat embarrassed by the admonition. “You see, gentlemen, this same Samsonov, whom I went to then...”

<!-- B09-C04-P029 -->
We shall not, of course, give his detailed account of what the reader already knows. He was impatient to tell everything down to the smallest detail and at the same time to finish as quickly as possible. But his testimony was recorded as he gave it, which meant that he necessarily had to be stopped. Dmitri Fyodorovich disapproved of this but submitted, grew angry, but so far still good-naturedly. True, he occasionally cried out, “Gentlemen, this would drive the Lord God himself mad,” or, “Gentlemen, do you realize you're only needlessly provoking me?” But even as he exclaimed this, he had not yet abandoned his expansive friendliness. In this way he told them how Samsonov had “made a fool of him” the day before yesterday. (He now fully suspected that he had been made a fool of then.) The sale of his watch for six rubles to raise money for the journey, which the investigator and prosecutor knew nothing about, immediately aroused their most intense interest, and, to Mitya's boundless indignation, they thought it necessary to record the fact in detail as a second confirmation that even on the previous day he had had scarcely a penny. Little by little Mitya began to grow gloomy. Then, after describing his journey to Pointer and the night spent in the hut full of stove fumes, and so on, he brought his story to his return to town, and here, without being specifically asked, began to describe his jealous torments over Grushenka in detail. They listened silently and attentively, taking particular note of the fact that he had long maintained an observation post to watch for Grushenka at Marya Kondratyevna's house, “behind” Fyodor Pavlovich's, and that Smerdyakov had brought him information: they paid close attention to this and wrote it down. He spoke heatedly and at length of his jealousy, and though inwardly ashamed to expose his most intimate feelings to “public disgrace,” so to speak, he was visibly overcoming his shame in order to tell the truth. The impassive severity of the investigator's gaze, and especially the prosecutor's, fixed on him throughout his account, finally disconcerted him considerably. “This boy Nikolai Parfenovich, with whom only a few days ago I was talking nonsense about women, and this sickly prosecutor aren't worth my telling them all this,” flashed sadly through his mind. “Disgraceful! ‘Endure, be humble, and be silent,’” he concluded his thought with a line of verse, but once again braced himself to go on. When he came to his account of Madame Khokhlakov, he even grew cheerful again and was about to tell a particular recent little anecdote about that lady, unrelated to the case, but the investigator stopped him and politely asked him to move on “to matters of greater substance.” Finally, after he had described his despair and told of the moment when, leaving Madame Khokhlakov's, he had even thought of “quickly cutting someone's throat and getting three thousand,” they stopped him again and wrote down that he “had intended to cut someone's throat.” Mitya let them write without a word. At last the story reached the point when he suddenly learned that Grushenka had deceived him and had left Samsonov's immediately after he had brought her there, although she had told him she would stay with the old man until midnight. “If I didn't kill that Fenya then, gentlemen, it was only because I had no time,” he suddenly blurted out at this point in his account. This, too, was carefully recorded. Mitya waited gloomily and had begun to tell how he had run to his father's garden when the investigator suddenly stopped him, opened the large portfolio lying beside him on the sofa, and took out the brass pestle.

<!-- B09-C04-P030 -->
“Do you recognize this object?” he asked, showing it to Mitya.

<!-- B09-C04-P031 -->
“Ah, yes!” He gave a gloomy smile. “Of course I recognize it! Let me see... Oh, the devil, never mind!”

<!-- B09-C04-P032 -->
“You forgot to mention it,” the investigator observed.

<!-- B09-C04-P033 -->
“Oh, the devil! I wouldn't have hidden it from you. We could hardly have got through the story without it, could we? It simply slipped my mind.”

<!-- B09-C04-P034 -->
“Then be so good as to explain in detail how you came to arm yourself with it.”

<!-- B09-C04-P035 -->
“Certainly, gentlemen, I'll be so good.”

<!-- B09-C04-P036 -->
And Mitya told them how he had taken the pestle and run.

<!-- B09-C04-P037 -->
“But what purpose did you have in view when you armed yourself with such an implement?”

<!-- B09-C04-P038 -->
“What purpose? No purpose! I grabbed it and ran.”

<!-- B09-C04-P039 -->
“Why, then, if you had no purpose?”

<!-- B09-C04-P040 -->
Mitya was seething with irritation. He looked hard at the “boy” and gave a dark, malicious smile. The fact was that he felt more and more ashamed of having just told “such people” the story of his jealousy so sincerely, with such an outpouring of feeling.

<!-- B09-C04-P041 -->
“To hell with the pestle!” burst out of him.

<!-- B09-C04-P042 -->
“But still, sir.”

<!-- B09-C04-P043 -->
“Well, I grabbed it to ward off dogs. Well, it was dark... Well, just in case.”

<!-- B09-C04-P044 -->
“And on previous occasions, when leaving the house at night, did you also take some weapon with you, since you were so afraid of the dark?”

<!-- B09-C04-P045 -->
“Oh, the devil, pah! Gentlemen, it's literally impossible to talk to you!” Mitya cried, exasperated beyond endurance; and turning to the clerk, his face flushed with anger, he spoke rapidly, with a frenzied note in his voice:

<!-- B09-C04-P046 -->
“Write it down this instant... this instant... ‘that I took the pestle with me in order to run and kill my father... Fyodor Pavlovich... with a blow to the head!’ Well, are you satisfied now, gentlemen? Does that make you feel better?” he said, staring defiantly at the investigator and prosecutor.

<!-- B09-C04-P047 -->
“We understand only too well that you have just made such a statement out of irritation with us and annoyance at the questions we put to you, which you consider trivial but which are, in fact, extremely significant,” the prosecutor replied dryly.

<!-- B09-C04-P048 -->
“But for pity's sake, gentlemen! Well, I took the pestle... Well, why does one pick something up in such circumstances? I don't know why. I grabbed it and ran. That's all. This is shameful, gentlemen, *passons*[^b09-c04-n003], or I swear I'll stop telling you anything!”

<!-- B09-C04-P049 -->
He leaned his elbow on the table and propped his head on his hand. Sitting sideways to them, he stared at the wall, struggling against his bitter feelings. He did, in fact, desperately want to get up and declare that he would not say another word, “even if you take me off to be executed.”

<!-- B09-C04-P050 -->
“You see, gentlemen,” he said suddenly, overcoming himself with difficulty, “you see. I listen to you, and I fancy... you see, I sometimes have this dream... a certain dream, and I often dream it, it keeps coming back: someone is chasing me, someone I'm terribly afraid of is chasing me in the dark, at night, looking for me, and I hide from him somewhere, behind a door or a wardrobe, I hide in humiliation, and the main thing is that he knows perfectly well where I've hidden from him, but he seems to pretend on purpose that he doesn't know where I'm sitting, to torment me longer, to enjoy my terror... That's what you're doing now! That's what it's like!”

<!-- B09-C04-P051 -->
“You have dreams like that?” the prosecutor inquired.

<!-- B09-C04-P052 -->
“Yes, I have dreams like that... Would you like to write that down too?” Mitya smiled crookedly.

<!-- B09-C04-P053 -->
“No, sir, not write it down, but your dreams are curious all the same.”

<!-- B09-C04-P054 -->
“This is no dream now! Realism, gentlemen, the realism of actual life! I'm the wolf and you're the hunters, so hunt your wolf down.”

<!-- B09-C04-P055 -->
“You are wrong to choose such a comparison...” Nikolai Parfenovich began with extraordinary gentleness.

<!-- B09-C04-P056 -->
“Not wrong, gentlemen, not wrong!” Mitya flared up again, though, having evidently relieved his feelings with this outburst of sudden anger, he was already growing more good-natured with every word. “You may disbelieve a criminal, or a defendant tortured by your questions, but a man of the highest honor, gentlemen, the noblest impulses of his soul—I shout it boldly!—no! You cannot disbelieve those... you have no right even... but—

> Be silent, heart,  
> Endure, be humble, and be silent!

Well, shall I go on?” he broke off gloomily.

<!-- B09-C04-P057 -->
“Certainly, please do,” Nikolai Parfenovich answered.



[^b09-c04-n003]: “Let’s move on” (French).
