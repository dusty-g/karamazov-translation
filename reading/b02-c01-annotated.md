# Chapter I — They Arrive at the Monastery

*Part I · Book II: An Inappropriate Gathering*

<!-- B02-C01-P001 -->

The day was beautiful, warm, and clear. It was late August. The meeting with the elder had been
arranged for just after the late liturgy, at about half past eleven. Our visitors, however, did not
favor the monastery with their presence for the service; they arrived just as everyone was
collecting their hats to leave. They came in two carriages. In the first, a smart carriage drawn by
a pair of expensive horses, came Pyotr Alexandrovich Miusov with a distant relative of his, a very
young man of about twenty, Pyotr Fomich Kalganov. This young man was preparing to enter a university,
and Miusov, with whom he was staying for some reason, was tempting him to go abroad with him, to
Zurich or Jena, to enroll at a university there and complete his studies. The young man had not yet
decided. He was thoughtful and seemed distracted. He had a pleasant face, a sturdy build, and was
fairly tall. A strange stillness sometimes came into his eyes: like all very absentminded people, he
would sometimes stare straight at you for a long time without seeing you at all. He was quiet and
somewhat awkward, but at times—though only when alone with someone—he would suddenly become terribly
talkative, impetuous, and full of laughter, laughing sometimes at God knows what. But his animation
died away just as quickly and suddenly as it had come. He was always well dressed, even elegantly
so: he already had some money of his own and expected a great deal more. He was a friend of
Alyosha's.

<!-- B02-C01-P002 -->

Fyodor Pavlovich and his boy Ivan Fyodorovich arrived too, in a very dilapidated, rattling but roomy
hired carriage drawn by a pair of old pinkish-gray horses that had fallen far behind Miusov's
carriage. Dmitri Fyodorovich had been told the appointed time the day before, but he was late. The
visitors left their carriages at the inn beside the enclosure and went on foot through the monastery
gates. Apart from Fyodor Pavlovich, the other three had apparently never seen a monastery of any
kind, and Miusov had perhaps not even been inside a church for thirty years. He looked around with a
certain curiosity, accompanied by a certain air of nonchalance he had put on for the occasion. But
inside the monastery his observant mind found nothing to take in beyond the church buildings and
outbuildings, which were, for that matter, quite ordinary. The last of the congregation were coming
out of the church, taking off their hats and crossing themselves. Among the common people were
visitors from higher society, two or three ladies and one very old general, all staying at the inn.
Beggars surrounded our visitors at once, but no one gave them anything. Only Petrusha Kalganov took
a ten-kopeck coin from his purse and, suddenly flustered and embarrassed, God knows why, hurriedly
thrust it at a peasant woman, saying quickly, “Share it equally.” None of his companions said
anything to him about this, so he had no reason to be embarrassed, but when he noticed that, he grew
still more embarrassed.

<!-- B02-C01-P003 -->

It was strange, though: surely they ought to have been expected, perhaps even with a certain
distinction. One had recently donated a thousand rubles, and another was an exceedingly wealthy
landowner and, so to speak, a highly cultivated man, on whom everyone here was to some extent
dependent for the fishing in the river, depending on how the lawsuit turned out. And yet no one in
any official capacity had come to meet them. Miusov looked absently at the gravestones near the
church and was about to remark that those graves must have cost the people burying their dead a
pretty penny for the right to bury them in such a “holy” place, but he kept quiet: simple liberal
irony was turning into something very close to anger.

<!-- B02-C01-P004 -->

“Damn it, who do we ask in all this confusion?... We ought to settle that, because time is passing,”
he said suddenly, as though speaking to himself.

<!-- B02-C01-P005 -->

Just then an elderly, balding gentleman with sugary eyes, wearing a loose summer coat, came up to
them. Raising his hat, he introduced himself to the company at large in a honeyed lisp as Maximov, a
landowner from Tula. He immediately took our travelers under his wing.

<!-- B02-C01-P006 -->

“The elder Zosima lives in the hermitage, shut away in the hermitage, four hundred paces or so from
the monastery, through the little wood, through the little wood...”

<!-- B02-C01-P007 -->

“I know myself, sir, that it's through the little wood,” Fyodor Pavlovich replied, “but we don't
quite remember the way. It's been a long time since we were here.”

<!-- B02-C01-P008 -->

“Why, through these gates, and straight through the wood... the wood. Come along. If you would care
to... I myself... I can... This way, this way...”

<!-- B02-C01-P009 -->

They went out through the gates and set off through the wood. The landowner Maximov, a man of about
sixty, was not so much walking as almost running alongside them, examining them all with a
convulsive, almost inconceivable curiosity. There was something goggle-eyed about his eyes.

<!-- B02-C01-P010 -->

“You see, we have private business with this elder,” Miusov observed sternly. “We have, so to speak,
been granted an audience ‘with the aforesaid person,’ and so, grateful as we are to you for showing
us the way, we shall not be asking you to come in with us.”

<!-- B02-C01-P011 -->

“I've been, been, I've already been... *Un chevalier parfait!*”[^b02-c01-n001] And the landowner
snapped his fingers in the air.

<!-- B02-C01-P012 -->

“Who is this *chevalier*?” asked Miusov.

<!-- B02-C01-P013 -->

“The elder, a magnificent elder, the elder. Zosima is the honor and glory of the monastery. Such an
elder...”

<!-- B02-C01-P014 -->

But his disjointed speech was interrupted by a short monk in a monastic cowl who had caught up with
the travelers, very pale and haggard. Fyodor Pavlovich and Miusov stopped. With an exceedingly
courteous bow, almost from the waist, the monk said:

<!-- B02-C01-P015 -->

“Father Abbot most humbly invites all of you gentlemen to dine with him after your visit to the
hermitage. At one o'clock, no later. And you as well,” he added, turning to Maximov.

<!-- B02-C01-P016 -->

“I shall certainly do so!” cried Fyodor Pavlovich, enormously pleased by the invitation. “Certainly.
And you know, we've all given our word to behave properly here... Will you be joining us, Pyotr
Alexandrovich?”

<!-- B02-C01-P017 -->

“Why on earth wouldn't I? What else have I come here for, if not to see all their customs? There's
only one difficulty, and that is finding myself in your company, Fyodor Pavlovich...”

<!-- B02-C01-P018 -->

“Yes, Dmitri Fyodorovich doesn't exist yet.”

<!-- B02-C01-P019 -->

“And it would be just as well if he failed to turn up. Do you imagine I enjoy this whole mess of
yours, with you thrown in for good measure? Well, we shall come to dinner; please thank Father
Abbot,” he said to the monk.

<!-- B02-C01-P020 -->

“No, I must take you to the elder himself first,” the monk replied.

<!-- B02-C01-P021 -->

“Then I'll go to Father Abbot, I'll go straight to Father Abbot in the meantime,” the landowner
Maximov chirped.

<!-- B02-C01-P022 -->

“Father Abbot is occupied at present, but as you wish...” the monk said hesitantly.

<!-- B02-C01-P023 -->

“An insufferably intrusive little old man,” Miusov remarked aloud as the landowner Maximov ran back
toward the monastery.

<!-- B02-C01-P024 -->

“Looks like von Sohn,” Fyodor Pavlovich said suddenly.

<!-- B02-C01-P025 -->

“That's all you ever think of... What makes him look like von Sohn? Have you ever even seen von Sohn
yourself?”

<!-- B02-C01-P026 -->

“I've seen his photograph. If it isn't the features, then it's something indefinable. An absolute
second copy of von Sohn. I can always tell these things just from the face.”

<!-- B02-C01-P027 -->

“Perhaps so; you're an expert on such things. But listen, Fyodor Pavlovich: you yourself were just
pleased to mention that we had given our word to behave decently. Remember that. I'm telling you,
restrain yourself. If you start playing the buffoon, I have no intention of being put on the same
footing as you here... You see what sort of man he is,” he said to the monk. “I'm afraid to take him
into decent company.”

<!-- B02-C01-P028 -->

A faint, silent smile appeared on the monk's pale, bloodless lips, not without a certain slyness of
its own, but he made no reply, and it was all too clear that his silence came from a sense of his own
dignity. Miusov frowned still more.

<!-- B02-C01-P029 -->

“Oh, damn them all, a mere outward show cultivated over centuries, and underneath it all,
charlatanism and nonsense!” flashed through his mind.

<!-- B02-C01-P030 -->

“Here's the hermitage, we've arrived!” cried Fyodor Pavlovich. “An enclosure and locked gates.”

<!-- B02-C01-P031 -->

And he began crossing himself extravagantly before the saints painted above and beside the gates.

<!-- B02-C01-P032 -->

“You don't go into someone else's monastery with your own rule book,” he observed. “Twenty-five
saints in all working out their salvation here in the hermitage, looking at one another and eating
cabbage. And not a single woman comes through these gates—that's the particularly remarkable thing.
And it's quite true, too. But then how is it I've heard the elder receives ladies?” he asked the
monk suddenly.

<!-- B02-C01-P033 -->

“Women of the common people are here even now, over there, lying by the gallery, waiting. For ladies
of higher station two small rooms have been added here on the gallery, but outside the enclosure—you
can see their windows—and when the elder is well he goes to them by an inside passage, which still
takes him outside the enclosure. Even now a lady, a landowner from Kharkov, Madame Khokhlakov, is
waiting with her paralyzed daughter. He has probably promised to come out to them, though lately he
has grown so feeble that he scarcely appears before the people at all.”

<!-- B02-C01-P034 -->

“So they have, after all, made themselves a little loophole to the ladies. Don't imagine I mean
anything by it, holy father, I'm only saying. You know, on Mount Athos—have you heard of this?—they
don't just forbid visits from women, they forbid women altogether, and any female creature at all,
little hens, little turkeys, little heifers...”

<!-- B02-C01-P035 -->

“Fyodor Pavlovich, I'll turn back and leave you here on your own, and without me they'll take you by
the arms and lead you out. That's my prediction.”

<!-- B02-C01-P036 -->

“But how am I troubling you, Pyotr Alexandrovich? Just look!” he cried suddenly, stepping inside the
hermitage enclosure. “Look at the valley of roses they live in!”

<!-- B02-C01-P037 -->

In fact, although there were no roses now, there were masses of rare and beautiful autumn flowers
everywhere they could possibly be planted. They were clearly tended by an experienced hand. There
were flower beds in the churchyards and between the graves. The small wooden house containing the
elder's cell, a single-story building with a gallery before the entrance, was also planted all
around with flowers.

<!-- B02-C01-P038 -->

“Was all this here under the previous elder, Varsonofy? They say he had no taste for elegance, that
he would jump up and beat even the ladies with a stick,” Fyodor Pavlovich remarked as he climbed the
porch steps.

<!-- B02-C01-P039 -->

“The elder Varsonofy did sometimes seem rather like a holy fool, but people also tell a great deal
of nonsense. He never beat anyone with a stick,” the monk replied. “Now, gentlemen, please wait a
moment while I announce your arrival.”

<!-- B02-C01-P040 -->

“Fyodor Pavlovich, one last warning, do you hear? Behave yourself, or I'll make you pay for it,”
Miusov managed to mutter once more.

<!-- B02-C01-P041 -->

“There's no knowing what's got you so terribly worked up,” Fyodor Pavlovich observed mockingly.
“Afraid of your little sins, are you? They say he can tell from a person's eyes what has brought him
there. And how much you value their opinion—you, such a Parisian and a man of progress. You've
actually surprised me, that's what!”

<!-- B02-C01-P042 -->

But Miusov had no time to answer this sarcasm: they were asked to come in. He entered in a somewhat
irritated state...

<!-- B02-C01-P043 -->

“Well, now I know beforehand what I'll do: I'm irritated, I'll start arguing... I'll lose my
temper—and humiliate both myself and the idea,” flashed through his mind.
