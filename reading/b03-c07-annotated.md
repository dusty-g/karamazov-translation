# Book III: The Lustful
## VII. The Controversy

<!-- B03-C07-P001 -->
But Balaam's ass suddenly began to speak. The subject was a strange one. That morning, while collecting goods from the merchant Lukyanov's shop, Grigory had heard from him about a Russian soldier somewhere far away on the frontier, among the Asiatics. Taken prisoner, the soldier had been ordered to renounce Christianity and convert to Islam on pain of an immediate and agonizing death. He had refused to betray his faith, accepted the torture, let them flay him, and died glorifying and praising Christ. An account of this heroic deed had appeared in the newspaper that had arrived that very day. This was what Grigory began to tell them about at the table. Fyodor Pavlovich had always liked a laugh and a chat after dinner, over dessert, even if it was only with Grigory. This time he was in a light, pleasantly expansive mood. Sipping his cognac and hearing the news, he remarked that a soldier like that ought to be promoted to sainthood at once and his flayed skin forwarded to some monastery: “Just think how the people and the money would pour in.” Grigory grimaced to see that Fyodor Pavlovich was not moved in the slightest and was beginning to blaspheme as usual. Then suddenly Smerdyakov, standing at the door, smirked. Even before this, Smerdyakov had often been allowed to stand by the table, toward the end of dinner, that is. Since Ivan Fyodorovich's arrival in our town, he had begun appearing at dinner almost every day.

<!-- B03-C07-P002 -->
“What's that for?” Fyodor Pavlovich asked, catching the smirk at once and realizing, of course, that it was aimed at Grigory.

<!-- B03-C07-P003 -->
“Well, sir, what I mean is this,” Smerdyakov suddenly began, loudly and unexpectedly. “Though that praiseworthy soldier's deed was indeed very great, sir, in my opinion there would have been no sin either in renouncing, under those circumstances, say, the name of Christ and one's own baptism, so as to save one's life for good works, and through them atone over the years for one's faintheartedness.”

<!-- B03-C07-P004 -->
“What do you mean, no sin? You're lying. They'll send you straight to hell for that and roast you like a piece of mutton,” Fyodor Pavlovich put in.

<!-- B03-C07-P005 -->
It was at this very moment that Alyosha came in. As we have seen, Fyodor Pavlovich was overjoyed to see him.

<!-- B03-C07-P006 -->
“Your very own subject, your very own subject!” he giggled happily, seating Alyosha to listen.

<!-- B03-C07-P007 -->
“That's not so about the mutton, sir, and nothing will happen to me there for it, nor should it, in all justice,” Smerdyakov observed with gravity.

<!-- B03-C07-P008 -->
“What do you mean, in all justice?” cried Fyodor Pavlovich, more delighted than ever, nudging Alyosha with his knee.

<!-- B03-C07-P009 -->
“He's a scoundrel, that's what he is!” Grigory suddenly burst out. He looked Smerdyakov angrily in the eye.

<!-- B03-C07-P010 -->
“Hold off on the scoundrel for a moment, Grigory Vasilyevich,” Smerdyakov replied calmly and with restraint. “You'd do better to consider it yourself. If I'm taken prisoner by persecutors of the Christian race and they demand that I curse God's name and renounce my holy baptism, my own reason fully authorizes me to do so, since there will be no sin in it at all.”

<!-- B03-C07-P011 -->
“You've said that already. Stop going on about it and prove it!” cried Fyodor Pavlovich.

<!-- B03-C07-P012 -->
“Broth-maker!” Grigory whispered contemptuously.

<!-- B03-C07-P013 -->
“Hold off on the broth-maker too, sir, and consider it for yourself without calling names, Grigory Vasilyevich. For as soon as I tell my torturers, ‘No, I am not a Christian, and I curse my true God,’ then at once, by God's supreme judgment, I am immediately and specifically cursed with anathema and cast out of the holy Church, just like a heathen; indeed, at that very instant, sir—not even as soon as I say it, but as soon as I think of saying it, so that not even a quarter of a second passes before I'm cast out. Isn't that so, Grigory Vasilyevich?”

<!-- B03-C07-P014 -->
He addressed Grigory with obvious pleasure, although in fact he was answering only Fyodor Pavlovich's questions and knew it perfectly well; he was deliberately pretending that it was Grigory who had asked them.

<!-- B03-C07-P015 -->
“Ivan!” Fyodor Pavlovich suddenly cried. “Bend down, right to my ear. He's arranged all this for you, he wants you to praise him. Give him some praise.”

<!-- B03-C07-P016 -->
Ivan Fyodorovich listened to his papa's excited communication with complete seriousness.

<!-- B03-C07-P017 -->
“Wait, Smerdyakov, be quiet for a moment,” Fyodor Pavlovich cried again. “Ivan, bend down to my ear again.”

<!-- B03-C07-P018 -->
Ivan Fyodorovich bent down once more, with the gravest possible expression.

<!-- B03-C07-P019 -->
“I love you just as much as Alyoshka. Don't think I don't love you. Some cognac?”

<!-- B03-C07-P020 -->
“Give me some.” *You've had quite a load yourself, though,* thought Ivan Fyodorovich, looking hard at his father. He had been watching Smerdyakov with extraordinary curiosity.

<!-- B03-C07-P021 -->
“You're cursed with anathema already!” Grigory suddenly erupted. “And after that, you scoundrel, how dare you argue, when—”

<!-- B03-C07-P022 -->
“No abuse, Grigory, no abuse!” Fyodor Pavlovich interrupted.

<!-- B03-C07-P023 -->
“Wait, Grigory Vasilyevich, even if it's only the very shortest time, sir, and hear me out, because I haven't finished. For at the very time when God immediately curses me, sir, at that very, that supreme moment, sir, I've already become just like a heathen, and my baptism is taken away from me and counts for nothing. Surely that much is true, sir?”

<!-- B03-C07-P024 -->
“Get to the conclusion, my lad, hurry up and get to it,” Fyodor Pavlovich urged, taking a pleasurable gulp from his glass.

<!-- B03-C07-P025 -->
“And if I'm no longer a Christian, then I haven't lied to my torturers when they ask whether I am a Christian or not, because God himself has already stripped me of my Christianity for the mere intention, before I could even say a word to my torturers. And once I've been stripped of my rank, then how, and by what justice, can they call me to account in the next world as a Christian for renouncing Christ, when the mere thought, even before the renunciation, had already stripped me of my baptism? If I'm no longer a Christian, then I can't renounce Christ either, because there'll be nothing left for me to renounce. Who would call a filthy heathen Tatar to account, Grigory Vasilyevich, even in heaven, for not having been born a Christian, and who would punish him for it, considering that you can't take two hides off one ox? And even if God Almighty himself does call a Tatar to account when he dies, I suppose he'll give him some very small punishment—since you can't leave him entirely unpunished—reasoning that it's not his fault he came into the world a heathen, born of heathen parents. Surely the Lord God can't forcibly take a Tatar and say that he too was a Christian? That would mean the Lord Almighty was telling an outright untruth. And can the Lord Almighty of heaven and earth tell a lie, even in a single word, sir?”

<!-- B03-C07-P026 -->
Grigory stood dumbfounded, staring at the speaker with bulging eyes. Though he did not properly understand what was being said, something in all that rubbish had suddenly got through to him, and he stopped short like a man who had unexpectedly struck his forehead against a wall. Fyodor Pavlovich finished his glass and burst into shrill laughter.

<!-- B03-C07-P027 -->
“Alyoshka, Alyoshka, how's that! Oh, you casuist! He's been with the Jesuits somewhere, Ivan. Oh, you stinking Jesuit, who taught you all this? But you're lying, casuist, lying, lying, lying. Don't cry, Grigory, we'll smash him to dust this very minute. Tell me this, you ass: suppose you're in the right as far as your torturers are concerned, but inwardly you still renounced your faith, and you say yourself you were cursed with anathema that very instant. And once you're under anathema, they won't pat you on the head for it in hell. What do you think of that, my splendid Jesuit?”

<!-- B03-C07-P028 -->
“There's no doubt, sir, that I renounced it inwardly, but even in that there was no particular sin, sir, and if there was a little sin, it was a very ordinary one indeed, sir.”

<!-- B03-C07-P029 -->
“What do you mean, a very ordinary one indeed, sir!”

<!-- B03-C07-P030 -->
“You're lying, you d-d-damned creature,” Grigory hissed.

<!-- B03-C07-P031 -->
“Consider it yourself, Grigory Vasilyevich,” Smerdyakov continued evenly and with composure, conscious of his victory but making a show of magnanimity toward his defeated opponent. “Consider it yourself, Grigory Vasilyevich: doesn't Scripture say that if you have faith even as small as the smallest grain, and tell this mountain to move into the sea, it will move without the slightest delay, at your very first command? Well, then, Grigory Vasilyevich, if I'm an unbeliever and you're such a believer that you're forever abusing me, try telling this mountain yourself, sir, to move—not even into the sea, because the sea's a long way from here, sir, but just into our stinking little river that runs behind the garden. You'll see for yourself that very instant that nothing will move, sir, and everything will stay just as it was, all in one piece, however much you shout. And that means you don't believe properly either, Grigory Vasilyevich, yet you abuse other people every which way for it. Then again, take the fact that nowadays nobody—not only you, sir, but absolutely nobody, from the very highest personages down to the very lowest peasant—can shove a mountain into the sea, except perhaps one person on the whole earth, two at most, and even they may be somewhere in the Egyptian desert, secretly working out their salvation, so that you couldn't find them at all. If that's so, sir, if everyone else turns out to be an unbeliever, is the Lord really going to curse all those others, meaning the population of the whole earth, sir, except for those two hermits or so, and with all his well-known mercy forgive none of them? And so I trust that, if I should doubt once, I will be forgiven when I shed tears of repentance.”

<!-- B03-C07-P032 -->
“Wait!” Fyodor Pavlovich squealed in an ecstasy of delight. “So you do still suppose there are two such people who can move mountains? Ivan, mark that trait, write it down: there's the whole Russian in it!”

<!-- B03-C07-P033 -->
“You're quite right to point out that this is a feature of the people's faith,” Ivan Fyodorovich agreed with an approving smile.

<!-- B03-C07-P034 -->
“You agree! Then it must be so, if even you agree! Alyoshka, isn't that true? Isn't that a thoroughly Russian sort of faith?”

<!-- B03-C07-P035 -->
“No, Smerdyakov's faith is not Russian at all,” Alyosha said seriously and firmly.

<!-- B03-C07-P036 -->
“I don't mean his faith, I mean that trait, those two hermits, just that one little detail. That's Russian, isn't it, Russian?”

<!-- B03-C07-P037 -->
“Yes, that trait is thoroughly Russian,” Alyosha said, smiling.

<!-- B03-C07-P038 -->
“That remark of yours is worth a gold coin, you ass, and I'll send you one today, but in everything else you're still lying, lying, lying. Understand, fool, that all of us here are unbelievers only out of thoughtlessness, because we haven't the time: first, we're swamped with business, and second, God gave us so little time, allotting just twenty-four hours to the day, so there's not even time to get enough sleep, let alone repent. But there you were, before your torturers, renouncing your faith when you had nothing to think about except faith, and when that was precisely what you had to show! That makes a difference, my lad, I should think?”

<!-- B03-C07-P039 -->
“It makes a difference, certainly it makes a difference, but consider it yourself, Grigory Vasilyevich: the more difference it makes, the more it excuses me. For if I'd had true faith then, faith as it ought to be, it really would have been a sin not to accept torture for my faith and to convert to the foul Mohammedan faith. But it would never have come to torture then, sir, because all I'd have had to do at that very instant was say to this mountain, ‘Move and crush my torturer,’ and it would have moved and crushed him that instant like a cockroach, and I'd have walked away as though nothing had happened, singing the praises and glory of God. But suppose at that very moment I had tried all that, and had deliberately shouted to this mountain, ‘Crush these torturers,’ and it didn't crush them: tell me, how could I have failed to doubt then, and at such a terrible hour, in such great fear of death? I already know I won't attain the kingdom of heaven in all its fullness—because the mountain didn't move at my word, which means they don't have much faith in my faith up there, and there's no very great reward waiting for me in the next world—so why should I let them flay me on top of it, with nothing to be gained? Because even if they'd already stripped the skin halfway down my back, that mountain still wouldn't move at my word or my cry. At a moment like that, you can not only be overcome by doubt, you can even lose your reason from fear, so that it's quite impossible to reason at all. And therefore how am I particularly to blame if, seeing no advantage or reward for myself either there or here, I at least save my own skin? And so, trusting greatly in the Lord's mercy, I cherish the hope that I will be forgiven altogether, sir . . .”
