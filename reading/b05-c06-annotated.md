# Book V: Pro and Contra
## VI. Still Very Unclear

<!-- B05-C06-P001 -->
After parting with Alyosha, Ivan Fyodorovich went home, to Fyodor Pavlovich's house. But something strange happened: he was suddenly overcome by unbearable anguish, which, above all, grew with every step as he approached the house. The strange thing was not the anguish itself, but that Ivan Fyodorovich could not identify what it consisted of. He had often felt anguish before, and it would hardly have been surprising at a moment when, having abruptly broken with everything that had drawn him here, he was preparing to leave the very next day, turn sharply in another direction, and embark on a new, wholly unknown path, once again completely alone as before, hoping for much but not knowing for what, expecting much, far too much, from life, yet unable to define anything in his expectations or even his desires. And still, at this moment, although the anguish of the new and unknown really was in his soul, that was not what tormented him. “Is it disgust at my father's house?” he thought. “It looks like it. It's become so repulsive that even though today is the last time I'll cross that wretched threshold, I still can't bear it…” But no, that was not it either. Could it be his parting with Alyosha and their conversation? “All these years I've kept silent with everyone, not deigning to speak, and suddenly I've spouted so much nonsense.” Indeed, it might have been the youthful irritation of youthful inexperience and youthful vanity: irritation at having failed to express himself, and to someone like Alyosha, on whom he undoubtedly placed great hopes in his heart. That was there too, of course—the irritation certainly must have been there—but that was not it either, not it at all. “I'm sick with anguish, and I can't work out what I want. Perhaps I should stop thinking…”

<!-- B05-C06-P002 -->
Ivan Fyodorovich tried “not thinking,” but that did not help either. The main reason this anguish annoyed and irritated him was that it seemed somehow accidental, entirely external; he could feel that. Some person or object was standing somewhere, sticking out, like something that sometimes sticks out in front of your eyes and goes unnoticed for a long time while you're busy or deep in conversation, though all the while you're plainly irritated, almost tormented, until at last you think to remove the offending object, often something utterly trivial and ridiculous: an object left out of place, a handkerchief fallen on the floor, a book not put away in the bookcase, and so on and so on. At last Ivan Fyodorovich reached his father's house in a thoroughly foul, irritable mood, and suddenly, about fifteen paces from the wicket gate, he looked toward the main gate and understood at once what had been tormenting and troubling him.

<!-- B05-C06-P003 -->
The servant Smerdyakov was sitting on the bench by the gate, enjoying the cool evening air, and at his first glance Ivan Fyodorovich understood that the servant Smerdyakov was sitting in his soul too, and that this was the very man his soul could not bear. Everything suddenly lit up and became clear. Earlier, while Alyosha was telling him of his encounter with Smerdyakov, something dark and repulsive had pierced his heart and immediately provoked answering anger. Then, during the conversation, Smerdyakov had been forgotten for a time, but had remained in his soul; and as soon as Ivan Fyodorovich parted from Alyosha and walked home alone, the forgotten feeling quickly began to surface again. “Can that vile wretch really trouble me so much?” he thought with unbearable anger.

<!-- B05-C06-P004 -->
The fact was that Ivan Fyodorovich had indeed taken a strong dislike to this man lately, especially in the last few days. He had even begun to notice this growing near-hatred of the creature himself. Perhaps the hatred had become so acute precisely because at first, when Ivan Fyodorovich had just arrived in our town, things had been quite different. Then he had suddenly taken a particular interest in Smerdyakov and even found him very original. He himself had encouraged him to talk with him, though he was always puzzled by a certain muddle—or rather restlessness—in his mind, unable to understand what could so constantly and persistently trouble this “contemplative.” They talked about philosophical questions, even about why there was light on the first day when the sun, moon, and stars were only made on the fourth, and how this was to be understood. But Ivan Fyodorovich soon became convinced that it was not the sun, moon, and stars at all: although an interesting subject, for Smerdyakov they were utterly secondary, and he wanted something quite different. Be that as it may, an immense self-regard began to show itself and stand exposed, and wounded self-regard at that. Ivan Fyodorovich disliked this very much. His aversion began there. Later came the troubles in the house: Grushenka appeared, the scenes with Dmitri began, the turmoil started. They talked about this too, but although Smerdyakov always spoke of it with great agitation, it was again impossible to discover what he himself wanted. The illogic and disorder of certain wishes that escaped him involuntarily, always equally obscure, were enough to astonish one. Smerdyakov kept asking questions, indirect and obviously contrived questions, without explaining why; and usually, at the most heated moment of his own questioning, he would suddenly fall silent or turn to something entirely different. But what finally irritated Ivan Fyodorovich beyond endurance and filled him with such disgust was a peculiar, loathsome familiarity that Smerdyakov had begun displaying toward him, more and more markedly. It was not that he allowed himself to be impolite; on the contrary, he always spoke with extreme respect. Yet things had reached a point where Smerdyakov plainly considered himself, God knew why, to be in some sort of accord with Ivan Fyodorovich. He always spoke as though something had already been agreed between them, something secret, something once said on both sides, known only to the two of them and quite incomprehensible to the other mortals bustling around them. Yet here too Ivan Fyodorovich had long failed to understand the true cause of his growing aversion, and only very recently had he guessed what it was. Now, with a feeling of irritated revulsion, he was about to pass silently through the wicket gate without looking at Smerdyakov. But Smerdyakov rose from the bench, and from that gesture alone Ivan Fyodorovich knew at once that he wanted a special conversation. Ivan Fyodorovich looked at him and stopped; and the fact that he had suddenly stopped instead of walking past, as he had intended a moment earlier, infuriated him until he shook. With anger and disgust he stared at Smerdyakov's drained, eunuch-like face, the hair at his temples combed smooth, a little tuft brushed up on top. His slightly narrowed left eye winked and smirked, as though saying: “Where are you going? You won't go past. You see, we two intelligent men have something to discuss.” Ivan Fyodorovich trembled:

<!-- B05-C06-P005 -->
“Out of my way, scoundrel! I'm no companion of yours, you fool!” was about to fly from his tongue, but to his utter astonishment something quite different came out:

<!-- B05-C06-P006 -->
“Is Father asleep, or has he woken?” he asked quietly and meekly, surprising himself, and then, just as unexpectedly, sat down on the bench. For a moment he was almost frightened; he remembered that afterward. Smerdyakov stood facing him, his hands behind his back, looking at him confidently, almost sternly.

<!-- B05-C06-P007 -->
“He's still resting, sir,” he said unhurriedly. (“You spoke first, not me.”) “I'm surprised at you, sir,” he added after a pause, lowering his eyes affectedly, putting his right foot forward and playing with the toe of his patent-leather shoe.

<!-- B05-C06-P008 -->
“Why are you surprised at me?” Ivan Fyodorovich asked curtly and harshly, restraining himself with all his might. Suddenly he realized with disgust that he was intensely curious and would not leave until his curiosity was satisfied.

<!-- B05-C06-P009 -->
“Why don't you go to Chermashnya, sir?” Smerdyakov suddenly raised his eyes and smiled familiarly. “And if you're an intelligent man, you ought to know why I'm smiling,” his narrowed left eye seemed to say.

<!-- B05-C06-P010 -->
“Why should I go to Chermashnya?” Ivan Fyodorovich asked in surprise.

<!-- B05-C06-P011 -->
Smerdyakov paused again.

<!-- B05-C06-P012 -->
“Fyodor Pavlovich himself has begged you so earnestly, sir,” he said at last, slowly and as though he attached no importance to his answer: I'm putting you off with a third-rate reason, just to say something.

<!-- B05-C06-P013 -->
“Oh, damn it, speak plainly! What do you want?” Ivan Fyodorovich finally cried angrily, passing from meekness to rudeness.

<!-- B05-C06-P014 -->
Smerdyakov brought his right foot back beside his left and stood straighter, but continued to look at him with the same calm and the same little smile.

<!-- B05-C06-P015 -->
“Nothing substantial, sir… just making conversation…”

<!-- B05-C06-P016 -->
Silence fell again. They were silent for nearly a minute. Ivan Fyodorovich knew he ought to get up at once and lose his temper, while Smerdyakov stood before him as though waiting: “Now let's see whether you'll get angry.” That, at least, was how it seemed to Ivan Fyodorovich. At last he shifted to rise. Smerdyakov seemed to seize the exact moment.

<!-- B05-C06-P017 -->
“My position is dreadful, Ivan Fyodorovich. I don't even know how to help myself,” he suddenly said firmly and distinctly, sighing at his last words. Ivan Fyodorovich immediately settled back into his seat.

<!-- B05-C06-P018 -->
“They're both quite crazy, sir, both reduced to the behavior of the smallest children,” Smerdyakov continued. “I mean your father and your brother Dmitri Fyodorovich. Now Fyodor Pavlovich will get up and start pestering me every minute: ‘Why hasn't she come? Why hasn't she come?’ And so on until midnight, even past midnight. And if Agrafena Alexandrovna doesn't come—for perhaps she has no intention of ever coming at all, sir—he'll fall on me again tomorrow morning: ‘Why didn't she come? Why didn't she come, when will she come?’ As though I were somehow to blame. On the other hand, there's this, sir: as soon as it gets dark, or even earlier, your brother will turn up next door with a weapon in his hands. ‘Watch out, you rogue, you soup cook: if you miss her and don't tell me she's come, I'll kill you before anyone else.’ The night passes, and in the morning he'll begin tormenting me just as mercilessly as Fyodor Pavlovich: ‘Why didn't she come? Will she show herself soon?’ As though, once again, I were to blame for his lady's failure to appear. And with every day and every hour they both grow so much angrier, sir, that sometimes I think of taking my own life out of fear. I have no confidence in them, sir.”

<!-- B05-C06-P019 -->
“Why did you get involved? Why did you start carrying tales to Dmitri Fyodorovich?” Ivan Fyodorovich asked irritably.

<!-- B05-C06-P020 -->
“How could I help getting involved, sir? In fact I didn't get involved at all, if you wish to know the exact truth. From the very beginning I kept silent, not daring to object, and he himself appointed me his servant Licharda.[^b05-c06-n001] Since then he's known only one thing to say: ‘I'll kill you, you rogue, if you let her through!’ I firmly believe, sir, that tomorrow I'll have a long epileptic fit.”

<!-- B05-C06-P021 -->
“What do you mean, a long epileptic fit?”

<!-- B05-C06-P022 -->
“A long attack, sir, an extremely long one. They last several hours, sir, or perhaps a day or two. Once it lasted three days; that was when I fell from the attic. The convulsions would stop and then start again, and I couldn't come to my senses for three days. Fyodor Pavlovich sent for Herzenstube, the doctor here, and he put ice on the crown of my head and used another remedy as well… I might have died, sir.”

<!-- B05-C06-P023 -->
“But they say you can't predict an epileptic fit, that it will happen at such-and-such an hour. How can you say it will come tomorrow?” Ivan Fyodorovich asked with a peculiar, irritable curiosity.

<!-- B05-C06-P024 -->
“It's true you can't predict it, sir.”

<!-- B05-C06-P025 -->
“Besides, that time you fell from the attic.”

<!-- B05-C06-P026 -->
“I climb into the attic every day, sir. I could fall from it tomorrow too. And if not from the attic, then into the cellar. I go into the cellar every day too, sir, on my business.”

<!-- B05-C06-P027 -->
Ivan Fyodorovich looked at him for a long time.

<!-- B05-C06-P028 -->
“You're spinning something, I see, and I don't quite understand you,” he said quietly, but somehow menacingly. “Do you mean to pretend tomorrow that you're having fits for three days? Is that it?”

<!-- B05-C06-P029 -->
Smerdyakov, who had been looking at the ground and again playing with his right toe, put his right foot back and extended his left instead, raised his head, and said with a smirk:

<!-- B05-C06-P030 -->
“Even if I could perform that trick, sir, that is, pretend—and it's not at all difficult for someone experienced—even then I'd be entirely within my rights to use that means to save my life from death. For when I'm lying ill, even if Agrafena Alexandrovna comes to his father, he can't demand of a sick man, ‘Why didn't you report it?’ He'd be ashamed himself.”

<!-- B05-C06-P031 -->
“Oh, damn it!” Ivan Fyodorovich suddenly flared up, his face twisted with anger. “Why are you always trembling for your life? All these threats of Dmitri's are just words spoken in a rage, nothing more. He won't kill you. He'll kill, but not you!”

<!-- B05-C06-P032 -->
“He'd kill me like a fly, sir, and me before anyone else. But I'm even more afraid of something else: that they'll consider me his accomplice when he does something outrageous to his father.”

<!-- B05-C06-P033 -->
“Why would they consider you an accomplice?”

<!-- B05-C06-P034 -->
“Because I've told him those very signals, in the strictest secrecy, sir.”

<!-- B05-C06-P035 -->
“What signals? Who did you tell? Damn you, speak more clearly!”

<!-- B05-C06-P036 -->
“I must make a full confession,” Smerdyakov drawled with pedantic calm. “There's a secret here between Fyodor Pavlovich and me. As you yourself know—if indeed you do know—for several days now, as soon as night or even evening comes, he locks himself in. Lately you've taken to going back upstairs early every time, and yesterday you didn't come out at all, sir, so perhaps you don't know how carefully he's begun locking himself in for the night. Even if Grigory Vasilyevich himself came, he'd only open the door after making sure of his voice. But Grigory Vasilyevich doesn't come, sir, because I'm the only one who waits on him in his rooms now. He arranged it himself from the very moment he began this scheme with Agrafena Alexandrovna. And now, at his orders, I too withdraw at night and sleep in the servants' lodge, on the understanding that I don't sleep until midnight, but stay on duty, get up and go around the yard, and wait for Agrafena Alexandrovna to come, sir, because for several days he's been expecting her like a madman. This is how he reasons, sir: she's afraid of him, he says, meaning Dmitri Fyodorovich—he calls him Mitka, sir—and so she'll come to me late at night, by the back way. You keep watch for her, he says, until midnight and beyond. And if she comes, run to the door and knock on the door, or with your hand on the window from the garden: first two soft knocks, like this, one-two, then immediately three quicker ones, knock-knock-knock. Then I'll know at once that she's come, he says, and quietly open the door for you. He gave me another signal in case something urgent happened: first two quick knocks, knock-knock, then, after a pause, one more, much louder. Then he'll understand that something unexpected has happened and I badly need to see him; he'll open the door again, and I'll go in and report. All this in case Agrafena Alexandrovna doesn't come herself but sends word about something. Besides, Dmitri Fyodorovich might come too, so I'd have to report that he's nearby. He's very afraid of Dmitri Fyodorovich, so even if Agrafena Alexandrovna has already come and he's shut himself in with her, if Dmitri Fyodorovich turns up somewhere nearby, I'm still absolutely obliged to report it at once by knocking three times. So the first signal, five knocks, means ‘Agrafena Alexandrovna has come,’ and the second signal, three knocks, means ‘It's very urgent.’ That's how he explained it and demonstrated it to me several times himself. And since we're the only two people in the entire universe who know these signals, he'll open the door without any doubts or calling out at all—he's very afraid to call out aloud. Well, these very signals have now become known to Dmitri Fyodorovich.”

<!-- B05-C06-P037 -->
“How did they become known? Did you tell him? How dared you tell him?”

<!-- B05-C06-P038 -->
“Out of that very fear, sir. How could I have dared keep it from him? Dmitri Fyodorovich kept pressing me every day: ‘You're deceiving me. What are you hiding from me? I'll break both your legs!’ So I told him those secret signals, so at least he would see my servility and be convinced that I wasn't deceiving him, but reporting everything I could.”

<!-- B05-C06-P039 -->
“If you think he'll use the signals and try to get in, don't let him.”

<!-- B05-C06-P040 -->
“But when I'm lying in a fit myself, sir, how can I keep him out, even if I could dare refuse him entry, knowing him to be so desperate, sir?”

<!-- B05-C06-P041 -->
“Oh, damn it! Why are you so sure you'll have a fit, damn you? Are you making fun of me or not?”

<!-- B05-C06-P042 -->
“How could I dare make fun of you? Is this a time for laughter, with such fear? I have a presentiment of a fit, I have that presentiment; the fear alone will bring it on, sir.”

<!-- B05-C06-P043 -->
“Oh, to hell with it! If you're laid up, Grigory will keep watch. Warn Grigory beforehand; he certainly won't let him in.”

<!-- B05-C06-P044 -->
“I wouldn't dare tell Grigory Vasilyevich about the signals without the master's orders, sir. As for Grigory Vasilyevich hearing him and keeping him out, he happens to have fallen ill today, since yesterday, and Marfa Ignatyevna intends to treat him tomorrow. They agreed on it earlier. Their treatment is very curious, sir: Marfa Ignatyevna knows a certain tincture and always keeps it—a strong one, made from some herb; she possesses that secret, sir. She treats Grigory Vasilyevich with this secret medicine about three times a year, when his whole lower back gives out, sir, almost as if he were paralyzed, about three times a year. Then she takes a towel, sir, wets it in the tincture, and rubs his whole back for half an hour, until it's dry, sir, until it actually grows red and swollen. Then she gives him the rest from the bottle to drink, sir, with a certain prayer—not all of it, though, because on this rare occasion she keeps a small portion for herself and drinks it too, sir. And I tell you, since neither of them drinks, they both collapse and sleep very deeply for a long time, sir. When Grigory Vasilyevich wakes, he's almost always well afterward, but when Marfa Ignatyevna wakes, she always has a headache. So if Marfa Ignatyevna carries out her intention tomorrow, sir, they're unlikely to hear anything or keep Dmitri Fyodorovich out. They'll be asleep, sir.”

<!-- B05-C06-P045 -->
“What nonsense! And everything will just happen to coincide, as if on purpose: you having fits and both of them unconscious!” Ivan Fyodorovich shouted. “Don't you mean to arrange it yourself so it all coincides?” he suddenly blurted out, frowning menacingly.

<!-- B05-C06-P046 -->
“How could I arrange it, sir… and why arrange anything when it all depends on Dmitri Fyodorovich alone, sir, and on his thoughts alone? If he wants to do something, he'll do it; if not, I'm hardly going to bring him there on purpose and push him in to his father.”

<!-- B05-C06-P047 -->
“Why should he go in to Father, and secretly at that, if, as you yourself say, Agrafena Alexandrovna won't come at all?” Ivan Fyodorovich continued, turning pale with anger. “You say so yourself, and all the time I've lived here I've been certain the old man's only fantasizing and that creature won't come to him. Why should Dmitri break in on the old man if she doesn't come? Speak! I want to know your thoughts.”

<!-- B05-C06-P048 -->
“You know yourself why he'd come; what have my thoughts to do with it? He'd come out of sheer anger, or out of suspicion if, for example, I was ill; he'd grow doubtful and in his impatience go searching through the rooms, as he did yesterday: perhaps she's somehow slipped in without his knowing. He also knows perfectly well that Fyodor Pavlovich has a large envelope ready, with three thousand inside, sealed with three seals, sir, tied with a ribbon and inscribed in his own hand: ‘To my angel Grushenka, if she cares to come.’ Then three days later he added: ‘and my little chick.’ Now that's what makes it doubtful, sir.”

<!-- B05-C06-P049 -->
“Nonsense!” Ivan Fyodorovich shouted, almost beside himself. “Dmitri won't go and steal the money, and kill his father into the bargain. Yesterday he might have killed him over Grushenka, like a crazed, furious fool, but he won't go there to rob him!”

<!-- B05-C06-P050 -->
“He needs money very badly now, sir, desperately badly, Ivan Fyodorovich. You don't even know how badly,” Smerdyakov explained with extreme calm and remarkable distinctness. “Besides, he considers those very three thousand his own, in a manner of speaking, and explained it to me himself: ‘My father still owes me exactly three thousand,’ he says. And on top of all that, Ivan Fyodorovich, consider a certain plain truth, sir: it's almost certain, one must say, that if Agrafena Alexandrovna wishes, she'll make him marry her—the master himself, Fyodor Pavlovich, I mean—if she wishes, sir. And perhaps she will wish it. I'm only saying she won't come, but perhaps she'll want more than that: to become mistress of the house outright. I know myself that her merchant Samsonov told her quite frankly that it wouldn't be a foolish thing to do, and laughed as he said it. And she's far from foolish herself, sir. She has no reason to marry a pauper like Dmitri Fyodorovich. So now, taking that into account, consider for yourself, Ivan Fyodorovich: then neither Dmitri Fyodorovich nor even you and your brother Alexei Fyodorovich will get anything at all after your father's death, not a ruble, sir, because Agrafena Alexandrovna would marry him precisely to have everything made over to her, every bit of capital transferred to her name. But if your father died now, before any of that has happened, each of you would immediately receive a sure forty thousand, even Dmitri Fyodorovich, whom he hates so much, because he hasn't made a will, sir… Dmitri Fyodorovich knows all that perfectly well…”

<!-- B05-C06-P051 -->
Something seemed to twist and tremble in Ivan Fyodorovich's face. He suddenly reddened.

<!-- B05-C06-P052 -->
“Then why,” he suddenly interrupted Smerdyakov, “after all this, do you advise me to go to Chermashnya? What did you mean by that? I'll leave, and this is what will happen here.” Ivan Fyodorovich had difficulty catching his breath.

<!-- B05-C06-P053 -->
“Precisely so, sir,” Smerdyakov said quietly and judiciously, though watching Ivan Fyodorovich intently.

<!-- B05-C06-P054 -->
“What do you mean, precisely so?” Ivan Fyodorovich asked, struggling to control himself, his eyes flashing menacingly.

<!-- B05-C06-P055 -->
“I said it out of concern for you. In your place, if it were me here, I'd leave it all behind… rather than stay beside such a business, sir…” Smerdyakov answered, looking into Ivan Fyodorovich's flashing eyes with the most open expression. Both fell silent.

<!-- B05-C06-P056 -->
“You seem to be a great idiot, and certainly… a dreadful scoundrel!” Ivan Fyodorovich suddenly rose from the bench. He was about to pass straight through the wicket gate, but suddenly stopped and turned toward Smerdyakov. Something strange happened: Ivan Fyodorovich abruptly bit his lip as though in a spasm and clenched his fists; another instant and he would certainly have thrown himself on Smerdyakov. At least Smerdyakov noticed it that same instant, started, and recoiled with his whole body. But the moment passed safely for him, and Ivan Fyodorovich turned silently toward the gate, as though in some bewilderment.

<!-- B05-C06-P057 -->
“I'm leaving for Moscow tomorrow, if you want to know—early tomorrow morning—that's all!” he suddenly said, angrily, distinctly, and loudly, later wondering himself what had made him feel the need to tell Smerdyakov that.

<!-- B05-C06-P058 -->
“That would be the very best thing, sir,” the other picked up, as though he had been waiting for it. “Only they might trouble you in Moscow with a telegram from here, sir, in the event of anything of that sort.”

<!-- B05-C06-P059 -->
Ivan Fyodorovich stopped again and again turned quickly toward Smerdyakov. But something seemed to have happened to him too. All his familiarity and carelessness had vanished in an instant; his whole face expressed extreme attention and expectation, but now timid and obsequious. “Won't you say something else, won't you add something?” could plainly be read in his fixed gaze, which seemed fastened onto Ivan Fyodorovich.

<!-- B05-C06-P060 -->
“And wouldn't they summon me from Chermashnya too… in the event of anything of that sort?” Ivan Fyodorovich suddenly shouted, for some unknown reason raising his voice enormously.

<!-- B05-C06-P061 -->
“From Chermashnya too, sir… they'd trouble you, sir…” Smerdyakov murmured almost in a whisper, as though disconcerted, but still staring, staring straight into Ivan Fyodorovich's eyes.

<!-- B05-C06-P062 -->
“Only Moscow's farther away, and Chermashnya's nearer. So is it the posting fare you're worried about when you insist on Chermashnya, or is it me you're sorry for, having to make such a long detour?”

<!-- B05-C06-P063 -->
“Precisely so, sir…” Smerdyakov murmured, his voice now failing him, smiling nastily and again bracing himself convulsively to leap back in time. But to Smerdyakov's surprise, Ivan Fyodorovich suddenly laughed and passed quickly through the gate, still laughing. Anyone looking at his face would certainly have concluded that he was not laughing because he was amused. Nor could he himself have explained what was happening to him at that moment. His movements and his walk were almost convulsive.

[^b05-c06-n001]: Licharda is a servant in the popular Russian tale of Bova the Prince. Smerdyakov uses the name for the role Dmitri has forced on him.
