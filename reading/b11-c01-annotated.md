# Book XI: Brother Ivan Fyodorovich
## I. At Grushenka’s

<!-- B11-C01-P001 -->
Alyosha headed for Cathedral Square, to Grushenka’s rooms in the merchant woman Morozova’s house. Early that morning she had sent Fenya with an urgent request that he come. On questioning Fenya, Alyosha learned that her mistress had been in a state of great and unusual anxiety since the day before. Throughout the two months since Mitya’s arrest, Alyosha had often visited Morozova’s house, both on his own initiative and on errands for Mitya. Three days after the arrest, Grushenka had fallen seriously ill and remained ill for nearly five weeks. For one of those weeks she had been unconscious. Her face had changed greatly; she had grown thin and sallow, although for almost two weeks now she had been well enough to go out. To Alyosha, however, her face seemed more attractive than ever, and he liked meeting her eyes when he came in. Something firm and thoughtful seemed to have taken hold in her gaze. Some spiritual upheaval was making itself felt, revealing a steady, humble, yet kindly and irrevocable resolve. A small vertical crease had appeared on her forehead between her eyebrows, giving her lovely face an air of inward concentration, almost stern at first glance. Of her former flightiness, for instance, not a trace remained. Alyosha also found it strange that, despite all the misfortune that had befallen this poor woman—the bride of a man arrested for a terrible crime almost at the very moment she became his bride—despite her subsequent illness and the all but inevitable verdict looming ahead, Grushenka had still not lost her old youthful gaiety. A kind of serenity now shone in her once proud eyes, although… although, for that matter, from time to time those eyes still blazed with a rather ominous fire when an old anxiety visited her, one that, far from dying away, had grown stronger in her heart. Its object was still the same: Katerina Ivanovna, whom Grushenka had mentioned even in her delirium while she was ill. Alyosha understood that she was fiercely jealous of her over Mitya, the imprisoned Mitya, although Katerina Ivanovna had never once visited him in prison, as she could have done whenever she wished. All this had become a difficult problem for Alyosha, for Grushenka confided her heart to him alone and continually asked his advice, while at times he could find nothing at all to say to her.

<!-- B11-C01-P002 -->
He entered her rooms with some anxiety. She was already home, having returned from Mitya’s half an hour before, and the very swiftness with which she sprang from her chair at the table to meet him told him how impatiently she had been waiting. Cards lay on the table, dealt for a game of Fool. On the leather sofa across the table a bed had been made, and on it Maximov lay propped up in a dressing gown and cotton nightcap, visibly ill and weak, though smiling sweetly. Ever since returning with Grushenka from Mokroye two months earlier, this homeless old man had remained with her, never leaving her side. Arriving with her then in rain and slush, soaked through and frightened, he had sat down on the sofa and stared at her in silence with a timid, pleading smile. Grushenka, in terrible grief and already coming down with a fever, had almost forgotten him amid her various concerns for the first half hour after their return; then she suddenly looked hard at him. He gave her a pitiful, lost little giggle. She called Fenya and told her to give him something to eat. He sat in the same place all day, scarcely moving; when it grew dark and the shutters had been closed, Fenya asked her mistress:

<!-- B11-C01-P003 -->
“Ma’am, is he staying the night, then?”

<!-- B11-C01-P004 -->
“Yes, make up a bed for him on the sofa,” Grushenka answered.

<!-- B11-C01-P005 -->
Questioning him more closely, Grushenka learned that he really did have nowhere at all to go just then, and that “Mr. Kalganov, my benefactor, told me outright he wouldn’t take me in anymore, and gave me five rubles.” “Well, God be with you, stay then,” Grushenka decided in her misery, giving him a compassionate smile. At that smile a shudder passed through the old man, and his lips trembled with grateful tears. And so the wandering dependent had stayed with her ever since. Even during her illness he had not left the house. Fenya and her mother, Grushenka’s cook, did not turn him out but went on feeding him and making his bed on the sofa. In time Grushenka even grew used to him, and when she came back from Mitya—whom she had begun visiting the moment she was a little better, before she had properly recovered—she would sit down and talk to “Maximushka” about all sorts of trifles to ease her misery, simply to keep her mind off her grief. It turned out that the old man sometimes had a story worth telling, so that he eventually became almost indispensable to her. Apart from Alyosha, who did not come every day and never stayed long, Grushenka received scarcely anyone. Her old merchant, meanwhile, lay desperately ill: he was “passing on,” as people in town said, and indeed he died just a week after Mitya’s trial. Three weeks before his death, sensing the end approaching, he finally summoned his sons upstairs with their wives and children and ordered them not to leave him again. From that moment he strictly forbade the servants to admit Grushenka; if she came, they were to tell her: “He wishes you a long and happy life, and asks you to forget him altogether.” Grushenka nevertheless sent almost every day to inquire after his health.

<!-- B11-C01-P006 -->
“At last you’re here!” she cried, throwing down her cards and greeting Alyosha joyfully. “Maximushka kept frightening me, saying you might not come at all. Oh, how I need you! Sit down at the table. What will you have—coffee?”

<!-- B11-C01-P007 -->
“Yes, please,” Alyosha said, sitting down at the table. “I’m very hungry.”

<!-- B11-C01-P008 -->
“There now! Fenya, Fenya, coffee!” Grushenka called. “It’s been boiling away, waiting for you. And bring some pies, nice and hot. No, wait, Alyosha, these pies caused a storm today. I took some to him at the prison, and would you believe it, he threw them back at me and wouldn’t eat a thing. One pie he actually flung on the floor and stamped on. So I said, ‘I’ll leave them with the guard. If you haven’t eaten them by tonight, it means your poisonous spite is feeding you!’ And I left. We’ve quarreled again, believe it or not. Every time I go, we quarrel.”

<!-- B11-C01-P009 -->
Grushenka poured all this out in a single agitated burst. Maximov, instantly intimidated, smiled with his eyes lowered.

<!-- B11-C01-P010 -->
“What did you quarrel about this time?” Alyosha asked.

<!-- B11-C01-P011 -->
“Something I never expected! Imagine, he’s jealous of my ‘old one’: ‘Why are you keeping him? So you’ve started keeping him now?’ Always jealous, always jealous of me! Sleeping or eating—jealous. Once last week he was even jealous of Kuzma.”

<!-- B11-C01-P012 -->
“But he knew about your ‘old one,’ didn’t he?”

<!-- B11-C01-P013 -->
“Exactly. He’s known from the very beginning right up to today, and today he suddenly got up and started abusing me. I’m ashamed even to repeat what he said. The fool! Rakitka came in just as I was leaving. Perhaps Rakitka’s setting him on me, eh? What do you think?” she added, as if distracted.

<!-- B11-C01-P014 -->
“He loves you, that’s what it is. He loves you very much. And just now he’s irritable.”

<!-- B11-C01-P015 -->
“No wonder he’s irritable, with his trial tomorrow. That’s why I went, to have my say about tomorrow, because, Alyosha, I’m afraid even to think what will happen! You say he’s irritable—what about me? And all he can talk about is the Pole! What a fool! He isn’t jealous of Maximushka, I notice.”

<!-- B11-C01-P016 -->
“My wife was very jealous of me too,” Maximov put in.

<!-- B11-C01-P017 -->
“Of you!” Grushenka laughed despite herself. “Who could she have been jealous of you with?”

<!-- B11-C01-P018 -->
“The maids.”

<!-- B11-C01-P019 -->
“Oh, be quiet, Maximushka, I’m in no mood for jokes. I’m getting cross. Don’t stare at those pies: you’re not having any, they’re bad for you, and you’re not getting any of that cordial either. As if I haven’t enough to do without looking after him as well! My house is a regular almshouse, honestly,” she laughed.

<!-- B11-C01-P020 -->
“I don’t deserve your kindness. I’m a worthless creature,” Maximov said in a small, tearful voice. “You would do better to lavish your kindness on people who are needed more than I am.”

<!-- B11-C01-P021 -->
“Oh, everyone’s of use, Maximushka, and how can you tell who’s more use than whom? I wish that Pole didn’t exist at all, Alyosha. He’s decided to fall ill today too. I’ve been to see him as well. And now I’ll send him some pies out of sheer spite. I haven’t sent him any, but Mitya accused me of it, so now I’ll do it on purpose, on purpose! Ah, here’s Fenya with a letter. Yes, just as I thought: the Poles again, asking for money again!”

<!-- B11-C01-P022 -->
Pan Mussyalovich had indeed sent an extraordinarily long and elaborate letter, as was his habit, asking her to lend him three rubles. Enclosed was a receipt for the money, with an undertaking to repay it within three months; Pan Wróblewski had signed it too. Grushenka had already received a great many such letters, all with similar receipts, from her “old one.” It had begun as soon as she recovered, some two weeks before. She knew, however, that both gentlemen had come to inquire after her health even during her illness. The first letter Grushenka received was a long one, written on a large sheet of notepaper, sealed with an imposing family seal, and so dreadfully obscure and ornate that Grushenka read only half before throwing it aside, having understood precisely nothing. Besides, she had no time for letters then. The next day a second followed, in which Pan Mussyalovich asked her to lend him two thousand rubles for the briefest possible period. Grushenka left that letter unanswered too. A whole series followed, one a day, all just as grand and elaborate, but with the sum requested gradually falling to a hundred rubles, then twenty-five, then ten, until suddenly Grushenka received a letter in which the two gentlemen asked for just one ruble, enclosing a receipt signed by both of them. Then she suddenly felt sorry for them and, at dusk, ran over to see the gentleman herself. She found the two Poles in dreadful poverty, almost destitute, with no food, no firewood, no cigarettes, and in debt to their landlady. The two hundred rubles they had won from Mitya at Mokroye had quickly vanished somewhere. What surprised Grushenka, though, was that both gentlemen received her with haughty dignity and independence, the utmost ceremony and the most inflated speeches. Grushenka simply laughed and gave her “old one” ten rubles. Laughing still, she told Mitya about it that same day, and he was not jealous at all. But from then on the gentlemen latched on to Grushenka, bombarding her every day with letters asking for money, and each time she sent them a little. And now, today, Mitya had suddenly taken it into his head to be fiercely jealous.

<!-- B11-C01-P023 -->
“I was a fool to stop in there too, only for a minute, on my way to Mitya, because he’s fallen ill as well, my former gentleman,” Grushenka began again, flustered and hurried. “So I’m laughing and telling Mitya: ‘Imagine, my Pole’s taken to playing the guitar and singing me the old songs; he thinks I’ll get sentimental and marry him.’ And Mitya jumps up and starts swearing at me… Well, no, I’ll send the gentlemen some pies! Fenya, have they sent that girl here? Here, give her three rubles and wrap up ten or so pies in paper for them, and tell her to take them over. And you, Alyosha, be sure to tell Mitya I sent them pies.”

<!-- B11-C01-P024 -->
“I certainly won’t,” Alyosha said with a smile.

<!-- B11-C01-P025 -->
“Oh, you think he’s suffering. He acted jealous on purpose; he doesn’t care a bit,” Grushenka said bitterly.

<!-- B11-C01-P026 -->
“What do you mean, on purpose?” Alyosha asked.

<!-- B11-C01-P027 -->
“You’re a fool, Alyoshenka, that’s what you are. For all your cleverness, you don’t understand a thing about this. What hurts me isn’t that he’s jealous of a woman like me; what would hurt me is if he weren’t jealous at all. That’s how I am. I don’t take offense at jealousy. I have a cruel heart myself; I get jealous too. What hurts me is that he doesn’t love me at all, and now he’s made himself jealous on purpose, that’s what. Am I blind? Can’t I see? All of a sudden he starts talking to me about her, about Katka: how wonderful she is, how she’s sent for a doctor from Moscow for his trial, sent for him to save him, and sent for the very best lawyer too, the most learned one. So he loves her, if he’s started praising her to my face—the shameless look of him! He’s the one who’s wronged me, so he’s picked a fight with me to make me the guilty one first and put all the blame on me: ‘You were with your Pole before I did anything, so that gave me the right to be with Katka.’ That’s what it is! He wants to put all the blame on me. He picked the fight on purpose, on purpose, I tell you, but I’ll…”

<!-- B11-C01-P028 -->
Grushenka did not finish saying what she would do. She covered her eyes with her handkerchief and burst into violent sobs.

<!-- B11-C01-P029 -->
“He doesn’t love Katerina Ivanovna,” Alyosha said firmly.

<!-- B11-C01-P030 -->
“Well, whether he loves her or not, I’ll soon find out for myself,” Grushenka said with a threatening note in her voice, taking the handkerchief from her eyes. Her face was distorted. Alyosha saw with sorrow how suddenly it had changed from gentle and quietly cheerful to sullen and spiteful.

<!-- B11-C01-P031 -->
“Enough of this nonsense!” she broke off abruptly. “It’s not why I sent for you at all. Alyosha, dear, what will happen tomorrow, tomorrow? That’s what’s tormenting me! And I’m the only one it torments! I look at everyone, and no one’s thinking about it, no one cares a bit. Are you at least thinking about it? His trial’s tomorrow! Tell me how they’re going to try him. It was the servant, the servant who killed him, the servant! Lord! Are they really going to convict him instead of the servant, and no one will stand up for him? They haven’t even troubled the servant, have they?”

<!-- B11-C01-P032 -->
“He was questioned very closely,” Alyosha observed thoughtfully, “but they all concluded it wasn’t him. He’s lying very ill now. He’s been ill ever since then, since that epileptic fit. He really is ill,” Alyosha added.

<!-- B11-C01-P033 -->
“Lord, why don’t you go to that lawyer yourself and explain the case to him face to face? They’ve brought him from Petersburg for three thousand, people say.”

<!-- B11-C01-P034 -->
“The three of us provided the three thousand: me, my brother Ivan, and Katerina Ivanovna. She sent for the doctor from Moscow herself, for two thousand. The lawyer, Fetyukovich, would have charged more, but the case has become known all over Russia; all the newspapers and magazines are talking about it. Fetyukovich agreed to come more for the fame, because it’s become such a celebrated case. I saw him yesterday.”

<!-- B11-C01-P035 -->
“Well? Did you tell him?” Grushenka demanded eagerly.

<!-- B11-C01-P036 -->
“He listened and said nothing. He said he’d already formed a definite opinion. But he promised to take what I said into consideration.”

<!-- B11-C01-P037 -->
“What does he mean, into consideration? Oh, the rogues! They’ll destroy him! And the doctor—why did she send for the doctor?”

<!-- B11-C01-P038 -->
“As an expert. They want to establish that my brother is mad and killed him in a fit of madness, without knowing what he was doing,” Alyosha said with a quiet smile. “Only my brother won’t agree to that.”

<!-- B11-C01-P039 -->
“Oh, but it would be true, if he’d killed him!” Grushenka exclaimed. “He was out of his mind then, quite out of his mind, and it was me, me, the vile creature, who was to blame! Only he didn’t kill him, he didn’t! And they all say he did, the whole town. Even Fenya’s testimony makes it look as though he killed him. And in the shop, and that official, and what they heard earlier in the tavern! Everyone, everyone’s against him, shouting him down.”

<!-- B11-C01-P040 -->
“Yes, an awful lot of testimony has accumulated,” Alyosha observed darkly.

<!-- B11-C01-P041 -->
“And Grigory, Grigory Vasilyich, he still insists the door was unlocked, keeps hammering away that he saw it—you can’t shake him. I went over there and spoke to him myself. And he even scolded me!”

<!-- B11-C01-P042 -->
“Yes, that may be the strongest evidence against my brother,” Alyosha said.

<!-- B11-C01-P043 -->
“And as for Mitya being mad, he’s just the same now,” Grushenka suddenly began, looking peculiarly worried and secretive. “You know, Alyoshenka, I’ve been meaning to tell you for a long time: I go to him every day, and I simply don’t know what to make of him. Tell me, what do you think: what’s all this he’s started talking about? He talks and talks, and I can’t understand a thing. I think it must be something clever—I’m stupid, I tell myself, I can’t understand it. Only then he suddenly started talking to me about a babe, some little child: ‘Why is the babe so poor?’ he says. ‘It’s for that babe I’m going to Siberia now. I didn’t kill him, but I have to go to Siberia!’ What does it mean, what babe? I didn’t understand a bit of it. I just started crying as he spoke, because he said it all so beautifully; he was crying himself, and I cried too, and suddenly he kissed me and made the sign of the cross over me. What does it mean, Alyosha? Tell me, what’s this ‘babe’?”

<!-- B11-C01-P044 -->
“Rakitin has taken to visiting him for some reason,” Alyosha said with a smile. “Though… this isn’t from Rakitin. I didn’t see him yesterday. I’ll go today.”

<!-- B11-C01-P045 -->
“No, it isn’t Rakitka. It’s his brother Ivan Fyodorovich who’s unsettling him. He goes to see him, that’s what…” Grushenka said, and suddenly seemed to catch herself. Alyosha stared at her in astonishment.

<!-- B11-C01-P046 -->
“What do you mean, goes to see him? Has he been there? Mitya himself told me Ivan had never once come!”

<!-- B11-C01-P047 -->
“Well… well, there I go! I’ve let it out!” Grushenka exclaimed in confusion, suddenly flushing all over. “Wait, Alyosha, don’t say anything. Very well, since I’ve let it out, I’ll tell you the whole truth: he’s been there twice. The first time was just after he arrived—he rushed here from Moscow straightaway then, before I’d even taken to my bed—and the second was a week ago. He told Mitya not to tell you, absolutely forbade him, and not to tell anyone else either. He came secretly.”

<!-- B11-C01-P048 -->
Alyosha sat deep in thought, trying to work something out. The news had plainly startled him.

<!-- B11-C01-P049 -->
“My brother Ivan doesn’t talk to me about Mitya’s case,” he said slowly. “In fact, he’s hardly talked to me at all these past two months, and whenever I went to see him he was always displeased that I’d come, so I haven’t been to see him for three weeks now. Hmm… If he was there a week ago, then… there really has been some change in Mitya over the past week…”

<!-- B11-C01-P050 -->
“A change, a change!” Grushenka took him up eagerly. “They have a secret, they had a secret! Mitya told me himself it was a secret, and you know, it’s such a secret he can’t find any peace. Before that he was cheerful—and he’s cheerful now too, only, you know, when he starts shaking his head like that and pacing the room, and tugging at his hair here on his temple with this finger of his right hand, then I know something’s troubling his soul… I know him now!… Before, he was cheerful; and he’s cheerful today too!”

<!-- B11-C01-P051 -->
“But you said he was irritable?”

<!-- B11-C01-P052 -->
“He is irritable, and cheerful too. He keeps getting irritable, but just for a minute, then he’s cheerful, then suddenly irritable again. And you know, Alyosha, I keep marveling at him: with such a terrible thing ahead, sometimes he laughs at the merest trifle, like a child himself.”

<!-- B11-C01-P053 -->
“And is it true he told you not to tell me about Ivan? Did he actually say, ‘Don’t tell him’?”

<!-- B11-C01-P054 -->
“He said exactly that: ‘Don’t tell him.’ You’re the one Mitya’s afraid of most. Because there’s a secret; he told me himself there’s a secret… Alyosha, dear, go and find out what this secret of theirs is, and come and tell me,” Grushenka suddenly cried, pleading with him. “Put me out of my misery, let me know my cursed fate! That’s why I sent for you.”

<!-- B11-C01-P055 -->
“You think it’s something about you? But then he wouldn’t have mentioned the secret in front of you.”

<!-- B11-C01-P056 -->
“I don’t know. Perhaps he wants to tell me, but doesn’t dare. He’s warning me. There’s a secret, he says, but he won’t say what it is.”

<!-- B11-C01-P057 -->
“What do you think it is yourself?”

<!-- B11-C01-P058 -->
“What do I think? That it’s all over for me, that’s what. The three of them have arranged my ruin, because Katka’s in it. It’s all Katka, she’s behind it. ‘She’s so wonderful’—which means I’m not. He’s telling me in advance, warning me in advance. He’s planning to leave me, that’s the whole secret! The three of them thought it up together: Mitka, Katka, and Ivan Fyodorovich. Alyosha, I’ve been meaning to ask you: a week ago he suddenly told me Ivan was in love with Katka, because he goes to see her so often. Was he telling me the truth or not? Tell me honestly, cut me to the heart.”

<!-- B11-C01-P059 -->
“I won’t lie to you. Ivan isn’t in love with Katerina Ivanovna, in my opinion.”

<!-- B11-C01-P060 -->
“Well, that’s just what I thought then! He’s lying to me, the shameless man, that’s what! And he’s acted jealous now so he can put the blame on me later. He’s a fool, he doesn’t know how to cover his tracks, he’s so open about everything… But I’ll show him, I’ll show him! ‘You believe I killed him,’ he says—to me, he says that, to me, that’s what he reproaches me with! God be with him! Just wait, that Katka will get it from me at the trial! I’ll have a word to say there… I’ll tell them everything!”

<!-- B11-C01-P061 -->
And again she wept bitterly.

<!-- B11-C01-P062 -->
“Here’s what I can tell you for certain, Grushenka,” Alyosha said, getting up. “First, he loves you, loves you more than anyone in the world, and only you. Believe me about that. I know. I really do know. Second, I won’t try to worm his secret out of him, but if he tells me of his own accord today, I’ll tell him plainly that I’ve promised to tell you. Then I’ll come to you today and tell you. Only… it seems to me… Katerina Ivanovna has nothing to do with it; this secret is about something else. I’m sure it is. It doesn’t sound at all as though it concerns Katerina Ivanovna, to my mind. Goodbye for now!”

<!-- B11-C01-P063 -->
Alyosha pressed her hand. Grushenka was still crying. He saw that she had put very little faith in his consolations, but even the relief of speaking out and venting her grief had done her good. He was sorry to leave her in this state, but he was in a hurry. There was still much for him to do.
