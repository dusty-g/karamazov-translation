# Chapter II — He Got His First Son Off His Hands

*Part I · Book I: The Story of One Little Family*

<!-- B01-C02-P001 -->
Of course, one can imagine what kind of father and caregiver such a man would make. As a father, he behaved precisely as one might have expected: he utterly and completely abandoned his child by Adelaida Ivanovna, not out of malice toward the boy or any wounded feelings as a husband, but simply because he forgot about him completely.

While he was pestering everyone with his tears and complaints and turning his house into a den of debauchery, Mitya, the three-year-old boy, was taken into the care of Grigory, the household's faithful servant; had he not looked after him then, there might have been no one to change the child's little shirt. As it happened, the child's mother's family, too, seemed to forget about him at first. His grandfather—that is, Mr. Miusov himself, Adelaida Ivanovna's father—was already dead; his widowed wife, Mitya's grandmother, had moved to Moscow and become very ill, while the sisters had married, so Mitya had to spend almost a whole year with the servant Grigory, living in his cottage in the yard. Besides, even if Papa had remembered him (he could hardly have been unaware of his existence, after all), he himself would have banished him back to the cottage, since the child would only have interfered with his carousing.

But it so happened that the late Adelaida Ivanovna's first cousin, Pyotr Alexandrovich Miusov, returned from Paris. He was later to spend many years abroad without a break, but at the time he was still a very young man, though a distinctive figure among the Miusovs: enlightened, metropolitan, accustomed to life abroad, and, moreover, a European all his life, and toward the end of it a liberal of the forties and fifties. In the course of his career he had formed connections with many of the most liberal people of his age, both in Russia and abroad; he had known Proudhon and Bakunin personally[^b01-c02-n001] and, toward the end of his travels, especially liked to recall and recount the three days of the February Revolution in Paris in 1848, hinting that perhaps he himself had been among the participants on the barricades. It was one of the most delightful memories of his youth.

He had an independent fortune, amounting, by the old reckoning, to about a thousand souls.[^b01-c02-n003] His splendid estate lay just outside our little town and bordered the lands of our famous monastery, with which Pyotr Alexandrovich, while still very young, the moment he received his inheritance, had promptly begun an interminable lawsuit over some fishing rights in the river or timber-cutting rights in the forest—I don't know for certain—but he actually regarded bringing a lawsuit against the “clericals” as his duty as an enlightened citizen.

When he heard the whole story about Adelaida Ivanovna, whom he naturally remembered and had even noticed at one time, and learned that Mitya had been left behind, he involved himself in the affair despite all his youthful indignation and contempt for Fyodor Pavlovich. It was then that he first made Fyodor Pavlovich's acquaintance. He told him outright that he would like to take charge of the child's upbringing. For a long time afterward, as an illustration of the man's character, he would tell how, when he began speaking to Fyodor Pavlovich about Mitya, the latter for a while looked as though he had absolutely no idea what child he was talking about, and even seemed surprised that he had a little son somewhere in the house. There may have been some exaggeration in Pyotr Alexandrovich's account, but there must still have been something like the truth in it.

And indeed, all his life Fyodor Pavlovich liked to put on an act, suddenly to play out some unexpected role before you, and, above all, sometimes for no reason whatsoever, even to his own direct disadvantage, as in this instance, for example. This trait, however, is shared by a great many people, even very intelligent ones, let alone Fyodor Pavlovich.

Pyotr Alexandrovich took up the matter with fervor and was even appointed the child's guardian (jointly with Fyodor Pavlovich), since, after all, his mother had left a little property—a house and an estate. Mitya did move in with this cousin of his mother's, but the man had no family of his own. As soon as he had arranged and secured the payments due him from his estates, he immediately hurried back to Paris for another long stay, so he entrusted the child to one of his parents’ cousins, a Moscow lady. As it happened, once settled in Paris, he too forgot about the child, especially when that same February Revolution came along, which so struck his imagination and which he could never forget for the rest of his life. The Moscow lady died, and Mitya passed into the care of one of her married daughters. I believe he later changed nests yet again, for a fourth time.

I will not go into all that now, especially since I shall have a great deal more to tell about this firstborn son of Fyodor Pavlovich's; for the present I will confine myself to the most essential facts about him, without which I cannot even begin my novel.

First of all, this Dmitri Fyodorovich was the only one of Fyodor Pavlovich's three sons to grow up believing that he did, after all, have a certain fortune and would be independent once he came of age. His youth and early manhood passed in disorder: he never finished at the gymnasium, then got into a military school, then found himself in the Caucasus, worked his way up, fought a duel, was reduced to the ranks, worked his way up again, did a great deal of wild living, and spent a fair amount of money, relatively speaking. He only began receiving it from Fyodor Pavlovich when he came of age, and by then he had run up debts.

It was only after coming of age that he first saw and got to know his father, Fyodor Pavlovich, when he came to our part of the country expressly to have matters out with him about his property. It seems he did not like his parent even then; he stayed with him only a short time and left as quickly as he could, having managed only to get a certain sum out of him and enter into some arrangement with him about future payments of income from the estate, though on that occasion he never did manage to get Fyodor Pavlovich to tell him either how much it yielded or what it was worth—a noteworthy fact.

Fyodor Pavlovich noticed then, at that very first meeting (and this must be remembered), that Mitya's idea of his fortune was exaggerated and mistaken. Fyodor Pavlovich was very pleased about this, with certain calculations of his own in mind. He concluded only that the young man was frivolous, unruly, full of passions, impatient, a carouser, and that all he needed was to get hold of a little something to tide him over, and he would calm down at once, if only for a short time, of course. And this was what Fyodor Pavlovich began to exploit: he put him off with small handouts, occasional remittances.

In the end it so happened that when, some four years later, Mitya lost patience and appeared in our little town for a second time, to settle matters with his parent once and for all, it suddenly emerged, to his utter astonishment, that he had precisely nothing left, that it was hard even to add it all up, that he had already received from Fyodor Pavlovich, in money, the entire value of his property, and perhaps even owed him money himself; that under such-and-such agreements, which he himself had wished to enter into at such-and-such times, he had no right to demand anything further, and so on, and so on. The young man was stunned, suspected falsehood, fraud, almost lost control of himself, and seemed to lose his mind.

It was this very circumstance that led to the catastrophe whose story will form the subject of my first, introductory novel—or, rather, the outward side of it. But before I turn to this novel, I must also tell you about Fyodor Pavlovich's other two sons, Mitya's brothers, and explain where those two came from.

[^b01-c02-n001]: Pierre-Joseph Proudhon (1809–1865) was a French socialist thinker and political writer; Mikhail Bakunin (1814–1876) was a Russian revolutionary and a formative anarchist.

[^b01-c02-n003]: “Souls” counts the male serfs recorded in the imperial tax census, rather than the estate’s entire population.
