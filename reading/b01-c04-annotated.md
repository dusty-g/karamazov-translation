# Chapter IV — The Third Son, Alyosha

*Part I · Book I: The Story of One Little Family*

<!-- B01-C04-P001 -->

He was only twenty then (his brother Ivan was twenty-three, and their eldest brother, Dmitri,
twenty-seven). Let me say first of all that this young man, Alyosha, was no fanatic, and, in my
opinion at least, no mystic either. I will give you my whole view at the outset: he was simply
someone whose love of humanity had come early, and if he had thrown himself into monastic life, it
was only because at that time this path alone had caught his imagination and offered his soul,
struggling out of the darkness of worldly spite toward the light of love, what seemed, so to speak,
the ideal path of escape. And this path had caught his imagination only because on it he had encountered
someone he considered extraordinary—our celebrated monastery elder, Zosima—to whom he had attached
himself with all the ardent first love of his insatiable heart. Still, I do not deny that he was
already very strange then, and had been, indeed, from the cradle. As I have mentioned, though he
lost his mother when he was only three, he remembered her all his life afterward, her face, her
caresses, “just as though she were standing here before me, alive.” Such memories can remain with us
from an even earlier age, even from the age of two—as everyone knows—but all through life they
emerge only as points of light out of the darkness, like a corner torn from an enormous picture that
has faded and vanished except for that one small corner. That was exactly how it was with him: he
remembered one evening, a quiet summer evening, an open window, the slanting rays of the setting sun
(it was those slanting rays he remembered best), an icon in the corner of the room, a little lamp
burning before it, and before the icon his mother on her knees, sobbing as though in hysterics, with
shrill cries and screams, clutching him in both arms, hugging him so tightly it hurt, praying to the
Mother of God for him, holding him out from her embrace with both hands toward the icon, as though
entrusting him to the Mother of God’s protection… and suddenly the nurse runs in and tears him away
from her in fright. There was a picture! Alyosha remembered his mother’s face at that moment too: he
said it was frenzied but beautiful, as far as he could recall. But he seldom cared to share this
memory with anyone. As a child and a boy he was not demonstrative, not even very talkative, though
not from mistrust, timidity, or sullen dislike of company—quite the opposite—but from something
else, from what seemed to be some inward concern, entirely his own, having nothing to do with anyone
else, but so important to him that it seemed to make him forget other people. Yet he loved people:
he seemed to live his whole life with complete faith in them, and still no one ever took him for a
simpleton or thought him naive. There was something about him that said, that made people feel—and
did so all his life afterward—that he had no wish to sit in judgment on them, that he would not take
it upon himself to condemn them and would never condemn them for anything. He even seemed to accept
everything without the least condemnation, though often with very bitter sorrow. More than that, in
this respect he had reached the point where no one could astonish or frighten him, even in his
earliest youth. When he came to his father’s at nineteen, into an outright den of filthy debauchery,
he, chaste and pure, would simply withdraw in silence when the sight became unbearable, without the
slightest show of contempt or condemnation for anyone. His father, who had once lived by sponging on
others and was therefore acutely sensitive to slights, had greeted him at first with gloomy
suspicion (“says so little, must be thinking a great deal to himself”); but before long, within a
couple of weeks at most, he had taken to embracing and kissing him at every turn—with drunken tears,
it is true, in a fit of tipsy tenderness, but plainly with a sincere and deep love for him, such as
a man like him had surely never managed to feel for anyone before…

And everyone loved this young man, wherever he appeared, even from his earliest childhood. When he
entered the home of his benefactor and guardian, Yefim Petrovich Polenov, the whole family grew so
attached to him that they truly regarded him as their own child. Yet he had come into that house at
an age when it would have been impossible to expect a child to show calculating cunning, slyness, or
any skill at ingratiating himself and giving pleasure, any knack for making people love him. So this
gift of inspiring a special love belonged, so to speak, to his very nature, artlessly and
spontaneously. The same thing happened at school, although he might have seemed just the sort of
child to arouse his schoolmates’ mistrust, sometimes their mockery, perhaps even their hatred. He
would fall into thought, for instance, and seem to withdraw from them. From childhood he liked to go
off into a corner and read books; and yet his schoolmates grew so fond of him that he could fairly
be called everyone’s favorite throughout his schooldays. He was seldom lively, seldom even merry,
but anyone looking at him could see at once that this did not come from any gloominess in him: on
the contrary, he was serene and untroubled. He never wanted to show off among boys his own age.
Perhaps that was just why he was never afraid of anyone, while the boys immediately understood that
he took no pride in his fearlessness; he looked at them as though he did not even realize he was
brave and unafraid. He never held a grudge. An hour after someone had hurt him, he might answer that
person, or start talking to him himself, with such a trusting, open look that it was as if nothing
had happened between them. Nor did he seem to have forgotten the offense by chance or forgiven it
deliberately: he simply did not count it as an offense, and this utterly charmed and won over the
other boys. There was only one trait in him that throughout the gymnasium, from the lowest forms to
the highest, kept tempting his schoolmates to tease him, not out of malicious mockery but because
they found it fun. This trait was a wild, almost frenzied modesty and chastity. He could not bear to
hear certain words, certain conversations about women. Unfortunately, those “certain” words and
conversations cannot be rooted out of schools. Boys pure in heart and soul, almost children still,
very often like to talk in class, among themselves and even out loud, about things, scenes, and
images that even soldiers will not always discuss; more than that, there is much of this kind that
soldiers do not know or understand, but that is already familiar to the very young children of our
educated society and upper classes. Perhaps there is no moral corruption in it yet, no real, inward,
corrupt cynicism either, but there is an outward cynicism, and that is often thought positively
refined, sophisticated, dashing, and worthy of imitation. When they saw that “Alyoshka Karamazov”
quickly stopped his ears with his fingers whenever they began talking “about that,” they would
sometimes deliberately crowd around him and, forcing his hands away from his ears, shout obscenities
into both of them, while he struggled free, sank to the floor, lay down and covered himself, all
without saying a word to them, without abusing them, bearing the insult in silence. In the end,
though, they left him alone and stopped calling him a “girl”; indeed, in this respect they came to
look on him with pity. Incidentally, he was always among the best students in his class, but never
ranked first.

After Yefim Petrovich died, Alyosha spent another two years at the provincial gymnasium. Almost
immediately after her husband’s death, Yefim Petrovich’s inconsolable wife went to Italy for a long
stay with the entire family, all of them female, and Alyosha came to live in the house of two ladies
he had never so much as seen before, some distant relatives of Yefim Petrovich’s; on what terms, he
himself did not know. It was also a characteristic trait of his, a very characteristic one, that he
never worried about whose money he was living on. In this he was the exact opposite of his older
brother Ivan Fyodorovich, who had struggled through his first two years at the university supporting
himself by his own work, and who had felt bitterly from childhood that he was eating a benefactor’s
bread. But this strange trait in Alexei’s character could not, it seemed, be judged too harshly,
because anyone who knew him even slightly, once the question arose, immediately felt sure that
Alexei was one of those young men who are, as it were, something like holy fools:[^b01-c04-n001] if
a whole fortune should suddenly fall into his hands, he would have no trouble giving it away at the very first
request, for a good cause or perhaps simply to some clever rogue who asked him for it. In general,
too, he seemed to have no idea of the value of money—not in the literal sense, of course. When he
was given spending money, which he never asked for himself, he would either spend whole weeks not
knowing what to do with it or be terribly careless with it, and it would vanish in an instant. Pyotr
Alexandrovich Miusov, a man very scrupulous about money and bourgeois honesty, later studied Alexei
and once pronounced the following aphorism about him: “Here may be the only man in the world whom
you could suddenly leave alone and penniless in a square in an unfamiliar city of a million people,
and he would never come to grief or die of hunger and cold, because someone would feed him at once,
someone would find him a place at once, and if no one found him a place he would find one himself at
once, and it would cost him no effort and no humiliation, and the person who took him in would find
it no burden—might, on the contrary, consider it a pleasure.”

He did not finish at the gymnasium. He still had a full year left when he suddenly announced to his
ladies that he was going to see his father about something that had come into his head. They were
very sorry for him and at first did not want to let him go. The fare was very cheap, and the ladies
would not let him pawn his watch—a gift from his benefactor’s family before they went abroad—but
provided him lavishly with money, and even new clothes and linen. He gave half the money back,
however, declaring that he insisted on traveling third class. When he arrived in our little town and
his father first asked, “What exactly have you come for, without finishing school?” he gave no
direct answer and, people say, seemed unusually absorbed in thought. It soon emerged that he was
looking for his mother’s grave. He even admitted at the time that this was the only reason he had
come. But that can hardly have been the whole reason for his arrival. Most likely he himself did not
know then, and could not possibly have explained, just what it was that seemed suddenly to have
risen from his soul and drawn him irresistibly onto some new, unknown, but now unavoidable path.
Fyodor Pavlovich could not show him where he had buried his second wife, because he had never been
to her grave since the coffin was covered with earth, and after so many years had quite forgotten
where they had buried her…

Speaking of Fyodor Pavlovich: he had lived away from our town for a long time before this. Three or
four years after his second wife’s death he went to southern Russia and eventually ended up in
Odessa, where he stayed for several years in succession. At first he made the acquaintance, in his
own words, “of all sorts of kikes, little kikes, kikelets, and kike brats,” and in the end he
was received not only by kikes but “even by Jews.” It must have been during this period of his life
that he developed his special talent for scraping money together and squeezing it out of people. He
returned to settle in our little town only about three years before Alyosha’s arrival. His former
acquaintances found him terribly aged, though he was still by no means an old man. His manner was
not more dignified, but somehow more brazen. The former buffoon had developed, for example, an
insolent need to dress others up as buffoons. His taste for lewd antics with women had not stayed as
it was: it seemed to have grown still more disgusting. He soon founded many new taverns throughout
the district. By all appearances he had perhaps as much as a hundred thousand rubles, or only a
little less. Many of the town’s and district’s inhabitants promptly fell into his debt, against the
soundest collateral, of course. Just lately he had grown somehow bloated, had begun somehow to lose
his steadiness and his awareness of what he was doing, had even fallen into a kind of frivolity: he
would begin one thing and end with another, scatter his energies, and get drunk more and more often.
Had it not been for that same servant Grigory, who had also aged considerably by then and sometimes
looked after him almost like a tutor, Fyodor Pavlovich might not have got through life without
serious trouble. Alyosha’s arrival seemed to affect him even morally, as though something long
dormant in his soul had awakened in this prematurely old man. “Do you know,” he began saying often
to Alyosha, looking closely at him, “you look like her, like the klikusha?” This was what he called
his dead wife, Alyosha’s mother. It was the servant Grigory who finally showed Alyosha the
“klikusha’s” grave. He took him to our town cemetery and there, in a far corner, pointed out an
inexpensive but neat cast-iron slab, which even bore an inscription giving the dead woman’s name,
social rank, age, and year of death; beneath it was engraved something like a quatrain from the old
cemetery verses commonly found on the graves of ordinary people. Surprisingly, this slab turned out
to be Grigory’s doing. He had put it over the poor “klikusha’s” grave himself, at his own expense,
after Fyodor Pavlovich—whom he had already pestered many times with reminders about the
grave—finally left for Odessa, washing his hands not only of graves but of all his memories. Alyosha
showed no particular emotion at his mother’s grave; he merely listened to Grigory’s solemn, sensible
account of erecting the slab, stood for a while with his head bowed, and went away without a word.
After that he did not visit the cemetery again, perhaps for the entire year. But this small episode
had its effect on Fyodor Pavlovich too, and a very original effect it was. He suddenly took a
thousand rubles to our monastery for prayers for the soul of his wife—not his second wife, not
Alyosha’s mother, not the “klikusha,” but his first wife, Adelaida Ivanovna, who used to beat him.
That evening he got drunk and railed at the monks to Alyosha. He himself was far from a religious
man; perhaps he had never in his life set even a five-kopeck candle before an icon. Such people have
strange impulses of sudden feeling and sudden thought.

I have already said that he had grown very bloated. By then there was something in his face that
testified starkly to the character and essence of the whole life he had lived. Besides the long,
fleshy bags beneath his small eyes, forever insolent, suspicious, and mocking, besides the many deep
wrinkles in his small but fat face, a large Adam’s apple hung beneath his pointed chin, fleshy and
elongated like a purse, giving him a disgustingly lustful look. Add to this a long, ravenous mouth
with plump lips, beneath which showed little stumps of black, nearly rotted teeth. He sprayed saliva
whenever he began to speak. He liked to make jokes about his face himself, though he seemed pleased
with it. He drew particular attention to his nose, not very large but very thin, with a pronounced
hump: “A true Roman nose,” he would say. “Put it together with the Adam’s apple and you have the
very face of an ancient Roman patrician in the age of decadence.” He seemed proud of this.

And so, fairly soon after finding his mother’s grave, Alyosha suddenly announced to him that he
wanted to enter the monastery and that the monks were willing to accept him as a novice. He
explained that this was his most fervent wish and that he was formally asking his permission as his
father. The old man already knew that the elder Zosima, who was working out his salvation in the
monastery’s hermitage, had made a particular impression on his “quiet boy.”

<!-- B01-C04-P008 -->

“That elder is certainly the most honest monk they have,” he said, having listened to Alyosha
silently and thoughtfully, though with almost no surprise at his request. “Hm, so that’s where you
want to go, my quiet boy!” He was half drunk, and suddenly his long mouth spread into a half-drunken
smile, not without cunning and drunken slyness. “Hm, I had a feeling you’d end up doing something
like this, can you imagine? That’s just where you were heading. Well then, why not? You’ve got your
own two little thousand rubles—there’s your dowry—and I’ll never abandon you, my angel; I’ll
pay whatever’s required for you even now, if they ask. And if they don’t ask, why should we press it
on them, eh? You spend money like a canary, two little seeds a week… Hm. You know, there’s a
monastery with a settlement on the edge of town, and everyone there knows that the only people
living in it are ‘monastery wives,’ that’s what they call them there, thirty wives or so, I should
think… I’ve been there, and you know, it’s interesting, in its way, of course, for a change of
scene. The only trouble is, it’s terribly Russian, not a single Frenchwoman yet, and there could
be—the means are ample. Once word gets out, they’ll come. But here it’s all right, no monastery
wives here, and some two hundred monks. Respectable. Men who fast. I admit it… Hm. So
you want to go to the monks? I’m sorry to lose you, though, Alyosha, truly, believe me, I’ve grown
to love you… Still, here’s a convenient opportunity: you can pray for us sinners, we’ve done far too
much sinning, sitting here. I’ve kept wondering: who will ever pray for me? Is there anyone in the
world? My dear boy, I’m terribly stupid about these things, perhaps you don’t believe me? Terribly.
You see, stupid as I am, I keep thinking about it, keep thinking, now and then, of course, not all
the time. Surely, I think, the devils can’t forget to drag me down to them with hooks when I die.
And then I think: hooks? Where do they get them? What are they made of? Iron? Where do they forge
them? Have they got some sort of factory down there? Now the monks at the monastery probably think
there’s a ceiling in hell, for instance. I’m willing to believe in hell myself, only without a
ceiling; that seems more refined, more enlightened, Lutheran, you might say. But really, what
difference does it make, with a ceiling or without? That’s where the damned question lies! Well, if
there’s no ceiling, that means there are no hooks. And if there are no hooks, then to hell with the
whole thing, which means it’s unbelievable again: who’s going to drag me down with hooks then?
Because if they don’t drag even me down, what will happen then, where’s the justice in the world? Il
faudrait les inventer,[^b01-c04-n002] those hooks, specially for me, for me alone, because if you only knew,
Alyosha, what a filthy wretch I am!…”

“Yes, there are no hooks there,” Alyosha said quietly and seriously, looking closely at his father.

<!-- B01-C04-P010 -->

“Yes, yes, only the shadows of hooks. I know, I know. It’s like some Frenchman’s description of hell:
‘J'ai vu l'ombre d'un cocher, qui avec l'ombre d'une brosse frottait l'ombre d'un carrosse.’[^b01-c04-n003] How do
you know there are no hooks, my dear? Spend some time with the monks and you’ll sing a different
tune. Still, go, get to the truth there, and come back and tell me: it’ll be easier to go to the
next world if you know for certain what’s there. And it will suit you better with the monks than
with me, a drunken old geezer, and the girls… though nothing can touch you—you’re like an angel.
Well, let’s hope nothing touches you there either; that’s why I’m letting
you go, because I’m counting on that last hope. The devil hasn’t eaten your wits. You’ll burn, then
go out; you’ll be cured and come back. And I’ll be waiting for you: I can feel that
you’re the only person on earth who hasn’t condemned me, my dear boy, I can feel it, how could I
help feeling it!…”

He even began to snivel. He was sentimental. He was evil and sentimental.

[^b01-c04-n002]: French: “They would have to be invented.” An adaptation of Voltaire’s “If God did not exist, he would have to be invented.”
