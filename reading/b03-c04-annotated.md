# Book III: The Lustful
## IV. Confession of a Passionate Heart: In Anecdotes

<!-- B03-C04-P001 -->
“I went on sprees there. Earlier today Father said I paid thousands to seduce young girls. That’s a filthy fantasy; nothing of the kind ever happened, and what did happen didn’t require money for ‘that’ itself. With me, money is an accessory, the heat of the soul, the setting. Today it’s a lady I’m with; tomorrow a girl from the street takes her place. I entertain them both, throw money around by the handful: music, uproar, Gypsy women. If need be, I give her money too, because they take it, take it eagerly, you have to admit that, and they’re pleased, and they’re grateful. Fine ladies liked me—not all of them, but it happened, it happened; still, I always loved the little lanes, the quiet, dark back alleys beyond the square: that’s where the adventures are, the surprises, the nuggets of gold in the mud. I’m speaking allegorically, brother. Our little town had no actual alleys like that, but it had moral ones. If you were what I am, you’d understand what those mean. I loved debauchery, and I loved the shame of debauchery. I loved cruelty: aren’t I a bedbug, a vicious insect? A Karamazov—that says it all! Once the whole town went out on a picnic in seven troikas. It was winter, and in the darkness of the sleigh I began squeezing the hand of a girl beside me and forced the girl to kiss me—a civil servant’s daughter, poor, sweet, gentle, defenseless. She let me, let me do a great deal in the dark. The poor thing thought I’d come to her the next day and propose—being an eligible husband was my chief attraction, after all; but afterward I never said a word to her, not half a word for five months. I saw her eyes following me from a corner of the ballroom when people were dancing—and they were always dancing in our town. I saw the little flame in them, the flame of gentle indignation. That game amused nothing but the insect’s lust I was nurturing inside me. Five months later she married a civil servant and went away… angry, and perhaps still in love. They live happily now. Notice that I told no one, didn’t ruin her reputation; my desires may be base, and I may love baseness, but I’m not dishonorable. You’re blushing, your eyes flashed. You’ve had enough of this filth. And all this was only a beginning, little flowers out of Paul de Kock, though the cruel insect was already growing, already spreading through my soul. There’s a whole album of memories there, brother. God grant them good health, the dear creatures. I liked to part without quarreling. And I never betrayed them, never ruined a single one’s reputation. But enough. Did you really think I called you over here just for that rubbish? No, I’ll tell you something more interesting; only don’t be surprised that I’m not ashamed before you, that I even seem glad.”

<!-- B03-C04-P002 -->
“You’re saying that because I blushed,” Alyosha suddenly observed. “I didn’t blush at what you said or at what you’ve done. I blushed because I’m just the same as you.”

<!-- B03-C04-P003 -->
“You? Now you’ve gone a little too far.”

<!-- B03-C04-P004 -->
“No, I haven’t,” Alyosha said fervently. (Clearly, the thought had been with him for a long time.) “They’re all steps on the same staircase. I’m on the lowest, and you’re up above, somewhere on the thirteenth. That’s how I see it, but it’s all the same thing, entirely the same kind of thing. Whoever sets foot on the bottom step is bound to reach the top one too.”

<!-- B03-C04-P005 -->
“So you shouldn’t set foot on it at all?”

<!-- B03-C04-P006 -->
“If you can keep off it, you should.”

<!-- B03-C04-P007 -->
“And can you?”

<!-- B03-C04-P008 -->
“I don’t think so.”

<!-- B03-C04-P009 -->
“Don’t, Alyosha, don’t, my dear. I feel like kissing your hand, you move me so. That rogue Grushenka knows people. She once told me she’d eat you up one of these days. I’ll stop, I’ll stop! Let’s leave these vile things, this field fouled by flies, and come to my tragedy—another field fouled by flies, meaning every kind of baseness. The thing is, although the old fellow lied about my seducing innocents, in essence that’s what did happen in my tragedy, though only once, and even then it didn’t come off. The old man who reproached me with that made-up story doesn’t know about this affair. I’ve never told anyone; you’re the first I’ll tell—except Ivan, of course. Ivan knows everything. He’s known for a long time, long before you. But Ivan’s a grave.”

<!-- B03-C04-P010 -->
“Ivan’s a grave?”

<!-- B03-C04-P011 -->
“Yes.”

<!-- B03-C04-P012 -->
Alyosha listened with the closest attention.

<!-- B03-C04-P013 -->
“Well, I was serving as an ensign in that battalion, a line battalion, but all the same I was more or less under supervision, like some sort of exile. The little town received me wonderfully. I threw a lot of money around; they believed I was rich, and I believed it myself. Still, I must have pleased them in some other way too. They shook their heads, but really, they liked me. My lieutenant colonel, already an old man, took an instant dislike to me. He kept finding fault, but I had someone to back me, and besides, the whole town stood up for me; he couldn’t go too far. I was to blame myself: I deliberately failed to show him proper respect. I was proud. This stubborn old man, a very decent fellow and the kindest, most hospitable of hosts, had been married twice; both wives had died. The first came from common people and left him a daughter of the same common background. By the time I knew her she was an unmarried woman of about twenty-four and lived with her father and her aunt, her dead mother’s sister. The aunt was meek simplicity itself, while the niece, the lieutenant colonel’s elder daughter, was a lively simple soul. I like to put in a good word when I remember someone: my dear fellow, I never knew a woman with a more delightful disposition than that girl. Her name was Agafya, imagine—Agafya Ivanovna. And she wasn’t bad-looking either, in the Russian style: tall, sturdy, full-bodied, with beautiful eyes, though her face was a little coarse. She hadn’t married, though she’d had two proposals; she’d turned them down and kept her spirits. We grew close—not in that way, no, there was nothing of that in it; just friends. I’ve often been friends with women quite innocently. The things I’d tell her, so frankly—whew! And she’d only laugh. Many women like frankness, remember that, and she was unmarried too, which amused me greatly. And another thing: you could never have called her a young lady. She and her aunt lived in her father’s house and seemed to accept a lower place of their own accord, without claiming to be on a level with the rest of society. Everyone liked her and needed her, because she was a splendid dressmaker: she had a talent. She didn’t ask to be paid, did it as a kindness, though when people gave her presents she didn’t refuse them. But the lieutenant colonel—he was another matter! The lieutenant colonel was one of the foremost people in the place. He lived in style, entertained the whole town: dinners, dances. When I arrived and joined the battalion, word went all round town that the lieutenant colonel’s second daughter would soon be gracing us with a visit from the capital, a beauty among beauties, who’d just finished at one of the aristocratic girls’ institutes there. This second daughter is Katerina Ivanovna herself, his daughter by his second wife. And that second wife, now dead, had come from a distinguished family, the family of some important general, though I know for a fact that she hadn’t brought the lieutenant colonel any money either. So she had family connections and that was all, perhaps some expectations, but nothing in hand. Yet when the institute girl arrived—for a visit, not for good—our whole little town seemed transformed. Our grandest ladies, two wives of excellencies and a colonel’s wife, and then everyone, everyone after them, took her up at once, fought over her, set about entertaining her. Queen of the balls, queen of the picnics; they put together some tableaux vivants for the benefit of some governesses. I kept quiet, kept carousing; just then I’d pulled a stunt that had set the whole town talking. Once I saw her look me up and down—it was at the battery commander’s—but I didn’t go up to her: as if to say I couldn’t be bothered to make her acquaintance. Some time later I did approach her, at another party, and spoke to her. She barely looked at me, pursed her lips in contempt, and I thought: just you wait, I’ll get my own back! Most of the time back then I was an appalling military lout, and I knew it myself. Above all, I sensed that ‘Katenka’ wasn’t merely some innocent institute girl. She was a woman of character, proud and truly virtuous, and most of all she had brains and education, and I had neither. Do you think I wanted to propose? Not a bit of it. I simply wanted revenge because I was such a fine fellow and she didn’t appreciate it. Meanwhile, more carousing and smashing things up. At last the lieutenant colonel put me under arrest for three days. Right about then Father sent me six thousand, after I’d sent him a formal renunciation of any and every claim: we were ‘all square,’ so to speak, and I’d demand nothing more. I understood nothing then. Brother, right up to my arrival here, even up to these last few days, perhaps even up to today, I understood nothing about all these money disputes between Father and me. But to hell with that, it can wait. Back then, after I’d received the six thousand, I suddenly learned something from a friend’s letter, something definite and of the greatest interest to me: they weren’t pleased with our lieutenant colonel; they suspected irregularities. In a word, his enemies were cooking up something for him. Sure enough, the divisional commander arrived and gave him a merciless dressing-down. A little later he was told to submit his resignation. I won’t go into all the details of how it happened; he really did have enemies. But suddenly the town turned terribly cold toward him and his entire family; everyone seemed to draw away at once. That was when I made my first move. I met Agafya Ivanovna, with whom I’d always kept up my friendship, and said, ‘You know, Papa is four thousand five hundred rubles short of government money.’ ‘What do you mean? Why do you say that? The general was here just recently; it was all there…’ ‘It was then, but it isn’t now.’ She was terribly frightened. ‘Please don’t frighten me. Who told you?’ ‘Don’t worry,’ I said. ‘I won’t tell anyone, and you know I’m a grave when it comes to things like that. There’s just one thing I wanted to add on the subject, “in case of need,” so to speak: when they ask Papa for those four thousand five hundred, and he hasn’t got them, then instead of letting him face a court-martial and end up a private in his old age, you’d do better to send your institute girl to me on the quiet. I’ve just been sent some money. I might let her have the four thousand, and I’ll keep the secret sacred.’ ‘Oh, what a scoundrel you are!’ she said—those were her very words. ‘What a wicked scoundrel! How dare you!’ She went off in a terrible fury, and I called after her once again that the secret would be kept sacred and inviolate. Let me say now that those two women, Agafya and her aunt, proved to be pure angels throughout this whole affair. They positively worshipped her sister, that proud Katya, made themselves humble before her, acted as her maids… Only Agafya went and passed on that little matter, our conversation, to her. I found it all out afterward, knew it like the back of my hand. She didn’t hide it, and of course that was just what I wanted. Suddenly a new major arrived to take over the battalion. He took over. The old lieutenant colonel suddenly fell ill, couldn’t move, stayed home for two days, didn’t hand over the government funds. Our doctor, Kravchenko, insisted he really was ill. But there was something I knew in detail, in secret, and had known for a long time: every time the authorities inspected those funds, the money disappeared afterward for a while. It had been happening for four years running. The lieutenant colonel lent it to a most trustworthy man, a merchant in our town named Trifonov, an old widower with a beard and gold spectacles. He’d go to a fair, turn the money over in whatever business he had to do there, and promptly return the lieutenant colonel’s money in full. He’d bring him a little present from the fair too, and with the presents a little interest. Only this time—I learned all this then quite by chance from Trifonov’s slobbering adolescent son, his son and heir, the most depraved boy the world ever produced—this time, I say, Trifonov came back from the fair and returned nothing. The lieutenant colonel rushed to see him. ‘I’ve never received anything from you, nor could I have,’ was his answer. So there was our lieutenant colonel sitting at home, his head wrapped in a towel, all three women putting ice on the crown of his head, when suddenly an orderly arrived with a register and an order: ‘Hand over the government funds at once, immediately, within two hours.’ He signed for it—I saw that signature in the register afterward—got up, said he was going to put on his uniform, hurried into his bedroom, took his double-barreled hunting gun, loaded it, put in a military bullet, took the boot off his right foot, pressed the gun against his chest and felt for the trigger with his foot. But Agafya already suspected something. She’d remembered what I’d said, crept up and looked in just in time. She burst into the room, threw herself on him from behind and put her arms around him. The gun went off into the ceiling; no one was hurt. The others ran in, seized him, took away the gun, held his arms… I found out every last detail afterward. I was at home then. It was dusk, and I was just about to go out: I’d dressed, combed my hair, scented my handkerchief, picked up my cap, when suddenly the door opened and there, in front of me, in my rooms, stood Katerina Ivanovna.

<!-- B03-C04-P014 -->
“Strange things happen: no one in the street saw her come to me, so the town never heard of it. I rented my rooms from two very old women of the civil-service class who also waited on me, respectful women who did whatever I said. At my command they both kept as quiet afterward as cast-iron posts. Of course I understood everything at once. She came in and looked straight at me, her dark eyes steady, even defiant, but I could see hesitation in her lips and around her mouth.

<!-- B03-C04-P015 -->
“‘My sister told me you would give me four thousand five hundred rubles if I came for them… came to you myself. I’ve come… give me the money!..’ She couldn’t hold out, couldn’t breathe, grew frightened, her voice broke, and the corners of her lips and the lines around her mouth began to tremble. Alyoshka, are you listening or asleep?”

<!-- B03-C04-P016 -->
“Mitya, I know you’ll tell the whole truth,” Alyosha said, deeply moved.

<!-- B03-C04-P017 -->
“That’s exactly what I’ll tell. If it’s the whole truth, this is how it happened; I won’t spare myself. My first thought was a Karamazov thought. Once a solpuga bit me, brother, and I lay in a fever for two weeks. Well, now I suddenly felt a solpuga bite me in the heart—that vicious insect, do you understand? I looked her up and down. Have you seen her? She’s a beauty. But that wasn’t what made her beautiful then. What made her beautiful at that moment was that she was noble and I was a scoundrel, that she stood in all the grandeur of her generosity and her sacrifice for her father, while I was a bedbug. And now she depended entirely on me, the bedbug and scoundrel, entirely, in every way, body and soul. A circle had been drawn around her. I’ll tell you straight: that thought, the solpuga’s thought, seized my heart so fiercely that it almost melted away with longing alone. You’d think there could no longer be any struggle: I’d simply act like a bedbug, like a vicious tarantula, without a trace of pity… It even took my breath away. Listen: naturally I’d have come the very next day to ask for her hand, to bring the whole thing to the most honorable conclusion, so to speak, and then no one would know or could know about it. Because although I’m a man of base desires, I’m an honorable man. And suddenly, that very second, someone whispered in my ear: ‘But a woman like this won’t even come out to see you tomorrow when you arrive to propose. She’ll tell the coachman to throw you out of the yard. Go on, she’ll say, disgrace me all over town; I’m not afraid of you!’ I looked at the girl. My voice hadn’t lied: that was how it would be, of course it was. I’d be thrown out by the scruff of the neck; you could tell that from her face already. Anger boiled up in me. I wanted to play the vilest, most swinish trick, a merchant’s trick: look at her with a sneer and then, while she stood there before me, floor her with the sort of tone only some petty merchant can manage:

<!-- B03-C04-P018 -->
“‘Four thousand, is it! Why, I was joking, madam. Whatever were you thinking? You’ve reckoned a little too trustingly, madam. A couple of hundred, perhaps, I’d be quite happy and willing, but four thousand—young lady, that’s too much money to throw away on such frivolities. You’ve been so kind as to trouble yourself for nothing.’

<!-- B03-C04-P019 -->
“You see, of course I’d have lost everything. She’d have run away, but it would have been infernal, such revenge, worth everything else. I’d have howled with remorse for the rest of my life, but just to play that trick now! Believe me, I’d never felt that with anyone, not a single woman: to look at her with hatred at such a moment. Yet I make the sign of the cross as I tell you: I looked at this one for three or five seconds with terrible hatred, the very hatred that’s only a hair’s breadth from love, from the maddest love! I went over to the window, pressed my forehead to the frozen glass, and I remember the ice burning my forehead like fire. Don’t worry, I didn’t keep her long. I turned, went to the table, opened a drawer and took out a five-thousand-ruble bearer bond paying five percent—I kept it in a French dictionary. Then I silently showed it to her, folded it, gave it to her, opened the door into the entryway myself, and, stepping back, bowed low to her, from the waist, with the most respectful, heartfelt bow—believe me! She gave a shudder, looked hard at me for a second, turned terribly pale, white as a tablecloth, and suddenly, without saying a word either, not in a rush but gently, deeply, quietly, bent all the way down at my feet, her forehead to the floor: not as they do at an institute, but in the Russian way! She sprang up and ran. When she’d run out—I was wearing my sword—I drew it and was about to run myself through on the spot. Why, I don’t know. It was horribly stupid, of course, but it must have been from rapture. Do you understand that some kinds of rapture can make you kill yourself? But I didn’t run myself through. I only kissed the sword and put it back in its scabbard—which, incidentally, I could have left out of the story. In fact, I think I’ve rather drawn things out just now, telling you about all my struggles, to make myself look good. But let it stand, let it stand, and to hell with all the spies of the human heart! That’s the whole of my past ‘affair’ with Katerina Ivanovna. So now our brother Ivan knows about it, and you—and no one else!”

<!-- B03-C04-P020 -->
Dmitri Fyodorovich rose, took a restless step or two, pulled out his handkerchief and wiped the sweat from his forehead. Then he sat down again, though not in the same place, but on the bench opposite, against the other wall, so that Alyosha had to turn right around to face him.
