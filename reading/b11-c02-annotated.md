# Book XI: Brother Ivan Fyodorovich
## II. The Sore Little Foot

<!-- B11-C02-P001 -->
The first of these errands took him to Madame Khokhlakov’s, and he hurried there to finish as quickly as possible and not be late for Mitya. Madame Khokhlakov had been unwell for three weeks: for some reason her foot had swollen, and although she was not confined to bed, she nevertheless spent the day reclining on a couch in her boudoir, in attractive but perfectly proper undress. Once, with an innocent smile, Alyosha had privately observed that despite her illness Madame Khokhlakov had begun almost to make a show of her clothes: little caps, bows, and loose jackets had appeared. He had an inkling why, though he dismissed such thoughts as idle. Among her other guests over the past two months, the young man Perkhotin had begun visiting Madame Khokhlakov. Alyosha had not been there for four days and, on entering the house, had been about to hurry straight to Lise, since his business was with her: only yesterday she had sent a maid urgently asking him to come at once “on a very important matter,” which, for certain reasons, had interested Alyosha. But while the maid went to announce him to Lise, Madame Khokhlakov learned of his arrival from someone and immediately sent to ask him to come to her “for just one little minute.” Alyosha decided he had better comply with the mother’s request first, since otherwise she would keep sending to Lise every minute while he was there. Madame Khokhlakov lay on her couch, dressed with a particularly festive air and plainly in a state of extreme nervous excitement. She greeted Alyosha with cries of delight.

<!-- B11-C02-P002 -->
“Ages, ages, whole ages since I’ve seen you! A whole week, just think—oh, though you were here only four days ago, on Wednesday. You’re here for Lise, I’m sure you meant to tiptoe straight in to her so I wouldn’t hear. Dear, dear Alexei Fyodorovich, if only you knew how she worries me! But that can wait. It’s the most important thing, but it can wait. Dear Alexei Fyodorovich, I trust you completely with my Lise. Since the death of the elder Zosima—God rest his soul!” She crossed herself. “Since his death I look on you as a monk of the Great Schema, though you do wear your new suit most charmingly. Wherever did you find such a tailor here? But no, no, that’s not the important thing, that can wait. Forgive me for calling you Alyosha sometimes; I’m an old woman, I’m allowed anything.” She smiled coquettishly. “But that can wait too. The important thing is not to forget the important thing. Please remind me yourself if I get carried away; just say, ‘And the important thing?’ Oh, how am I to know what’s important now! Ever since Lise took back her promise to you—her childish promise, Alexei Fyodorovich—to marry you, of course you’ve understood that it was all just the playful childish fancy of a sick girl who’d spent so long in a chair—thank God she can walk now. This new doctor Katya’s brought from Moscow for your unfortunate brother, who tomorrow… But why speak of tomorrow! The very thought of tomorrow is killing me! Mostly with curiosity… In short, this doctor came here yesterday and saw Lise… I paid him fifty rubles for the visit. But that’s not it, not it at all… You see, I’ve lost my thread completely now. I’m in a hurry. Why am I in a hurry? I don’t know. It’s dreadful how I’ve stopped knowing things. Everything’s tangled into a sort of knot. I’m afraid you’ll jump up and run away from sheer boredom, and that will be the last I see of you. Oh, my God! Why are we sitting here? First of all—coffee! Yulia, Glafira, coffee!”

<!-- B11-C02-P003 -->
Alyosha hastily thanked her and said he had only just had coffee.

<!-- B11-C02-P004 -->
“With whom?”

<!-- B11-C02-P005 -->
“With Agrafena Alexandrovna.”

<!-- B11-C02-P006 -->
“That’s… that’s that woman! Oh, she’s ruined everyone, though I don’t know, they say she’s become a saint, but rather late in the day. Better to have done it before, when it was needed. What good is it now? Don’t speak, don’t speak, Alexei Fyodorovich, because I have so much to say I don’t think I’ll manage to say anything. This dreadful trial… I shall certainly go. I’m making arrangements. They’ll carry me in in a chair, and besides, I can sit up, I’ll have people with me, and you know I’m a witness. What shall I say, what shall I say! I don’t know what I’ll say. One has to take an oath, doesn’t one, doesn’t one?”

<!-- B11-C02-P007 -->
“Yes, but I don’t think you’ll be able to attend.”

<!-- B11-C02-P008 -->
“I can sit up. Oh, you’re putting me off! This trial, this savage act, and then everyone goes to Siberia, other people get married, and it all happens so quickly, quickly, everything changes, and in the end it comes to nothing, everyone’s old and looking into the grave. Well, let them. I’m tired. This Katya—*cette charmante personne*, this charming person—has shattered all my hopes. Now she’ll follow one of your brothers to Siberia, and your other brother will follow her and live in the neighboring town, and they’ll all torment one another. It’s driving me out of my mind, and above all this publicity: they’ve written about it a million times in all the Petersburg and Moscow papers. Oh yes, imagine, they’ve even written about me, saying I was your brother’s ‘dear friend’—I refuse to say the nasty word. Imagine, just imagine!”

<!-- B11-C02-P009 -->
“That can’t be! Where did they write that, and how?”

<!-- B11-C02-P010 -->
“I’ll show you this minute. I got it yesterday and read it yesterday… Here, in *Rumors*, the Petersburg newspaper. *Rumors* only began publishing this year. I’m terribly fond of rumors, so I subscribed, and now see what I’ve brought on myself: these are the sort of rumors they turn out to be. Here, right here, read it.”

<!-- B11-C02-P011 -->
She handed Alyosha a sheet of newspaper that had been lying under her pillow.

<!-- B11-C02-P012 -->
She was not merely upset; she seemed utterly shattered, and perhaps everything in her head really had tangled into a knot. The newspaper item was highly characteristic and was bound to touch her on a very sensitive point, but fortunately for her, perhaps, she was incapable just then of concentrating on any one subject and could therefore forget even the newspaper a minute later and jump to something entirely different. Alyosha had long known that news of the terrible trial had spread throughout Russia, and God, what wild reports and dispatches he had read in those two months, among other, accurate reports, about his brother, about the Karamazovs in general, and even about himself! One newspaper actually said that, terrified by his brother’s crime, he had taken the Great Schema and shut himself away; another denied this and wrote that, on the contrary, he and his elder Zosima had broken into the monastery strongbox and “made off from the monastery.” The present report in *Rumors* was headed: “From Skotoprigonyevsk (alas, that is our town’s name; I have concealed it for a long time): The Karamazov Trial.” It was a short piece and said nothing directly about Madame Khokhlakov; indeed, all names were withheld. It merely stated that the criminal now about to be tried amid such a commotion, a retired army captain of an insolent stamp, an idler and a champion of serfdom, was forever pursuing love affairs and exerted a particular influence on certain “ladies languishing in solitude.” One such lady, a “languishing widow,” who affected youthfulness despite having a grown daughter, had been so captivated by him that only two hours before the crime she had offered him three thousand rubles on condition that he immediately run away with her to the gold mines. But the villain had preferred to murder his father and rob him of precisely the same three thousand, counting on doing so with impunity, rather than drag himself to Siberia with the forty-year-old charms of his languishing lady. As was only proper, this playful dispatch concluded with noble indignation at the immorality of parricide and the former institution of serfdom. Having read it with curiosity, Alyosha folded the sheet and handed it back to Madame Khokhlakov.

<!-- B11-C02-P013 -->
“How can it not be me?” she babbled again. “It is me—I was the one who proposed the gold mines to him barely an hour before, and suddenly it’s ‘forty-year-old charms’! Was that why I did it? He did this on purpose! May the eternal Judge forgive him for the forty-year-old charms, as I forgive him, but it’s… do you know who it is? Your friend Rakitin.”

<!-- B11-C02-P014 -->
“Perhaps,” Alyosha said, “though I haven’t heard anything.”

<!-- B11-C02-P015 -->
“It’s him, him, no ‘perhaps’ about it! I turned him out, you see… You know the whole story, don’t you?”

<!-- B11-C02-P016 -->
“I know you asked him not to visit you anymore, but precisely why, I… haven’t heard from you, at least.”

<!-- B11-C02-P017 -->
“So you’ve heard from him! What does he say? Does he abuse me, abuse me terribly?”

<!-- B11-C02-P018 -->
“Yes, he abuses you, but he abuses everyone. I haven’t heard from him either why you turned him away. In any case, I very seldom see him. We aren’t friends.”

<!-- B11-C02-P019 -->
“Well, then I’ll tell you everything, and I suppose I must confess, because there’s one little point in which I may be to blame myself. Only a tiny, tiny little point, the tiniest, so perhaps it isn’t there at all. You see, my dear…” Madame Khokhlakov suddenly assumed a playful air, and a sweet, if enigmatic, little smile flickered on her lips. “You see, I suspect… forgive me, Alyosha, I’m like a mother to you… Oh no, no, quite the reverse, I’m coming to you now as to my father… because mother is quite wrong here… Well, just as I’d confess to the elder Zosima, that’s exactly it, that fits beautifully: didn’t I call you a monk of the Great Schema just now? So this poor young man, your friend Rakitin—oh God, I simply can’t be angry with him! I am angry and cross, but not very—in short, this frivolous young man suddenly seems to have taken it into his head, imagine, to fall in love with me. I only noticed afterward, only afterward, all at once; but at first, about a month ago, he began coming more often, almost every day, though we’d known each other before. I had no idea… and suddenly it was as if a light dawned, and I began noticing things, to my surprise. You know I began receiving that modest, charming, worthy young man Pyotr Ilyich Perkhotin, who has a post here, two months ago. You’ve met him yourself so many times. Isn’t he worthy and serious? He comes once every three days, not every day—though he’d be welcome every day—and he’s always so well dressed. In general, Alyosha, I like young people who are talented and modest, like you, and his mind is almost that of a statesman, he speaks so charmingly, and I shall certainly, certainly put in a word for him. He’s a future diplomat. On that terrible day he almost saved me from death by coming here in the night. Whereas your friend Rakitin always comes in such boots, and stretches them out across the carpet… In short, he even began dropping hints, and then one day, as he was leaving, he suddenly squeezed my hand terribly hard. No sooner had he squeezed my hand than my foot began hurting. He’d met Pyotr Ilyich here before, and would you believe it, he’s always sniping at him, always sniping, practically growling at him over something. I just watch the two of them when they meet and laugh inside. Then one day I’m sitting alone—no, I was lying down by then—suddenly I’m lying alone and Mikhail Ivanovich comes in and, imagine, brings me some verses of his, the shortest little verses, about my sore foot. That is, he’s described my sore foot in verse. Wait, how does it go:

> This little foot, this little foot,  
> Has felt a little twinge or two—

“Or however it goes—I simply can’t remember poetry. I have it here somewhere. Well, I’ll show you later, but it’s charming, charming, and you know, it isn’t only about the foot, it’s instructive too, with a lovely idea, only I’ve forgotten it. In short, just the thing for an album. Well, naturally I thanked him, and he was plainly flattered. I’d hardly finished thanking him when Pyotr Ilyich came in, and Mikhail Ivanovich suddenly grew as black as night. I could see Pyotr Ilyich had interrupted something, because Mikhail Ivanovich certainly meant to say something just then, after the poem—I could sense it already—and in came Pyotr Ilyich. Suddenly I show Pyotr Ilyich the poem, without saying who wrote it. But I’m sure, I’m sure he guessed at once, though he won’t admit it even now and says he didn’t guess; but he does that on purpose. Pyotr Ilyich burst out laughing and began criticizing it: ‘Wretched little verses,’ he said, ‘some seminarian must have written them’—and with such relish, such relish, you know! Then your friend, instead of laughing, suddenly flew into a complete rage… Lord, I thought they’d come to blows: ‘I wrote it,’ he said. ‘I wrote it as a joke, because I consider writing verse beneath me… Only my verses are good. They want to put up a monument to your Pushkin for writing about women’s little feet, whereas mine has a social purpose, and you’re a champion of serfdom yourself,’ he said. ‘You have no humanity, you feel none of the enlightened feelings of our time, progress has left you untouched; you’re an official, you take bribes!’ At that I began crying out and pleading with them. But Pyotr Ilyich, you know, isn’t at all timid, and suddenly he took the most gentlemanly tone: looking at him mockingly, listening, and apologizing. ‘I didn’t know,’ he said. ‘If I’d known, I wouldn’t have said it; I’d have praised it… Poets are all so touchy,’ he said… In short, such mockery beneath the most gentlemanly manner. He explained to me afterward himself that it was all mockery, whereas I thought he meant it. Only suddenly I’m lying there just as I am before you now, thinking: would it or would it not be gentlemanly to turn Mikhail Ivanovich out for shouting so improperly at my guest in my own house? And would you believe it: I lie there with my eyes shut, thinking, would it or would it not be gentlemanly, and I can’t decide, and I torment myself and torment myself, and my heart pounds: shall I cry out or shan’t I? One voice says, ‘Cry out,’ and another says, ‘No, don’t!’ No sooner had that second voice spoken than I suddenly cried out and suddenly fainted. Well, of course there was a commotion. I suddenly get up and say to Mikhail Ivanovich: ‘It pains me to tell you, but I no longer wish to receive you in my house.’ And so I turned him out. Oh, Alexei Fyodorovich! I know myself I behaved badly. I was lying all along, I wasn’t angry with him at all, but it suddenly struck me—suddenly is the point—what a fine thing that scene would be… Only believe me, the scene was natural all the same, because I actually burst into tears and went on crying for several days afterward, and then one day after dinner I suddenly forgot the whole thing. So he’s stopped coming for two weeks now, and I think: surely he isn’t going to stop coming altogether? That was only yesterday, and then in the evening this *Rumors* arrives. I read it and gasped. Well, who wrote it? He wrote it: he went home that day, sat down, and wrote it; he sent it off, and they printed it. It was two weeks ago, after all. Only, Alyosha, it’s dreadful what I’m saying, and none of it what I ought to be saying! Oh, it all says itself!”

<!-- B11-C02-P020 -->
“It’s terribly important for me to reach my brother in time today,” Alyosha ventured.

<!-- B11-C02-P021 -->
“Exactly, exactly! You’ve reminded me of everything! Listen, what is a fit of passion?”

<!-- B11-C02-P022 -->
“What sort of fit of passion?” Alyosha asked in surprise.

<!-- B11-C02-P023 -->
“A legal fit of passion. The sort they forgive you everything for. Whatever you do, they forgive you straightaway.”

<!-- B11-C02-P024 -->
“But what do you mean?”

<!-- B11-C02-P025 -->
“I mean this: that Katya… Oh, she’s a sweet, sweet creature, only I have no idea whom she’s in love with. She was here the other day, and I couldn’t get anything out of her. Especially as she’s begun being so superficial with me now—in short, nothing but my health and nothing else, and she even adopts such a tone. So I said to myself: very well then, God be with you… Oh yes, this fit of passion: the doctor’s arrived. You know the doctor has arrived? Of course you do, the one who recognizes mad people; you sent for him yourselves—that is, not you, Katya. Always Katya! So you see, a man is sitting there, perfectly sane, only suddenly he has a fit of passion. He’s aware of himself and knows what he’s doing, yet he’s in a fit of passion. Well, that must certainly have happened to Dmitri Fyodorovich. As soon as they opened the new courts they discovered fits of passion. That’s one of the blessings of the new courts. This doctor came here and questioned me about that evening, about the gold mines: what was he like then, he asked? How could he not have been in a fit of passion? He came in shouting: ‘Money, money, three thousand, give me three thousand!’ Then he went off and suddenly killed him. ‘I don’t want to,’ he says, ‘I don’t want to kill him,’ and suddenly he kills him. That’s precisely what they’ll forgive him for: he resisted it, and yet he killed him.”

<!-- B11-C02-P026 -->
“But he didn’t kill him,” Alyosha interrupted rather sharply. Anxiety and impatience were increasingly getting the better of him.

<!-- B11-C02-P027 -->
“I know, that old man Grigory killed him…”

<!-- B11-C02-P028 -->
“What do you mean, Grigory?” Alyosha cried.

<!-- B11-C02-P029 -->
“Him, him, it was Grigory. When Dmitri Fyodorovich struck him he lay there, then he got up, saw the door was open, went in, and killed Fyodor Pavlovich.”

<!-- B11-C02-P030 -->
“But why, why?”

<!-- B11-C02-P031 -->
“He had a fit of passion. When Dmitri Fyodorovich struck him on the head, he came to and had a fit of passion, went in, and killed him. As for his saying he didn’t kill him, perhaps he doesn’t remember. Only you see, it would be better, much better, if Dmitri Fyodorovich had killed him. And that is what happened: though I say it was Grigory, it was certainly Dmitri Fyodorovich, and that’s much, much better! Oh, not better because a son killed his father—I don’t approve; on the contrary, children should honor their parents—but still, it’s better if it was him, because then you have nothing to cry about, since he killed him without knowing what he was doing, or rather, knowing everything but not knowing how it happened to him. No, let them forgive him; it’s so humane, and people will see the blessing of the new courts. I had no idea, though they say it’s been going on for ages, and when I found out yesterday I was so struck that I wanted to send for you at once. And then, if they forgive him, bring him straight from the court to dinner here. I’ll invite our friends, and we’ll drink to the new courts. I don’t think he’d be dangerous, and besides, I’ll invite a great many people, so we can always take him out if he does anything. And afterward he can be a justice of the peace somewhere, in another town, or something of the sort, because people who’ve suffered misfortune themselves make the best judges. Above all, who isn’t in a fit of passion nowadays? You, me—we’re all in fits of passion. There are so many examples: a man is sitting singing a love song, suddenly something displeases him, he picks up a pistol and kills the first person he comes across, and afterward they forgive him everything. I read about it recently, and all the doctors confirmed it. Doctors confirm things now, they confirm everything. Why, my Lise is in a fit of passion. Only yesterday I cried because of her, and the day before yesterday I cried, and today I realized it’s simply a fit of passion. Oh, Lise upsets me so! I think she’s gone quite mad. Why did she send for you? Did she send for you, or did you come to see her yourself?”

<!-- B11-C02-P032 -->
“Yes, she sent for me, and I’ll go to her now,” Alyosha said, beginning to rise resolutely.

<!-- B11-C02-P033 -->
“Oh, dear, dear Alexei Fyodorovich, perhaps this is the most important thing of all,” Madame Khokhlakov cried, suddenly bursting into tears. “God knows I sincerely trust you with Lise, and it’s of no consequence that she sent for you behind her mother’s back. But Ivan Fyodorovich, your brother—forgive me, I can’t entrust my daughter to him so readily, though I still consider him the most chivalrous young man. And imagine, he came to see Lise, and I knew nothing about it.”

<!-- B11-C02-P034 -->
“How? What? When?” Alyosha asked, astonished. He did not sit down again but listened standing.

<!-- B11-C02-P035 -->
“I’ll tell you. Perhaps that’s just why I sent for you, because I don’t know anymore why I sent for you. Here it is: Ivan Fyodorovich has been to see me only twice since his return from Moscow. The first time he called as an acquaintance, and the second time, quite recently, Katya was here, and he dropped in because he’d learned she was with me. Naturally, I didn’t claim the right to frequent visits, knowing how much he has to trouble him already—*vous comprenez, cette affaire et la mort terrible de votre papa*, you understand, this affair and your papa’s terrible death. Only suddenly I learn he’s been here again, but to Lise, not me. That was six days ago now: he came, stayed five minutes, and left. And I only learned about it from Glafira a full three days afterward, so I was quite struck. I called Lise at once, and she laughed: he thought you were asleep, she said, and came in to ask me about your health. Of course that’s what happened. Only Lise, Lise—oh God, how she upsets me! Imagine, one night she suddenly—this was four days ago, just after you’d last been here and left—suddenly in the night she has a fit, screaming, shrieking, hysterics! Why do I never have hysterics? Then the next day another fit, then on the third day, and yesterday, and yesterday this fit of passion. Suddenly she shouts at me: ‘I hate Ivan Fyodorovich. I insist that you stop receiving him, that you forbid him the house!’ I was dumbfounded by such an unexpected demand, and I objected: why should I turn away such a worthy young man, with so much knowledge and such a misfortune? Because all these affairs are a misfortune, after all, not good fortune, aren’t they? She suddenly burst out laughing at what I’d said, and so insultingly, you know. Well, I was glad, I thought, to have made her laugh, and now the fits would stop, especially since I’d wanted to turn Ivan Fyodorovich away myself for these strange visits without my consent, and demand an explanation. But this morning Lise suddenly woke up and grew angry with Yulia and, imagine, struck her in the face with her hand. It’s monstrous; I address my maids formally. Then an hour later she’s embracing Yulia and kissing her feet. As for me, she sent to say she wouldn’t come to me at all and never wanted to come again; and when I dragged myself in to see her, she threw herself on me, kissing me and crying, and while she kissed me she pushed me right out, without saying a word, so I learned nothing at all. Now, dear Alexei Fyodorovich, all my hopes rest on you, and of course the fate of my whole life is in your hands. I simply ask you to go to Lise, find out everything as only you can, and come and tell me—me, her mother—because, you understand, I’ll die, I’ll simply die if this goes on, or I’ll run away from home. I can’t bear any more. I have patience, but I may lose it, and then… then there’ll be horrors. Oh, my God, Pyotr Ilyich at last!” Madame Khokhlakov cried, her whole face suddenly lighting up as she caught sight of Pyotr Ilyich Perkhotin coming in. “You’re late, late! Well, sit down, tell us, settle our fate. What about this lawyer? Where are you going, Alexei Fyodorovich?”

<!-- B11-C02-P036 -->
“To Lise.”

<!-- B11-C02-P037 -->
“Oh yes! You won’t forget, you won’t forget what I asked you? It’s our fate, our fate!”

<!-- B11-C02-P038 -->
“Of course I won’t forget, if only I can… but I’m so late,” Alyosha muttered, beating as quick a retreat as possible.

<!-- B11-C02-P039 -->
“No, you must come back, must, none of this ‘if I can,’ or I’ll die!” Madame Khokhlakov called after him, but Alyosha had already left the room.
