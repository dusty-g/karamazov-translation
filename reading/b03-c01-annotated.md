# Book III: The Lustful

## I. In the Servants’ Quarters

<!-- B03-C01-P001 -->
Fyodor Pavlovich Karamazov’s house stood well away from the center of town, though not quite on the outskirts. It was rather dilapidated, but pleasant to look at: a single story with a small upper floor, painted gray, with a red iron roof. Still, it could have stood for a very long time yet, and it was roomy and comfortable. There were all sorts of little storerooms, hiding places, and unexpected stairways. Rats lived in it, but Fyodor Pavlovich did not altogether resent them: “At least it’s not so dull in the evenings when you’re alone.” And he did in fact make a habit of sending the servants off to the outbuilding for the night and locking himself up alone in the house until morning. This outbuilding stood across the yard; it was spacious and solidly built. Fyodor Pavlovich had also ordered the kitchen to be set up there, although there was a kitchen in the house: he disliked cooking smells, and his food was brought across the yard summer and winter. The house had been built for a large family, after all: it could have accommodated five times as many masters and servants. But at the time of our story only Fyodor Pavlovich and Ivan Fyodorovich lived in the house, while the servants’ outbuilding held just three people: old Grigory, his wife, old Marfa, and the servant Smerdyakov, still a young man. We must say a little more about these three members of the household staff. We have, however, already said a good deal about old Grigory Vasilyevich Kutuzov. He was a firm, unbending man, doggedly making straight for his goal once, for whatever reason—often an astonishingly illogical one—it had taken shape before him as an unshakable truth. Generally speaking, he was honest and incorruptible. His wife, Marfa Ignatyevna, though she had bowed unquestioningly to her husband’s will all her life, had, for instance, pestered him terribly just after the emancipation of the serfs to leave Fyodor Pavlovich, go to Moscow, and start some little business there (they had a little money put by); but Grigory had decided then and there, once and for all, that the woman was lying, “because every woman is dishonest,” and that they ought not to leave their former master, whatever sort of man he might be, “because that’s their duty now.”

“Do you understand what duty is?” he asked Marfa Ignatyevna.

<!-- B03-C01-P003 -->
“I understand about duty, Grigory Vasilyevich, but what duty we have to stay here, that I don’t understand at all,” Marfa Ignatyevna answered firmly.

<!-- B03-C01-P004 -->
“Then don’t understand. That’s how it’s going to be. Keep quiet from now on.”

<!-- B03-C01-P005 -->
And so it was: they did not leave, and Fyodor Pavlovich gave them a wage, a small one, and paid it. Besides, Grigory knew that his influence over his master was beyond dispute. He felt this, and he was right: that cunning, stubborn buffoon Fyodor Pavlovich, a man of very firm character “in certain things in life,” as he put it himself, could, to his own surprise, be decidedly weak in certain other “things in life.” And he himself knew which things; he knew, and was afraid of a great deal. In certain things in life you had to keep your wits about you, and it was hard to do that without someone you could rely on, while Grigory was utterly reliable. Many times in the course of his career, in fact, Fyodor Pavlovich might have been beaten, and badly beaten, and Grigory had always come to his rescue, though afterward he gave him a lecture every time. But a mere beating would not have frightened Fyodor Pavlovich: there were occasions of a higher order, even very subtle and complicated ones, when Fyodor Pavlovich himself might have been unable to define the extraordinary need for someone faithful and close to him that would sometimes suddenly, in an instant and inexplicably, arise within him. These were almost states of illness: utterly depraved, and often as cruel as a vicious insect in his lust, Fyodor Pavlovich would sometimes, in drunken moments, be seized by spiritual fear and a moral upheaval that made itself felt in his soul almost physically, so to speak. “It’s as though my soul were fluttering in my throat at those times,” he would sometimes say. At just such moments he liked to have someone near him, close by, if not in the same room then at least in the outbuilding—a man who was loyal, steadfast, quite unlike himself, not depraved; a man who, though he saw all the debauchery going on and knew every secret, would still allow it all out of loyalty, offer no resistance, and above all make no reproaches and threaten him with nothing, either in this world or in the next; and who, if need be, would even defend him—from whom? From someone unknown, but terrible and dangerous. The essential thing was to have another human being there, someone he had known for years, a friend, whom he could call in at a moment of distress simply to look into his face, perhaps exchange a word or two on some entirely unrelated subject; and if the man was all right, not angry, somehow his heart felt easier, but if he was angry, well, then it was sadder. It happened, though very rarely indeed, that Fyodor Pavlovich would even go to the outbuilding at night to wake Grigory and ask him to come over for a moment. Grigory would come, Fyodor Pavlovich would start talking about the most utter trifles, and soon he would send him away, sometimes even with a little sneer and a joke; then he would spit, lie down, and sleep the sleep of the just. Something of this kind also happened to Fyodor Pavlovich when Alyosha arrived. Alyosha “pierced his heart” by “living here, seeing everything, and judging nothing.” More than that, he brought something unprecedented: a complete absence of contempt for him, the old man; on the contrary, unfailing kindness and an entirely natural, openhearted affection for someone who so little deserved it. All this came as a complete surprise to the old lecher, a stranger to family life, something wholly unexpected by a man who until then had loved nothing but “filth.” After Alyosha left, he admitted to himself that he had understood something he had never before wanted to understand.

I have already mentioned at the beginning of my story how Grigory hated Adelaida Ivanovna, Fyodor Pavlovich’s first wife and the mother of his first son, Dmitri Fyodorovich, and how, on the contrary, he defended his second wife, the *klikusha*, Sofya Ivanovna, against his own master and anyone else who took it into their head to say anything unkind or flippant about her. His compassion for this unfortunate woman had become something sacred to him, so that even twenty years later he would not have tolerated so much as a disparaging hint about her, no matter who it came from, and would at once have objected. Outwardly Grigory was cold and solemn, not talkative, a man whose words were weighty, never frivolous. It would have been just as impossible to tell at first glance whether or not he loved his unresisting, submissive wife; yet he did love her, and she, of course, understood this. This Marfa Ignatyevna was not merely no fool; she may even have been cleverer than her husband, or at least more sensible in everyday affairs. Yet from the very beginning of their marriage she had submitted to him without a murmur or a word in reply, and unquestionably respected him for his spiritual superiority. It is remarkable that throughout their lives the two spoke very little to each other, except about the most necessary, immediate matters. Solemn and majestic, Grigory always thought over his affairs and concerns alone, so that Marfa Ignatyevna had long ago understood once and for all that he had no need whatever of her advice. She felt that her husband valued her silence and took it as a sign of intelligence. He had never beaten her, except just once, and then only lightly. In the first year of Adelaida Ivanovna’s marriage to Fyodor Pavlovich, while they were in the country, the village girls and women, still serfs at the time, were once gathered in the master’s yard to sing and dance. They struck up “In the Meadows,”[^b03-c01-n001] and suddenly Marfa Ignatyevna, still a young woman then, sprang out in front of the singers and performed a Russian dance in her own particular style, not in the village women’s country fashion but as she had danced when she was a servant girl in the wealthy Miusovs’ household, in their private estate theater, where a dancing master brought from Moscow had taught the actors to dance. Grigory saw his wife perform, and an hour later, back in their cottage, taught her a lesson by pulling her about a little by the hair. But that was the end of the beatings once and for all; there was never another for the rest of their lives, and Marfa Ignatyevna, for her part, swore off dancing from that day on.

God had given them no children; there had been one little child, but he had died. Grigory evidently loved children and did not even conceal it—that is, he was not ashamed to show it. When Adelaida Ivanovna ran away, he had taken Dmitri Fyodorovich into his arms as a three-year-old boy and tended him for almost a year, combing his hair himself, even washing him himself in a tub. Later he had also looked after Ivan Fyodorovich and Alyosha, and had received a slap in the face for his pains; but I have already told all that. His own little child had brought him joy only as a hope, while Marfa Ignatyevna was still pregnant. Once born, he struck Grigory’s heart with grief and horror. The fact was that the boy was born with six fingers. The sight so crushed Grigory that not only did he say nothing right up to the day of the baptism, he deliberately went off into the garden to be silent. It was spring, and for all three days he dug the vegetable beds in the garden. The baby was to be baptized on the third day; by then Grigory had worked something out. He entered the cottage, where the clergy had gathered and the guests had arrived, including, at last, Fyodor Pavlovich himself, who had come in person to stand as godfather, and suddenly declared that the child “ought not to be baptized at all.” He did not say it loudly or at length, but barely squeezed out a word at a time, all the while staring blankly and fixedly at the priest.

<!-- B03-C01-P008 -->
“Why is that?” the priest inquired with cheerful surprise.

<!-- B03-C01-P009 -->
“Because it’s a... dragon...” Grigory muttered.

<!-- B03-C01-P010 -->
“What do you mean, a dragon? What dragon?”

<!-- B03-C01-P011 -->
Grigory was silent for a while.

<!-- B03-C01-P012 -->
“There’s been a confusion of nature...” he muttered, very obscurely but very firmly, and with plainly no desire to say any more.

<!-- B03-C01-P013 -->
They laughed, and of course the poor little baby was baptized. Grigory prayed fervently beside the font but did not change his opinion of the newborn. Still, he interfered with nothing; only, throughout the two weeks the sickly boy lived, he hardly looked at him, would not even acknowledge him, and stayed away from the cottage most of the time. But when the boy died of thrush after two weeks, he laid him in his little coffin himself and gazed at him in deep anguish; and when they filled in his small, shallow grave, he knelt and bowed down to the ground before it. For many years afterward he never once mentioned his child, and Marfa Ignatyevna never spoke of her child in his presence either; when she happened to talk to anyone about her “little one,” she spoke in a whisper, even if Grigory Vasilyevich was not there. From the time of that little grave, Marfa Ignatyevna observed, he chiefly occupied himself with “holy things,” reading the *Chet’i-Minei*, mostly silently and alone, always putting on his large, round silver spectacles. He seldom read aloud, except during Lent. He loved the Book of Job, had got hold somewhere of a manuscript copy of the discourses and sermons of “our God-bearing father Isaac the Syrian,” and read it doggedly for years on end, understanding almost nothing at all; but perhaps that was precisely why he valued and loved the book most. Very recently he had begun listening to and looking into the teachings of the Khlysty, having found an opportunity in the neighborhood; he was visibly shaken, but did not see fit to embrace the new faith. His wide reading in “holy things” had, of course, given his face a still greater solemnity.

<!-- B03-C01-P014 -->
Perhaps he had a bent for mysticism. And then, as if on purpose, the birth of his six-fingered baby and the child’s death coincided with another very strange, unexpected, and unusual event, which left, as he himself once put it afterward, a “seal” upon his soul. It so happened that on the very day they buried the six-fingered little one, Marfa Ignatyevna woke in the night and heard what seemed to be a newborn baby crying. Frightened, she woke her husband. He listened and remarked that it sounded more like someone moaning, “a woman, it seems.” He got up and dressed; it was a fairly warm May night. Going out onto the porch, he listened and heard distinctly that the moans were coming from the garden. But the garden was locked from the yard at night, and there was no other way into it, since a high, sturdy fence ran all the way around it. Grigory went back inside, lit a lantern, took the garden key, and, paying no attention to his wife’s hysterical terror as she went on insisting that she could hear a baby crying and that it must be her boy crying and calling to her, silently set off for the garden. There he made out clearly that the moans were coming from their little bathhouse, which stood in the garden not far from the gate, and that it really was a woman moaning. He opened the bathhouse and saw a sight that left him rooted to the spot: the town’s holy fool, who wandered the streets and was known to everyone as Lizaveta the Stinking, had made her way into their bathhouse and just given birth to a baby. The baby lay beside her, and she lay dying beside him. She said nothing, if only because she was unable to speak. But all this needs to be explained separately.
