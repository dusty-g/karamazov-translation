# Book VI: The Russian Monk
## II. From the Life of the Elder Zosima, Priest-Monk of the Great Schema[^b06-c02-n001], Who Fell Asleep in God, Compiled from His Own Words by Alexei Fyodorovich Karamazov

<!-- B06-C02-P001 -->
### Biographical Information

### a) The Elder Zosima's Young Brother

<!-- B06-C02-P002 -->
Beloved fathers and teachers, I was born in a distant northern province, in the town of V. My father was a nobleman, but neither distinguished nor of any great rank. He died when I was only two, and I have no memory of him at all. He left my mother a small wooden house and some money—not a great deal, but enough for her to live with her children without want. There were only two of us: myself, Zinovy, and my older brother, Markel. He was some eight years older than I, quick-tempered and irritable, but kind, never mocking, and strangely silent, especially at home, with me, our mother and the servants. He did well at grammar school, but did not make friends with his schoolmates, though neither did he quarrel with them; at least, that was how our mother remembered it. Six months before his death, when he was already seventeen, he took to visiting a solitary man in our town, a sort of political exile, banished from Moscow to our town for freethinking. This exile was a considerable scholar and a distinguished philosopher at the university. For some reason he grew fond of Markel and began receiving him. The young man would spend whole evenings with him, all that winter, until the exile was recalled to government service in Petersburg at his own request, for he had patrons. Lent began, but Markel refused to fast, cursed and laughed at it. “It's all nonsense,” he said, “and there isn't any God either.” He horrified our mother and the servants, and me too, little as I was; although I was only nine, I too was very frightened by these words. All our servants were serfs, four of them, all bought in the name of a landowner we knew. I still remember our mother selling one of the four, the cook Afimya, who was elderly and lame, for sixty rubles in banknotes, and hiring a free woman in her place. Then, in the sixth week of Lent, my brother suddenly grew worse. He had always been sickly, with weak lungs, a frail constitution and a tendency to consumption; he was quite tall, but thin and delicate, with a very handsome face. Perhaps he had caught a chill; the doctor came, and soon whispered to our mother that it was galloping consumption and he would not live through the spring. Our mother began to weep and cautiously asked my brother—chiefly so as not to frighten him—to prepare himself and receive God's holy mysteries, for he was still up and about. At this he grew angry and abused the church of God, but then fell into thought: he had guessed at once that he was dangerously ill and that this was why our mother was sending him to prepare himself and take communion while he still had the strength. He had long known he was unwell, however; a year before, sitting at table with our mother and me, he had once said quite coolly, “I won't be here among you much longer. Perhaps I won't live another year,” and now it seemed he had foretold it. Three days passed, and Holy Week came. On Tuesday morning my brother began his preparation. “I'm really doing this for you, Mother, to make you happy and set your mind at rest,” he told her. Our mother wept for joy and for sorrow too: “His end must be near, for him to change so suddenly.” But he did not go to church for long: he took to his bed, and his confession was heard and communion given to him at home.

The days were bright and clear, full of fragrance; Easter fell late that year. I remember him coughing all night, sleeping badly, but every morning he would dress and try to sit in the upholstered armchair. That is how I remember him: sitting there quietly, gently, smiling, ill himself but with a cheerful, joyful face. His whole soul had changed—what a wondrous transformation had suddenly begun in him! His old nurse would come into his room: “Let me light the little lamp before your icon too, my dear.” Before, he had never allowed it; he had even blown it out. “Light it, dear, light it. I was a monster to forbid you before. You pray to God as you light the lamp, and I pray as I rejoice in you. So we are praying to the same God.” These words seemed strange to us. Our mother would go off to her room and weep and weep, only drying her eyes and putting on a cheerful face when she came in to see him. “Mother, don't cry, my darling,” he would say. “I have so much life left, so much joy to share with you. Life is so cheerful, so joyful!” “Oh, my dear, what joy can you have, burning with fever all night and coughing as though your chest would burst?” “Mama,” he would answer, “don't cry. Life is paradise, and we are all in paradise, but we refuse to know it; if we chose to know it, the whole world would be paradise tomorrow.” Everyone marveled at his words, spoken so strangely and with such conviction; they were moved and wept. Friends came to see us. “My dear, dear ones,” he would say, “what have I done to deserve your love? Why do you love someone like me, and how was it I never knew it before, never valued it?” Again and again he would say to the servants coming in, “My dear, dear ones, why do you serve me? Am I worthy to be served? If God had mercy and let me live, I would serve you myself, for we must all serve one another.” Our mother would shake her head as she listened. “My dear boy, it's your illness making you talk like this.” “Mama, my joy,” he said, “there must be masters and servants, but let me too be a servant to my servants, just as they are to me. And I will tell you something else, Mother: each of us is guilty before everyone for everything, and I more than anyone.” At this our mother even smiled, smiling through her tears. “How can you be more guilty before everyone than anyone else?” she said. “There are murderers and robbers, but what sins have you had time to commit, to accuse yourself more than them all?” “Mother, my own flesh and blood,” he said—he had begun using these unexpected words of affection—“my dear, joyful flesh and blood, know that truly each of us is guilty before everyone, for everyone and everything. I don't know how to explain it to you, but I feel it so strongly it hurts. How could we live as we did, getting angry and knowing nothing of it?”

That was how he would wake from sleep, every day more tender, more joyful, trembling all over with love. The doctor would come—an old German, Eisenschmidt, attended him—and he would joke, “Well, doctor, will I live another little day in this world?” “Not just a day, you'll live many days,” the doctor would answer, “months and years yet.” “But what are years, what are months!” he would exclaim. “Why count the days? One day is enough for a person to know all happiness. My dear ones, why do we quarrel, boast to one another, hold grudges against one another? Let us go straight into the garden and walk and play, love one another and praise one another and kiss one another and bless our life.” “Your son has not long to live,” the doctor told our mother as she saw him to the steps. “The illness is deranging his mind.” The windows of his room looked out onto the garden, a shady garden with old trees; spring buds had formed on their branches, the early birds had arrived and were calling and singing outside his windows. Looking at them and delighting in them, he suddenly began asking their forgiveness too. “Birds of God, joyful birds, forgive me too, for I have sinned against you as well.” No one in our house could understand that at all, but he wept for joy. “Yes,” he said, “all this glory of God was around me—birds, trees, meadows, the sky—and I alone lived in disgrace, I alone dishonored everything, and never noticed its beauty and glory.” “You're taking too many sins on yourself,” our mother would say, weeping. “Mother, my joy, I'm crying from happiness, not sorrow. I want to be guilty before them myself, only I can't explain it to you, for I don't even know how to love them. Let me have sinned against everyone, but then everyone will forgive me, and there is paradise. Am I not in paradise now?” There was much more that I can neither remember nor set down.

I remember once coming into his room alone, when no one else was with him. It was a clear evening; the sun was setting, lighting the whole room with a slanting ray. When he saw me, he beckoned, and I went up to him. He took me by the shoulders with both hands and looked into my face tenderly, lovingly. He said nothing, only looked at me like that for a minute. “Well,” he said, “off you go now. Play, live for me!” I went out then and went to play. But many times in later life I remembered with tears how he had told me to live for him. He said many more such wondrous and beautiful things, though we did not understand them then. He died in the third week after Easter, still conscious, and although he had stopped speaking, he did not change until his very last hour: he looked joyful, with happiness in his eyes, sought us with his gaze, smiled at us and called to us. Even in the town there was much talk of his death. All this shook me then, but not too deeply, though I cried a great deal when he was buried. I was young, a child, but everything remained indelibly in my heart; the feeling lay hidden. In its own time it would all rise up and answer. And so it did.

### b) Holy Scripture in Father Zosima's Life

<!-- B06-C02-P004 -->
My mother and I were left alone. Soon well-meaning friends advised her: “You have only one boy left now, and you aren't poor, you have money. Why not follow other people's example and send your son to Petersburg? If he stays here, you may be depriving him of a distinguished future.” They persuaded her to take me to Petersburg and put me in the cadet corps, so that afterward I could enter the Imperial Guard. My mother hesitated for a long time—how could she part with her last son?—but in the end she decided, though with many tears, hoping to further my happiness. She took me to Petersburg and placed me there, and I never saw her again, for three years later she too died, having spent all three years grieving and trembling for us both. I brought away only precious memories from my parents' home, for no memories are more precious than those of earliest childhood in one's parents' house, and this is almost always so, if there is even a little love and harmony in the family. Even from the worst family precious memories can remain, if only your soul knows how to seek what is precious. Among these memories of home I count my memories of sacred history, which, child though I was, I was very eager to learn in my parents' house. I had a book of sacred history then, with beautiful pictures, called *One Hundred and Four Sacred Stories from the Old and New Testaments*, and it was from this book that I learned to read. It is still here on my shelf; I keep it as a precious remembrance.

But even before I learned to read, I remember receiving my first glimmer of spiritual understanding, when I was only eight. My mother took me alone—I don't remember where my brother was—to the Lord's church for the liturgy on the Monday of Holy Week. It was a clear day, and remembering it now I seem to see again the incense rising from the censer, quietly ascending, while from above, through a narrow window in the dome, God's rays pour down on us in the church, and the incense, rising toward them in waves, seems to melt into them. I watched with a heart full of tenderness, and for the first time in my life I knowingly received the first seed of God's word into my soul. A boy came into the middle of the church carrying a large book, so large that it seemed to me then he could scarcely carry it. He laid it on the lectern, opened it and began to read, and suddenly, for the first time, I understood something; for the first time in my life I understood what was being read in God's church. There was a man in the land of Uz, righteous and devout, and he had so much wealth, so many camels, so many sheep and asses, and his children feasted, and he loved them dearly and prayed to God for them: perhaps they had sinned at their feasting. And the devil came up to God with the sons of God and told the Lord that he had traveled over all the earth and beneath the earth. “Have you seen my servant Job?” God asked him. And God boasted to the devil, pointing to his great and holy servant. The devil smiled scornfully at God's words: “Give him over to me, and you will see your servant complain and curse your name.” And God gave his righteous man, whom he loved so dearly, over to the devil, and the devil struck down his children and his livestock and scattered his wealth, all at once, as though by God's thunderbolt. Job tore his garments, threw himself to the ground and cried, “Naked I came from my mother's womb, and naked I shall return to the earth. God gave, and God has taken away. Blessed be the name of the Lord, now and forever!”

Fathers and teachers, bear with my tears now, for my whole childhood seems to rise before me again, and I breathe as I breathed then with my little eight-year-old chest, and feel the same wonder, bewilderment and joy. Those camels so possessed my imagination then, and Satan speaking to God like that, and God giving his servant over to destruction, and his servant crying, “Blessed be your name, even though you punish me”—and then the quiet, sweet singing in the church, “Let my prayer be set forth,” and once more the incense from the priest's censer, and the prayer on bended knees! Ever since then—I took it up again only yesterday—I cannot read this most holy story without tears. And how much there is in it that is great, mysterious, unimaginable! Later I heard the proud words of scoffers and blasphemers: how could the Lord give the best-loved of his saints to the devil for his amusement, take his children away, strike him with disease and sores so that he scraped the pus from his wounds with a potsherd? And for what? Merely to boast before Satan: “See what my saint can endure for me!” But its greatness lies precisely in its mystery: here the passing face of this earth and eternal truth have touched. Before earthly justice, eternal justice acts. Here the Creator, as in the first days of creation, when he ended each day with the praise, “What I have made is good,” looks upon Job and again takes pride in his creation. And Job, praising the Lord, serves not only him but will serve all his creation, from generation to generation and forever and ever, for that was what he was destined to do.

Lord, what a book it is, and what lessons it holds! What a book Holy Scripture is, what a miracle and what power are given to humankind through it! It is like a sculpted image of the world and man and human characters, everything named and shown for all eternity. And how many mysteries are resolved and revealed: God restores Job, gives him wealth again, many years pass, and now he has new children, different children, and he loves them—Lord! “But how could he love these new ones, it seems, when the others are gone, when he has lost them? Remembering the others, can he be as completely happy with the new ones as he was before, however dear they may be to him?” But he can, he can: through the great mystery of human life, old grief gradually passes into quiet, tender joy. In place of youth's seething blood comes a gentle, serene old age. I bless the sunrise every day, and my heart sings to it as before, but now I love the sunset more, its long slanting rays, and with them the quiet, gentle, tender memories, the dear images from a whole long and blessed life—and above it all God's truth, moving us to tenderness, reconciling us, forgiving all! My life is ending, I know it and feel it, but with every day left to me I feel my earthly life already touching a new, infinite, unknown life, yet one that is fast approaching, and at the foretaste of it my soul trembles with delight, my mind shines and my heart weeps for joy...

Friends and teachers, I have often heard—and lately it has become still more audible—how God's priests among us, especially the village priests, complain everywhere with tears of their meager income and their humiliation, and openly declare, even in print—I have read it myself—that they supposedly can no longer explain Scripture to the people because they are paid too little, and if Lutherans and heretics come and begin drawing away their flock, let them draw it away, for we are paid too little. Lord! I think, may God grant them more of this income they prize so much—for their complaint is just—but truly I say: if anyone is to blame, half the blame is ours! Granted, he has no time; granted, he speaks justly when he says he is constantly weighed down with work and the services he must perform—but surely not all the time. Surely he has at least one hour in the whole week to remember God. And the work does not last the whole year. Let him gather people in his home once a week, in the evening, even if it is only the children at first; the fathers will hear of it and begin to come too. And there is no need to build a mansion for it: simply welcome them into your cottage. Don't be afraid, they won't ruin your cottage; you are only gathering them for an hour. Let him open this book and begin to read, without learned words or self-importance, without setting himself above them, but tenderly and gently, rejoicing himself in reading to them and in their listening and understanding, loving these words himself. Only pause now and then to explain a word a common man would not understand. Don't worry, they will understand everything, the Orthodox heart will understand everything!

Read to them of Abraham and Sarah, of Isaac and Rebekah, of how Jacob came to Laban and wrestled in his sleep with the Lord and said, “How dreadful is this place”—and you will astonish the devout mind of the common man. Read to them, and especially to the children, of how the brothers sold their own brother into slavery, the dear boy Joseph, the dreamer and great prophet, and told their father that a wild beast had torn his son to pieces, showing him the bloodied garment. Read how the brothers later came to Egypt for grain, and Joseph, now a great court dignitary, whom they did not recognize, tormented them, accused them, detained their brother Benjamin, and all out of love: “I love you, and loving you, I torment you.” For all his life he had never ceased to remember how they had sold him to merchants somewhere out in the hot steppe beside a well, and how he had wrung his hands and wept and begged his brothers not to sell him as a slave into a foreign land. And now, seeing them after so many years, he loved them again beyond measure, but he distressed them and tormented them, always loving them. At last, unable to endure the anguish of his own heart, he leaves them, throws himself onto his bed and weeps; then he wipes his face and comes out radiant and bright and declares, “Brothers, I am Joseph, your brother!” Let him read on, how the old man Jacob rejoiced to learn that his dear boy was still alive, and set out for Egypt, leaving even his own country, and died in a foreign land, declaring for all eternity in his last testament the greatest word, which had dwelt mysteriously in his gentle, fearful heart throughout his life: that from his line, from Judah, would come the great hope of the world, its reconciler and savior!

Fathers and teachers, forgive me, and do not be angry that I speak like a little child of things you have long known and could teach me a hundred times more skillfully and beautifully. I speak only from rapture; forgive my tears, for I love this book! Let him too, God's priest, weep, and he will see his listeners' hearts shaken in response. Only a little seed is needed, a tiny one: let him cast it into the common man's soul, and it will not die, it will live in his soul all his life, hidden within him amid the darkness, amid the stench of his sins, like a point of light, like a great reminder. There is no need, no need for much explanation and teaching; he will understand it all simply. Do you think the common man will not understand? Try reading on to him the touching, tender story of the beautiful Esther and the proud Vashti, or the wondrous tale of the prophet Jonah in the whale's belly. Do not forget the Lord's parables either, chiefly from the Gospel of Luke—as I used to do—and then, from the Acts of the Apostles, the conversion of Saul: that without fail, without fail! And finally, from the calendar of saints' lives, at least the life of Alexei the Man of God and of that greatest of the great, the joyful sufferer, the seer of God and bearer of Christ, Mother Mary of Egypt. You will pierce his heart with these simple tales, and it takes only an hour a week, however small your income, one little hour. He will see for himself that our people are kind and grateful, and will repay him a hundredfold. Remembering the priest's care and tender words, they will willingly help him in his field, help him in his home, and show him greater respect than before—and there, his income has already increased. It is so simplehearted a thing that sometimes we are afraid even to say it, for people will laugh at us, and yet how certain it is! He who does not believe in God will not believe in God's people either. But he who has come to believe in God's people will see their holiness, even if he himself had never believed in it before. Only the people and their coming spiritual power will convert our atheists who have torn themselves away from their native soil. And what is the word of Christ without an example? Without the word of God the people will perish, for their souls thirst for the word and for every experience of beauty.

In my youth, long ago, nearly forty years now, Father Anfim and I traveled all over Russia collecting alms for the monastery, and once we spent the night with some fishermen on the bank of a great navigable river. A good-looking young peasant sat down with us, a youth who looked about eighteen; he was hurrying to reach his place the next day to haul a merchant's barge by a towrope. I saw him looking ahead, tender and clear-eyed. It was a bright, quiet, warm July night, the river broad, mist rising from it and cooling us, a little fish splashing softly; the birds had fallen silent, everything was quiet and beautiful, everything praying to God. Only the two of us were awake, that young man and I, and we began talking of the beauty of this world of God's and its great mystery. Every blade of grass, every little insect, the ant, the golden bee—all know their way so wonderfully, though they have no reason; they bear witness to God's mystery and constantly fulfill it themselves. And I saw the dear young man's heart catch fire. He told me that he loved the forest and its birds; he was a birdcatcher, understood every call and knew how to lure every bird. “I know nothing better than being in the forest,” he said, “and everything is good.” “Truly,” I answered, “everything is good and glorious, because everything is truth. Look,” I told him, “at the horse, that great animal so close to man, or at the ox that feeds him and labors for him, head bowed, thoughtful. Look at their faces: what gentleness, what devotion to man, who often beats them without mercy, what innocence, what trust, and what beauty in that face. It is moving even to know that there is no sin in the animal, for all is perfect, all except man is without sin, and Christ is with them before he is with us.” “Can it really be,” the young man asked, “that Christ is with them too?” “How could it be otherwise?” I said. “For the word is for everyone; all creation, every creature, every little leaf strives toward the word, sings God's glory, weeps to Christ, doing this without knowing it through the mystery of its sinless life. There,” I told him, “a terrible bear wanders in the forest, menacing and fierce, and entirely innocent of being so.” And I told him how a bear once came to a great saint who was seeking salvation in a little cell in the forest, and the great saint felt tenderness for it, went out fearlessly and gave it a piece of bread. “Go,” he said, “Christ be with you,” and the fierce beast went away obediently and gently, doing no harm. The young man was moved that it had gone away without doing any harm, and that Christ was with it too. “Oh, how good that is,” he said, “how good and wonderful everything of God's is!” He sat there lost in thought, quiet and sweet. I saw that he had understood. And he fell asleep beside me in a light, sinless sleep. Lord, bless the young! And I myself prayed for him then as I went to sleep. Lord, send peace and light to your people!

### c) Memories of the Elder Zosima's Youth and Early Manhood in the World. The Duel

<!-- B06-C02-P005 -->
I spent a long time in Petersburg, nearly eight years in the cadet corps, and my new upbringing smothered many of my childhood impressions, though I forgot nothing. In exchange I acquired so many new habits and even opinions that I became an almost savage, cruel and absurd creature. I acquired a polish of courtesy and social manners along with the French language, but all of us regarded the soldiers who served us in the corps as little better than animals, and I did too. Perhaps I more than anyone, for I was the most impressionable of all my comrades. When we became officers, we were ready to shed our blood for our regiment's wounded honor, yet scarcely one of us knew what true honor was, and had he learned, he would have been the first to laugh at it. We almost prided ourselves on drunkenness, brawling and swaggering. I will not say we were bad men; all those young men were good, but their behavior was bad, mine most of all. The chief thing was that I now had money of my own, and so I set out to enjoy myself with all the eagerness of youth, without restraint, under full sail. But here is something strange: I read books then, and with great pleasure too. Only the Bible I scarcely ever opened in those days, yet I never parted with it and carried it everywhere. Truly, without knowing it, I was keeping that book “for the day and the hour, for the month and the year.”

After some four years of service I found myself at last in the town of K., where our regiment was then stationed. The town's society was varied, numerous and merry, hospitable and wealthy, and I was well received everywhere, for I was cheerful by nature and, besides, reputed to be well off, which means a great deal in society. And then something happened that was the beginning of everything. I became attached to a beautiful young woman, intelligent and worthy, with a bright, noble character, the daughter of respected parents. They were people of consequence, with wealth, influence and power, and they received me warmly and kindly. And I took it into my head that the young woman felt a heartfelt affection for me; my heart blazed at the thought. Only later did I understand and fully realize that perhaps I had not loved her so very deeply at all, but merely esteemed her intelligence and her lofty character, as I could hardly help doing. My selfishness, however, prevented me from proposing at the time: it seemed hard and frightening to part with the temptations of a dissolute, free bachelor's life while still so young, and with money into the bargain. I did drop hints, however. In any case, I put off any decisive step for a little while.

Just then I was sent on duty to another district for two months. I returned after two months and suddenly learned that the young woman was already married, to a wealthy landowner from near the town, a man some years older than I but still young, who had connections in the capital and the best society, which I did not, and who was very agreeable and educated into the bargain, whereas I had no education at all. This unexpected turn struck me so hard that my mind was clouded. The main thing was that, as I learned at the same time, this young landowner had long been her fiancé, and I myself had met him many times at their house but had noticed nothing, blinded by my own merits. And this was what wounded me most: how could almost everyone have known, while I alone knew nothing? Suddenly I felt an unbearable rage. Blushing, I began to remember how many times I had almost declared my love to her, and since she had not stopped or warned me, it followed, I concluded, that she had been laughing at me. Later, of course, I reflected and remembered that she had not laughed at me at all, but on the contrary had playfully broken off such conversations and begun others instead. At the time I could not see this and burned for revenge. I remember with astonishment how burdensome and repulsive this revenge and anger were to me myself, for, being easygoing by nature, I could not stay angry with anyone for long; so I almost had to inflame myself deliberately, and finally became ugly and absurd.

I waited for my chance, and once, at a large gathering, I managed to insult my “rival” over an apparently quite unrelated matter, ridiculing an opinion he had expressed about an important event of that time—it was in 1826—and people said I had done it cleverly and wittily. Then I forced a confrontation with him and treated him so rudely during it that he accepted my challenge, despite the great difference between us, for I was younger, of no consequence and low in rank. Later I learned for certain that he too had accepted my challenge from something like jealousy: even before, he had been a little jealous of me in connection with his wife, then still his fiancée. Now he thought that if she learned he had borne an insult from me and had not dared to challenge me to a duel, she might involuntarily begin to despise him and her love might falter. I soon found a second, a comrade, a lieutenant in our own regiment. Duels were severely prosecuted then, but they were almost a fashion among military men—such savage prejudices sometimes spring up and take root. It was late June, and our meeting was set for the next morning, outside town, at seven—and truly, something almost fateful happened to me then.

Returning home the evening before, savage and ugly, I grew angry with my orderly, Afanasy, and struck him twice in the face with all my strength, until his face was bloody. He had not been serving me long, and I had hit him before, but never with such bestial cruelty. And would you believe it, my dear ones, forty years have passed, and even now I remember it with shame and pain. I went to bed, slept for three hours and woke as day was beginning. I got up at once; I no longer wanted to sleep. I went to the window and opened it—it looked onto the garden—and saw the sun rising. It was warm and beautiful, the birds were singing. What is this, I thought, this feeling of something shameful and base in my soul? Is it because I am going to shed blood? No, I thought, that doesn't seem to be it. Is it because I am afraid of death, afraid of being killed? No, not that at all, not that at all... And suddenly I understood what it was: I had beaten Afanasy the night before! It all appeared before me again at once, as though happening anew: he stood before me, and I struck him full in the face with all my force, while he kept his hands at his sides, his head straight, his eyes wide as though on parade, shuddering with every blow and not even daring to raise a hand to shield himself—and a human being had been brought to this, a human being was beating another human being! What a crime! It was as if a sharp needle had pierced right through my soul. I stood there dazed, while the sun shone, the leaves rejoiced and glittered, and the birds, the birds praised God... I covered my face with both hands, fell onto my bed and sobbed aloud.

And then I remembered my brother Markel and his words to the servants before he died: “My dear, dear ones, why do you serve me, why do you love me? Am I worthy to be served?” “Yes, am I worthy?” The thought suddenly sprang into my mind. What really makes me worthy to have another human being, just like me, made in God's image and likeness, serve me? For the first time in my life the question pierced my mind. “Mother, my own flesh and blood, truly each of us is guilty before everyone, for everyone. People simply do not know it, and if they knew it, paradise would come at once!” “Lord, can this too be untrue?” I thought, weeping. “Truly, perhaps I am guiltier than anyone for everyone, and worse than anyone in the world!” Suddenly the whole truth appeared before me in all its light: what was I going to do? I was going to kill a good, intelligent, noble man who had done me no wrong, and so deprive his wife of happiness forever, torment and kill her. I lay there on the bed, facedown in the pillow, and never noticed the time passing.

Suddenly my comrade the lieutenant came in to fetch me, with the pistols. “Ah,” he said, “good, you're up already. It's time, let's go.” I became flustered, quite lost, but we went out to get into the carriage. “Wait here a moment,” I told him. “I'll just run back, I've forgotten my purse.” And I ran back into the lodgings alone, straight to Afanasy's little room. “Afanasy,” I said, “I struck you twice in the face yesterday. Forgive me.” He started as though frightened and looked at me—and I saw that it was not enough, not enough. Suddenly, just as I was, in my epaulettes, I threw myself at his feet, my forehead to the ground. “Forgive me!” I said. He was utterly stunned. “Your Honor, dear master, how can you... am I worthy...” And suddenly he began to cry just as I had earlier, covering his face with both hands, turning to the window and shaking all over with tears. He looked at me. “Well, my friend, you're a fine fellow. I can see you'll uphold the uniform.”

We arrived at the place, and they were already there, waiting. They placed us twelve paces apart, with the first shot to him. I stood before him cheerfully, face-to-face, without blinking, looking at him with love, knowing what I would do. He fired; it only grazed my cheek a little and touched my ear. “Thank God!” I cried. “You haven't killed a man!” And I seized my own pistol, turned around and flung it up into the woods. “That's where you belong!” I cried. I turned to my opponent. “Sir,” I said, “forgive me, a foolish young man, for offending you through my own fault and now forcing you to shoot at me. I am ten times worse than you, and perhaps still more. Tell that to the person you honor above everyone in the world.” The moment I said it, all three of them cried out. “Really,” said my opponent, even growing angry, “if you didn't want to fight, why put us to the trouble?” “Yesterday,” I told him cheerfully, “I was still a fool. Today I've grown wiser.” “I believe you about yesterday,” he said, “but from your views it's difficult to draw that conclusion about today.” “Bravo!” I cried, clapping my hands. “I agree with you there too. “All this is sensible and devout,” my opponent said, “and in any case you are an original.” “Laugh,” I said, laughing too, “and later you will praise me yourself.” “I'm ready to praise you now,” he said. “Here, let me offer you my hand, for I think you really are a sincere man.” “No,” I said, “not now. Later, when I've become better and earned your respect, then offer it—that will be right.” We returned home; my second scolded me all the way, and I kept kissing him. My comrades heard at once and gathered that same day to pass judgment on me: “He's disgraced the uniform. Let him resign.” Defenders appeared too: “He faced the shot all the same,” they said. “Yes, but he was afraid of further shots and asked forgiveness at the barrier.” “If he'd been afraid of shots,” his defenders objected, “he'd have fired his own pistol first, before asking forgiveness. But he threw it into the woods still loaded. You can't put a monk on trial!” They laughed and could not stop, not mockingly at all but warmly, cheerfully. Suddenly they all grew fond of me, even my fiercest accusers, and for the whole month afterward, until my resignation came through, they almost carried me in their arms. “Ah, you monk!” they would say. Everyone had a kind word for me. They began trying to dissuade me, even pitying me: “What are you doing to yourself?” “No,” they would say, “he's a brave one. He faced the shot and could have fired his own pistol, but he had a dream the night before that he should become a monk. That's why.”

Almost the same thing happened in the town's society. Before, they had taken little particular notice of me, merely receiving me cordially, but now everyone suddenly heard of me and vied with one another in inviting me. They laughed at me themselves, yet loved me. I should mention here that although everyone talked openly of our duel, the authorities let the matter drop, for my opponent was a close relative of our general, and since it had ended without bloodshed, almost as a joke, and I had resigned besides, they really did turn it into a joke. And I began to speak openly and fearlessly, despite their laughter, for it was kindly laughter, not malicious. Most of these conversations took place in the evenings among the ladies; women were more fond of listening to me then and made the men listen too. “But how can I be guilty for everyone?” they would all laugh in my face. “How could I be guilty for you, for example?” “How could you know it,” I answered, “when the whole world has long since taken another road, and we regard outright falsehood as truth and demand the same falsehood from others? Here, for once in my life, I acted sincerely, and what happened? I became a sort of holy fool to you all. Although you've grown fond of me, you still laugh at me.” “But how could we help loving someone like you?” my hostess said, laughing aloud, and there was a large gathering at her house.

Suddenly I saw rising from among the ladies the very young woman over whom I had issued the challenge and whom I had so recently imagined as my bride; I had not even noticed her arrival that evening. She rose, came up to me and held out her hand. “Allow me to tell you,” she said, “that I for one am not laughing at you. On the contrary, I thank you with tears and declare my respect for you for what you did that day.” Her husband came over too, and then suddenly everyone moved toward me, almost kissing me. I felt such joy, but above all I suddenly noticed one gentleman, already an older man, who also came up to me. I had known him by name before, but had never been acquainted with him and had not exchanged a word with him before that evening.

### d) The Mysterious Visitor

<!-- B06-C02-P006 -->
He had served in our town for many years, held a prominent position and was respected by everyone. He was wealthy and known for his charity, had donated a considerable sum to an almshouse and an orphanage, and besides this did many good deeds secretly, without publicity, all of which came to light after his death. He was about fifty, almost stern in appearance, and spoke little. He had been married for no more than ten years to a wife who was still young, and had three small children by her. <!-- B06-C02-P009 -->

For in our age everyone has split off into separate units; each man retreats into his own burrow, withdraws from others, hides himself and hides what he has, and ends by pushing himself away from people and pushing people away from him. <!-- B06-C02-P015 -->

<!-- B06-C02-P017 -->
“What are you saying?” I cried.

For a long time I did not believe him, nor did belief come all at once, but only after he had visited me for three days and told me everything in detail. I thought he was mad, but in the end, to my great sorrow and astonishment, I became fully convinced. Fourteen years before, he had committed a great and terrible crime against a wealthy lady, young and beautiful, a widowed landowner who had a house of her own in our town for her visits. Falling deeply in love with her, he had declared his love and tried to persuade her to marry him. But she had already given her heart to another, a distinguished military man of considerable rank, then away on campaign, whom she expected back soon. She refused his proposal and asked him not to visit her. He stopped visiting, but knowing the layout of her house, he made his way in one night from the garden, across the roof, with the greatest daring and at the risk of being discovered. But, as very often happens, crimes committed with extraordinary daring succeed more often than others. Entering the attic through a dormer window, he went down the little staircase into the living quarters, knowing that through the servants' carelessness the door at the foot of the stairs was not always locked. He relied on this negligence again, and found it just as he had hoped. Once inside the living quarters he made his way through the dark to her bedroom, where an icon lamp was burning. And as though on purpose, both her young maids had slipped out without permission to a neighbor's name-day party in the same street. The other menservants and maids were asleep in the servants' quarters and the kitchen downstairs. At the sight of her sleeping his passion flared, and then a vengeful, jealous rage seized his heart. I wanted to embrace and kiss him but did not dare, his face was so contorted and his gaze so heavy. He left. “Lord,” I thought, “where has that man gone?” I fell to my knees before the icon and wept for him to the Most Holy Mother of God, our swift intercessor and helper. I had been praying in tears for about half an hour, and it was already late at night, around twelve, when suddenly I saw the door open and he came in again. I was astonished.

<!-- B06-C02-P052 -->
“Where have you been?” I asked him.

<!-- B06-C02-P053 -->
“I,” he said, “I think I forgot something... my handkerchief, I think... Well, even if I haven't forgotten anything, let me sit down...”

<!-- B06-C02-P054 -->
He sat on a chair. I stood over him. “Sit down too,” he said. I sat. We sat for two minutes or so; he looked at me intently and suddenly smiled—I remembered this—then rose, embraced me tightly and kissed me...

<!-- B06-C02-P055 -->
“Remember,” he said, “how I came to you a second time. Do you hear? Remember this!”

<!-- B06-C02-P056 -->
It was the first time he had addressed me as an intimate. And he left. “Tomorrow,” I thought.

<!-- B06-C02-P057 -->
And so it happened. That evening I did not know that the next day was his birthday. I had not gone anywhere in the past few days and so could not have heard it from anyone. Every year there was a large gathering at his house on that day; the whole town came. They came this time too. And after dinner he stepped into the middle of the room with a paper in his hands, a formal report addressed to the authorities. Since his superiors were there, he read it aloud then and there to everyone assembled. It contained a full account of the crime in every detail. “As a monster I cast myself out from among mankind. God has visited me,” he concluded his paper. “I wish to suffer!” Then he brought out and laid on the table everything he thought would prove his crime, everything he had kept for fourteen years: the murdered woman's gold objects, which he had stolen to divert suspicion from himself; her locket and cross, taken from her neck—the locket contained her fiancé's portrait; a notebook; and, finally, two letters: a letter from her fiancé announcing his imminent arrival, and her answer, which she had begun but not finished, leaving it on the table to send to the post the following day. He had taken both letters with him—why? Why had he kept them afterward for fourteen years instead of destroying them as evidence?

And what happened? Everyone was astonished and horrified, and no one would believe him. They all listened with extraordinary curiosity, but as though listening to a sick man, and a few days later every household had firmly decided and pronounced that the unfortunate man had gone mad. The authorities and the court could not avoid proceeding with the case, but they too came to a halt: though the objects and letters he produced gave them pause, here too it was decided that even if these documents proved genuine, they alone could not support a definitive accusation. Besides, the woman herself might have entrusted all the objects to him as an acquaintance. I heard, however, that their authenticity was later verified through many of the murdered woman's friends and relatives and that there was no doubt about it. Yet once again the case was not destined to reach a conclusion. Some five days later everyone learned that the sufferer had fallen ill and that there were fears for his life. What illness he had I cannot explain; they spoke of a disorder of the heartbeat. But it became known that a council of doctors, at his wife's insistence, had also examined his mental condition and concluded that he was already deranged.

I revealed nothing, though people rushed to question me, but when I wished to visit him I was prevented for a long time, chiefly by his wife. “It was you who unsettled him,” she told me. “He was gloomy before, and in the past year everyone noticed his unusual agitation and strange behavior, but then you simply destroyed him. You wore him out with your reading; he spent a whole month at your place.” And indeed, not only his wife but everyone in town turned on me and blamed me. “It's all your doing,” they said. I kept silent, but rejoiced in my soul, for I saw God's unmistakable mercy toward the man who had risen against himself and punished himself. I could not believe he was mad. At last I too was admitted; he himself had insisted on it so that he could say goodbye to me. I went in and saw at once that not merely his days but his hours were numbered. He was weak and yellow, his hands trembled, he gasped for breath, yet he looked at me tenderly and joyfully.

<!-- B06-C02-P058 -->
“It is accomplished!” he told me. “I've longed to see you. Why didn't you come?”

<!-- B06-C02-P059 -->
I did not tell him that they had refused to let me in.

<!-- B06-C02-P060 -->
“God has taken pity on me and is calling me to him. I know I am dying, but for the first time in so many years I feel joy and peace. I felt paradise in my soul at once, as soon as I had done what I had to do. Now I dare to love my children and kiss them. They do not believe me; no one believed me, neither my wife nor my judges, and my children will never believe it either. I see God's mercy toward my children in this. I shall die, and my name will remain unblemished for them. And now I have a foretaste of God, my heart rejoices as though in paradise... I have done my duty...”

<!-- B06-C02-P061 -->
He could not speak, he was gasping, pressing my hand warmly and looking at me with burning eyes. But we did not talk long; his wife kept looking in on us. Still, he managed to whisper:

<!-- B06-C02-P062 -->
“Do you remember how I came back to you that second time, at midnight? I even told you to remember it. Do you know why I came in? I had come to kill you!”

<!-- B06-C02-P063 -->
I shuddered.

<!-- B06-C02-P064 -->
“I went out from your house into the darkness, wandered the streets and struggled with myself. Suddenly I hated you so much that my heart could scarcely bear it. ‘Now,’ I thought, ‘he alone has bound me, and he is my judge. I can no longer refuse tomorrow's punishment, because he knows everything.’ It was not that I feared you would inform on me—I never thought of that—but I thought, ‘How will I look at him if I do not inform on myself?’ Even if you were at the other end of the earth, but alive, it would be all the same: the thought that you were alive and knew everything and were judging me was unbearable. I hated you as though you were the cause of everything and to blame for it all. I came back to you then, remembering the dagger on your table. I sat down and asked you to sit, and thought for a whole minute. If I had killed you, I would still have been lost for that murder, even if I never confessed the earlier crime. But I did not think of that at all, and did not want to think of it at that moment. I only hated you and wished with all my strength to take revenge on you for everything. But my Lord overcame the devil in my heart. Know, though, that you have never been closer to death.”

<!-- B06-C02-P065 -->
A week later he died. The whole town followed his coffin to the grave. The archpriest gave a heartfelt address. They mourned the terrible illness that had cut short his days. But after the burial the whole town turned against me and even stopped receiving me. True, some people, few at first and then more and more, began to believe his confession was true, and came to see me often, questioning me with great curiosity and delight, for man loves the fall and disgrace of the righteous. But I fell silent and soon left the town for good, and five months later the Lord God granted me the grace to set foot on a firm and beautiful path, blessing the invisible finger that had shown me that path so plainly. And to this day I remember the much-suffering servant of God Mikhail in my prayers, every day.
