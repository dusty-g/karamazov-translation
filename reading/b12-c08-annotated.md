# Book XII: A Judicial Error

## VIII. A Treatise on Smerdyakov

<!-- B12-C08-P001 -->
“First, how did such a suspicion become possible?” Ippolit Kirillovich began with this question. “The first person to shout that Smerdyakov had killed him was the defendant himself, at the moment of his arrest; yet from that first shout right up to this moment in court, he has produced not one fact to support his accusation—not merely no fact, but not so much as a reasonably intelligible hint of a fact. Only three people, moreover, support this accusation: the defendant’s two brothers and Madame Svetlova. But the elder of the defendant’s brothers declared his suspicion only today, while ill, in an unmistakable fit of derangement and fever; before that, throughout those two months, as we positively know, he fully shared the conviction of his brother’s guilt and did not even attempt to challenge it. We shall consider that separately a little later. Then the defendant’s younger brother told us himself a short while ago that he has no facts whatever, not the slightest, to support his belief in Smerdyakov’s guilt, and bases his conclusion solely on the defendant’s own words and ‘the expression on his face’—yes, his brother offered us this stupendous piece of evidence twice just now. Madame Svetlova’s statement was, perhaps, even more stupendous: ‘Believe whatever the defendant tells you; he’s not the sort of man to lie.’ That is all the factual evidence against Smerdyakov from these three people, who have far too great a personal stake in the defendant’s fate. And yet the accusation against Smerdyakov circulated and persisted, and persists still—can one believe it, can one imagine it?”

Here Ippolit Kirillovich thought it necessary to give a brief sketch of the late Smerdyakov’s character, “who ended his life in a fit of morbid derangement and insanity.” He portrayed him as a feeble-minded man with the beginnings of a little confused education, bewildered by philosophical ideas beyond his understanding and frightened by certain modern doctrines concerning duty and obligation, which had been amply taught him in practice by the reckless life of his late master and perhaps father, Fyodor Pavlovich, and in theory by various strange philosophical conversations with his master’s elder son, Ivan Fyodorovich, who readily indulged in this amusement—probably out of boredom, or a need to mock for which he had found no better outlet. “He told me himself about his state of mind during the last days he spent in his master’s house,” Ippolit Kirillovich explained, “but others testify to the same thing: the defendant himself, his brother, and even the servant Grigory—that is, all those who must have known him very well. Besides, afflicted with epilepsy, Smerdyakov was ‘as cowardly as a chicken.’ ‘He fell at my feet and kissed my feet,’ the defendant himself told us at a time when he did not yet realize that such a statement might be to his disadvantage. ‘He’s a chicken with the falling sickness,’ he said of him in his characteristic language. And this is the very man the defendant chooses as his confidant—by his own testimony—and intimidates until he finally agrees to serve as his spy and informer. In this role of household spy he betrays his master, tells the defendant both of the existence of the packet of money and of the signals by which he can gain admission to his master—and how could he have failed to tell him? ‘He’d kill me, sir, I could see plainly that he’d kill me, sir,’ he said during the investigation, shaking and trembling even before us, although the tormentor who had terrified him was by then under arrest himself and could no longer come to punish him. ‘He suspected me every minute, sir; I was in fear and trembling myself, and hurried to tell him every secret, just to appease his anger, sir, so that he could see I was innocent of doing him any wrong, sir, and let me live to repent, sir.’ Those are his own words; I wrote them down and remembered them: ‘Whenever he shouted at me, I’d drop to my knees before him.’ A young man of the utmost natural honesty, who had thereby gained the trust of his master, who recognized that honesty when he returned some money the master had lost, the unfortunate Smerdyakov must, one supposes, have suffered terrible remorse at betraying his master, whom he loved as his benefactor. According to the most profound psychiatrists, severe sufferers from epilepsy are always inclined to ceaseless and, of course, morbid self-accusation. They torment themselves over their ‘guilt’ for something and before someone, suffer pangs of conscience, often exaggerate their various offenses and crimes, and even invent them for themselves, without any grounds whatever. And here just such a person really does become guilty and criminal through fear and intimidation. Moreover, he had a strong foreboding that something bad might come of the circumstances taking shape before his eyes. When Fyodor Pavlovich’s elder son, Ivan Fyodorovich, was leaving for Moscow just before the catastrophe, Smerdyakov begged him to stay, yet, true to his cowardly habit, did not dare express all his fears clearly and categorically. He contented himself with hints, but the hints were not understood. It must be noted that in Ivan Fyodorovich he saw, as it were, his protection, a kind of guarantee that as long as he was at home, no disaster would occur. Remember the phrase in Dmitri Karamazov’s ‘drunken’ letter: ‘I’ll kill the old man, if only Ivan leaves’; evidently Ivan Fyodorovich’s presence seemed to everyone a sort of guarantee of peace and order in the house. And now this very man leaves, and immediately, scarcely an hour after the young master’s departure, Smerdyakov falls into an epileptic seizure. But that is perfectly understandable. Here it must be mentioned that, oppressed by his fears and a kind of despair, Smerdyakov had been particularly conscious in those last days that epileptic seizures might be approaching; they had always overtaken him before at moments of emotional strain and shock. The day and hour of such attacks cannot, of course, be predicted, but every epileptic can sense beforehand a predisposition to a seizure. That is what medicine tells us. And so Ivan Fyodorovich has scarcely driven out of the yard when Smerdyakov, under the impression of his orphaned state, so to speak, and his defenselessness, goes to the cellar on a household errand, descends the steps, and thinks: ‘Will I have a fit or won’t I? What if it comes now?’ And it is precisely this state of mind, this apprehension, these questions, that bring on the spasm in his throat which always precedes a seizure, and he plunges headlong, unconscious, to the bottom of the cellar. And in this most natural of accidents people contrive to see

some ground for suspicion, some indication, some hint that he deliberately pretended to be ill! But if it was deliberate, the question immediately arises: what for? With what calculation, for what purpose? I am not even speaking of medicine: science lies, they say, science is mistaken, the doctors could not distinguish the real thing from a pretense—very well, very well, but answer my question all the same: why should he pretend? Was it so that, having planned a murder, he could use this seizure to draw the household’s attention to himself beforehand, and as quickly as possible? You see, gentlemen of the jury, five people were in or passed through Fyodor Pavlovich’s house on the night of the crime: first, Fyodor Pavlovich himself, but clearly he did not kill himself; second, his servant Grigory, but he was almost killed himself; third, Grigory’s wife, the servant Marfa Ignatyeva, but it is simply shameful to imagine her murdering her master. That leaves two people in view: the defendant and Smerdyakov. But since the defendant insists that he did not kill him, it must therefore have been Smerdyakov; there is no other way out, because no one else can be found, no other murderer can be selected. So that, that is the origin of this ‘ingenious’ and stupendous accusation against the unfortunate idiot who killed himself yesterday! Solely because there is no one else to select! If there were even a shadow, even a suspicion, against someone else, some sixth person, I am convinced that even the defendant himself would then be ashamed to point to Smerdyakov and would point to that sixth person instead, because accusing Smerdyakov of this murder is utterly absurd.

“Gentlemen, let us set aside psychology, let us set aside medicine, let us even set aside logic itself, and turn only to the facts, to the facts alone, and see what the facts tell us. Smerdyakov killed him—but how? Alone, or in partnership with the defendant? Let us first consider the first case, that Smerdyakov commits the murder alone. Of course, if he killed him, it must have been for something, for some advantage. But without the shadow of any of the defendant’s motives for murder—hatred, jealousy, and so on and so forth—Smerdyakov could undoubtedly have killed only for money, to make off with those very three thousand rubles which he had himself watched his master put in the packet. And now, having planned a murder, he tells another person beforehand—and a person with an exceptionally strong interest, namely the defendant—all the details concerning the money and the signals: where the packet lies, exactly what is written on it, what it is wrapped in, and above all, above all, he tells him about those ‘signals’ by which he can gain admission to his master. What, does he do this simply to give himself away? Or to find himself a rival who may also wish to go in and acquire the packet? Yes, I shall be told, but he told him out of fear. How can that be? A man who did not flinch from planning such a fearless and savage deed, and then carrying it out, passes on information which he alone in the whole world possesses, information which, had he only kept silent, no one in the whole world would ever have guessed. No, however cowardly a man may be, once he has conceived such a deed he would certainly tell no one, at least about the packet and the signals, because that would mean giving himself away before anything else. He would invent something expressly, tell some other lie if information was absolutely demanded of him, but he would keep silent about this! On the contrary, I repeat, if he had kept silent even just about the money, and had then killed and taken that money for himself, no one in the whole world could ever have accused him, at least, of murder for the purpose of robbery, since no one but he had seen the money, no one knew it existed in the house. Even if he were accused, it would necessarily be supposed that he had killed for some other motive. But since no one had previously observed any such motives in him, and everyone had seen, on the contrary, that his master was fond of him and honored him with his trust, naturally he would be the last person suspected; suspicion would fall first on someone who did have those motives, who himself shouted that he had them, who did not conceal them but displayed them before everyone—in a word, suspicion would fall on the murdered man’s son, Dmitri Fyodorovich. Smerdyakov would have killed and robbed, and the son would have been accused—surely that would have suited Smerdyakov the murderer? Well, it is this very son, Dmitri, whom Smerdyakov, after planning a murder, tells beforehand about the money, the packet, and the signals—how logical, how clear it all is!

“The day of Smerdyakov’s intended murder arrives, and he falls down, pretending to have an epileptic seizure. Why? Certainly so that, first, the servant Grigory, who had planned to take his treatment, might, on seeing that there was absolutely no one to watch the house, postpone that treatment and sit up on guard. Second, of course, so that his master himself, seeing that no one was guarding him and greatly fearing his son’s arrival, a fear he did not conceal, would become even more suspicious and cautious. Finally, and chiefly, of course, so that he, Smerdyakov, struck down by the seizure, would immediately be carried from the kitchen, where he always slept apart from everyone else and had his own separate way in and out, to the other end of the wing, to Grigory’s little room, behind their partition, three paces from their own bed—as had always happened, from time immemorial, whenever epilepsy struck him, on the orders of his master and the tenderhearted Marfa Ignatyevna. Lying there behind the partition, he would most likely, of course, begin groaning to make himself seem more convincingly ill—that is, waking them all night long, as indeed happened, according to Grigory and his wife—and all this, all this, so that it would be more convenient for him suddenly to get up and then murder his master!

“But I may be told that he pretended precisely so that, as a sick man, he would not be suspected, and that he told the defendant about the money and the signals precisely to tempt him to come and kill the man himself; and when, you see, the defendant has killed him, leaves, and takes the money away, perhaps making a noise and a commotion and waking witnesses in the process, then, you see, Smerdyakov too will get up and go—well, go to do what? Why, precisely to kill his master a second time and carry off, a second time, the money already carried off. Gentlemen, you are laughing? I am ashamed myself to make such suppositions, and yet, imagine it, this is precisely what the defendant maintains: after me, he says, when I had already left the house, knocked Grigory down, and raised the alarm, he got up, went, killed, and robbed. I am not even speaking of how Smerdyakov could have calculated all this beforehand and foreseen every detail as plainly as his own fingers: namely, that the enraged, furious son would come solely to look respectfully through the window and, though he possessed the signals, retreat, leaving him, Smerdyakov, all the spoils! Gentlemen, I put the question seriously: where is the moment at which Smerdyakov committed his crime? Identify that moment, because without it no accusation is possible.

“‘But perhaps the epilepsy was real. The sick man suddenly came to, heard a cry, and went out’—well, and then? He looked and said to himself: why don’t I go and murder the master? And how did he know what had happened, what was going on, when he had been unconscious until then? But, gentlemen, even fantasy has its limits.

“‘Very good,’ the subtle minds will say, ‘but what if the two were in agreement, what if they both committed the murder together and divided the money? What then?’

“Yes, a serious suspicion indeed, and, to begin with, stupendous evidence immediately supports it: one man kills and takes all the trouble upon himself, while the other accomplice lies on his side, pretending to have epilepsy—precisely in order to arouse everyone’s suspicions beforehand, to alarm the master, to alarm Grigory. One wonders what motives could have led the two accomplices to devise this particular mad plan. But perhaps Smerdyakov’s complicity was not active at all, but passive and suffering, so to speak: perhaps the intimidated Smerdyakov merely agreed not to resist the murder and, foreseeing that he himself would be accused of allowing his master to be killed, of not crying out or resisting, secured Dmitri Karamazov’s permission in advance to spend that time lying as though in a seizure: ‘You do your murdering there however you like; I’ll keep out of it.’ But even if that were so, since, once again, the seizure was bound to cause an uproar in the house, Dmitri Karamazov, foreseeing this, could never have agreed to such an arrangement. But I concede the point: let us say he agreed. Even then it would still follow that Dmitri Karamazov was the murderer, the actual murderer and instigator, while Smerdyakov was merely a passive participant, indeed not even a participant, but someone who tolerated it through fear and against his will; the court would certainly have been able to distinguish the two. And what do we see? Scarcely has the defendant been arrested than he immediately shifts everything onto Smerdyakov alone and accuses him alone. He does not accuse him of complicity with himself, but him alone: he did it alone, he says, he murdered and robbed, it was his doing! What sort of accomplices immediately begin accusing one another? It simply never happens. And consider the risk to Karamazov: he is the principal murderer, the other is not; the other merely tolerated it and lay behind the partition, yet he shifts the blame onto the man lying there. Why, that man lying there could have become angry and, solely to preserve himself, hurried to tell the whole truth: we both took part, he would say, only I did not kill, I merely allowed it and tolerated it, out of fear. He, Smerdyakov, could surely have understood that the court would at once distinguish the degree of his guilt, and could therefore have calculated that, even if he were punished, it would be incomparably more lightly than the principal murderer, who wanted to shift everything onto him. But in that case he would inevitably have confessed. Yet we saw nothing of the kind. Smerdyakov did not breathe a word about complicity, although the murderer firmly accused him and kept pointing to him as the sole murderer. More than that: it was Smerdyakov himself who revealed to the investigators that he had told the defendant about the packet of money and the signals, and that without him the defendant would have known nothing. If he had really been an accomplice and guilty, would he have told the investigators so readily that he himself had given all this information to the defendant? On the contrary, he would have begun denying things and would certainly have distorted the facts and played them down. But he neither distorted nor played them down. Only an innocent man can act in that way, a man unafraid of being accused of complicity. And then yesterday, in a fit of morbid melancholy brought on by his epilepsy and by this whole catastrophe that had broken upon him, he hanged himself. In hanging himself, he left a note written in a peculiar style: ‘I destroy myself of my own will and desire, so that no one should be blamed.’ What would it have cost him to add: I am the murderer, not Karamazov? But he did not add it: did he have enough conscience for the one thing but not for the other?

“And what happens? A short while ago money is brought here, into court, three thousand rubles—‘the very money,’ we are told, ‘that lay in this very packet on the table of material evidence; I received it yesterday from Smerdyakov.’ But you, gentlemen of the jury, remember that sad scene yourselves. I shall not repeat its details, yet I will permit myself just two or three observations, choosing among the most insignificant—precisely because they are insignificant and therefore will not occur to everyone and will be forgotten. First, and once again: Smerdyakov returned the money yesterday out of remorse, and hanged himself. For without remorse he would not have returned the money. And surely it was only yesterday evening that he first confessed his crime to Ivan Karamazov, as Ivan Karamazov himself stated; otherwise why would he have remained silent until now? So he confessed: why then, I repeat again, did he not tell us the whole truth in his suicide note, knowing that the innocent defendant would face his terrible judgment the very next day? Money alone is no proof. For example, I and two other people in this room learned, quite by chance, a week ago, of one fact: that Ivan Fyodorovich Karamazov had sent two five-percent bonds, of five thousand rubles each, to be cashed in the provincial capital—a total, then, of ten thousand. I say this only to point out that anyone may happen to have money at a given time, and that bringing in three thousand cannot necessarily prove that this is the very same money, from that very drawer or packet. Finally, having received so important a communication yesterday from the real murderer, Ivan Karamazov remains at ease. But why did he not report it immediately? Why did he put everything off until morning? I think I am entitled to conjecture why. His health already impaired for a week, having himself admitted to his doctor and those close to him that he was seeing visions, that he was meeting people who were already dead, on the eve of the delirium tremens that struck him today, he suddenly learns of Smerdyakov’s death and at once reasons as follows: ‘The man is dead; I can accuse him and save my brother. And I have money: I’ll take a bundle and say Smerdyakov gave it to me before he died.’ You will say that this is dishonest; even if the man is dead, it is dishonest to lie, even to save a brother? True, but what if he lied unconsciously, if he himself imagined it had happened that way, precisely because the news of the servant’s sudden death had finally shattered his reason? You saw the scene just now, you saw what a state the man was in. He stood on his feet and spoke, but where was his mind? The feverish man’s testimony was followed by a document, the defendant’s letter to Madame Verkhovtsev, written two days before the crime, with a detailed program of that crime in advance. Why, then, are we looking for a program and its authors? It happened exactly according to that program, and was carried out by none other than its author. Yes, gentlemen of the jury, ‘it happened just as written!’ And we certainly, certainly did not run respectfully and timidly away from our father’s window, still firmly convinced that our beloved was with him at that very moment. No, that is absurd and unbelievable. He went in and—finished the business. Probably he killed in a fit of anger, inflamed with hatred at the first sight of his enemy and rival; but having killed him, perhaps with a single blow, a single swing of the hand armed with the brass pestle, and only afterward, following a thorough search, having satisfied himself that she was not there, he nevertheless did not forget to slip his hand under the pillow and take out the envelope of money, whose torn wrapper now lies here on the table of material evidence. I say this so that you will notice one circumstance which I consider extremely characteristic. Had this been an experienced murderer, and a murderer whose sole purpose was robbery, would he have left the envelope’s wrapper on the floor, as it was found beside the corpse? Had it been Smerdyakov, for example, killing for robbery, he would simply have taken the entire packet with him without bothering to unseal it over his victim’s corpse, since he knew for certain that there was money in the packet—it had been put in and sealed in his presence. And had he taken the packet away altogether, would anyone have known whether there had been a robbery? I ask you, gentlemen of the jury, would Smerdyakov have acted like that, would he have left the envelope on the floor? No, that is precisely how a frenzied murderer, already reasoning badly, was bound to act—a murderer who was no thief and had never stolen anything before, and even now, in snatching the money from under the bedding, was not stealing it as a thief but taking back his own property from the thief who had stolen it. For those were precisely Dmitri Karamazov’s ideas concerning these three thousand rubles, ideas that had grown into a mania. And so, seizing the packet, which he had never seen before, he tears the wrapper to make sure the money is there, then runs with the money in his pocket, forgetting even to consider that he is leaving on the floor the most stupendous accusation against himself in the shape of the torn wrapper. All because Karamazov, not Smerdyakov, failed to think, failed to work it out—and how could he have! He runs away; he hears the cry of the servant overtaking him; the servant seizes him, stops him, and falls, struck by the brass pestle. The defendant jumps down to him out of pity. Imagine, he suddenly assures us that he jumped down to him then out of pity, out of compassion, to see if there was anything he could do to help him. Was that a moment for showing such compassion? No, he jumped down precisely to make sure whether the sole witness to his crime was alive. Any other feeling, any other motive would have been unnatural! Observe: he busies himself over Grigory, wipes his head with a handkerchief, and, having satisfied himself that he is dead, runs back, distracted and covered in blood, to his beloved’s house—how could he not have thought that he was covered in blood and would be exposed immediately? But the defendant himself assures us that he did not even notice he was covered in blood. That can be admitted, that is very possible, that always happens to criminals at such moments. On one point, diabolical calculation; on another, not enough presence of mind. But at that moment he thought of only one thing: where was she? He had to find out as quickly as possible where she was, and so he runs to her lodgings and learns something unexpected and stupendous for him: she has gone to Mokroye with her ‘former,’ her ‘indisputable’ lover!”
