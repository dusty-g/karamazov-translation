# Book VIII: Mitya
## II. Pointer

<!-- B08-C02-P001 -->
So he had to “gallop off,” yet he still hadn’t a kopeck for horses—or rather he had two twenty-kopeck pieces, and that was all, all that remained of so many years of former prosperity! But at home he had an old silver watch that had long since stopped working. He seized it and took it to a Jewish watchmaker who had a little shop in the marketplace. The man gave him six rubles for it. “I didn’t expect even that!” cried the delighted Mitya—he was still delighted with everything—seized his six rubles, and ran home. At home he supplemented the sum by borrowing three rubles from his landlords, who gladly gave them, although they were handing over the last of their money: that was how fond of him they were. In his ecstatic state Mitya told them then and there that his fate was being decided, and related, in a terrible hurry of course, nearly the whole “plan” he had just laid before Samsonov, followed by Samsonov’s decision, his own hopes for the future, and so on and so forth. The landlords had already been let in on many of his secrets, which was why they looked on him as one of their own, a gentleman with no airs at all. Having thus assembled nine rubles, Mitya sent for post horses to take him to Volovya station. But in this way the fact was remembered and established that “at noon on the eve of a certain event Mitya had not a kopeck, and to get money sold his watch and borrowed three rubles from his landlords, all in front of witnesses.”

<!-- B08-C02-P002 -->
I record that fact in advance; why I do so will be explained later.

<!-- B08-C02-P003 -->
As he sped toward Volovya station, although Mitya glowed with the joyful anticipation that at last he would finish and settle “all these affairs,” he nevertheless trembled with fear as well: what would happen to Grushenka now, in his absence? What if this very day she finally decided to go to Fyodor Pavlovich? That was why he had left without telling her and ordered his landlords on no account to disclose his whereabouts if anyone came asking for him. “I must, I absolutely must be back by this evening,” he repeated as he jolted along in the cart. “And perhaps bring this Pointer back here too… to execute this deed…” So Mitya dreamed with a sinking heart; but alas, his dreams were very far from being destined to come true according to his “plan.”

<!-- B08-C02-P004 -->
First, he was delayed after leaving Volovya station by a country road. The road proved to be eighteen versts long, not twelve. Second, he did not find the Ilyinskoye priest at home; he had gone to a neighboring village. By the time Mitya found him there, driving to this neighboring village with the same, now exhausted horses, it was almost night. The priest, a little man with a timid, gentle appearance, immediately explained that although this Pointer had stayed with him at first, he was now at Sukhoy Posyolok, spending the night in a forest watchman’s hut, because he was bargaining for timber there as well. Mitya urgently begged him to take him to Pointer at once, and “thereby, so to speak, save him.” The priest hesitated at first but agreed to accompany him to Sukhoy Posyolok, evidently feeling curious; unfortunately, however, he suggested going “on foot,” since it was only a verst or so, “a little over.” Mitya naturally agreed and set off with such long strides that the poor priest almost ran after him. He was not yet an old man, and was very cautious. Mitya immediately began talking to him too about his plans, demanding advice about Pointer with heated nervousness, and talked the whole way. The priest listened carefully but offered little advice. To Mitya’s questions he answered evasively: “I don’t know, oh, I don’t know, how should I know?” and so on. When Mitya began speaking of his differences with his father over the inheritance, the priest even grew frightened, as he stood in some sort of dependent relationship to Fyodor Pavlovich. He did, however, ask in surprise why Mitya called the peasant trader Gorstkin “Pointer,” and obligingly explained that, though the man indeed was Pointer, he was not Pointer,[^b08-c02-n001] since the name offended him deeply and he must invariably be called Gorstkin: “Otherwise you won’t get anything done with him; he won’t even listen,” the priest concluded. Mitya was a little surprised, but only briefly, and explained that Samsonov himself had called him that. On hearing this, the priest immediately dropped the subject, though he would have done well to explain his suspicion to Dmitri Fyodorovich then and there: if Samsonov himself had sent him to this peasant as “Pointer,” might he have done so as some sort of joke, and could there be something wrong here? But Mitya had no time to dwell on “such trifles.” He hurried on, striding away, and only when they reached Sukhoy Posyolok did he realize that they had walked not one verst, or one and a half, but a good three. This irritated him, but he bore it. They went into the hut. The forester, an acquaintance of the priest’s, lived in one half, while Gorstkin had settled in the other, clean half across the passage. They entered this clean room and lit a tallow candle. The hut had been heated intensely. On the pine table stood a samovar that had gone out, a tray with cups, an empty rum bottle, a not quite empty quart bottle of vodka, and the remains of some white bread. The visitor himself lay stretched on a bench, with his outer clothes crumpled under his head for a pillow, snoring heavily. Mitya stood in perplexity. “Of course we must wake him: my business is far too important, I was in such a hurry, I’m in a hurry to get back today,” Mitya said anxiously; but the priest and the watchman stood silent, expressing no opinion. Mitya went over and began waking him himself, vigorously, but the sleeper would not wake. “He’s drunk,” Mitya decided, “but what am I to do, Lord, what am I to do!” And suddenly, in a frenzy of impatience, he began tugging the sleeper’s arms and legs, shaking his head, lifting him and sitting him up on the bench. Yet after prolonged effort all he achieved was to make the man begin mooing absurdly and swearing violently, though indistinctly.

<!-- B08-C02-P005 -->
“No, you’d better wait,” the priest finally pronounced, “because he’s clearly in no condition.”

<!-- B08-C02-P006 -->
“Been drinking all day,” the watchman put in.

<!-- B08-C02-P007 -->
“My God!” Mitya cried. “If only you knew how much I need this, and what despair I’m in!”

<!-- B08-C02-P008 -->
“No, you’d better wait until morning,” the priest repeated.

<!-- B08-C02-P009 -->
“Until morning? Have mercy, that’s impossible!” In despair he nearly rushed back to wake the drunkard again, but immediately abandoned the idea, realizing how futile his efforts were. The priest said nothing; the sleepy watchman looked sullen.

<!-- B08-C02-P010 -->
“What dreadful tragedies realism brings upon people!” said Mitya in utter despair. Sweat poured down his face. Seizing the moment, the priest pointed out, quite reasonably, that even if they succeeded in waking the sleeper, being drunk he would still be incapable of any conversation, “and your business is important, so it would be safer to leave it till morning…” Mitya spread his hands and agreed.

<!-- B08-C02-P011 -->
“I’ll stay here with the candle, Father, and watch for my moment. He’ll wake up, and then I’ll begin… I’ll pay you for the candle,” he said to the watchman, “and for the lodging too. You’ll remember Dmitri Karamazov. Only I don’t know what to do about you now, Father: where will you sleep?”

<!-- B08-C02-P012 -->
“No, I’ll go home, sir. I’ll ride back on his little mare,” he said, indicating the watchman. “And so farewell; I wish you every satisfaction.”

<!-- B08-C02-P013 -->
That was what they decided. The priest set off on the mare, delighted to be free at last, but still shaking his head uneasily and wondering whether he ought to inform his benefactor Fyodor Pavlovich of this curious incident in good time tomorrow, “or else he may hear about it, get angry, and put an end to his favors.” The watchman scratched himself and went back to his own half of the hut without a word, while Mitya sat on the bench to watch, as he had said, for his moment. A deep misery settled over his soul like a heavy fog. Deep, dreadful misery! He sat thinking, but could think nothing through. The candle’s wick grew long, a cricket chirped, and the heated room became unbearably stuffy. Suddenly he pictured the garden, the path behind the garden; a door was opening mysteriously in his father’s house, and Grushenka was darting through it… He sprang up from the bench.

<!-- B08-C02-P014 -->
“A tragedy!” he said through gritted teeth, and mechanically went over to the sleeper and began studying his face. He was a lean peasant, not yet old, with a very long face, light-brown curls, and a long, thin, reddish beard, wearing a printed cotton shirt and a black waistcoat with a silver watch chain showing at the pocket. Mitya studied this face with terrible hatred; for some reason the man’s curls were especially hateful to him. Above all it was intolerably insulting that he, Mitya, should be standing over him with his urgent business, having sacrificed so much, abandoned so much, tormented through and through, while this parasite, “on whom my whole fate now depends, snores as though nothing were the matter, as if he were from another planet.” “Oh, the irony of fate!” Mitya exclaimed, and suddenly, losing his head completely, flung himself once more into waking the drunken peasant. He tried with a kind of fury, wrenching him about, pushing him, even hitting him, but after struggling for some five minutes and again achieving nothing, he returned in helpless despair to his bench and sat down.

<!-- B08-C02-P015 -->
“Stupid, stupid!” Mitya exclaimed. “And… how dishonorable it all is!” he suddenly added for some reason. His head had begun to ache terribly. “Should I drop it? Go away altogether?” flashed through his mind. “No, I’ll stay till morning. I’ll stay out of sheer defiance, sheer defiance! What did I come here for, then? And there’s nothing to leave in; how can I get away now? Oh, the senselessness of it!”

<!-- B08-C02-P016 -->
His headache, however, grew worse and worse. He sat motionless and could not remember how he had dozed off and suddenly fallen asleep sitting up. Apparently he slept for two hours or more. He woke with an unbearable headache, so unbearable he could have screamed. His temples throbbed, the crown of his head ached; even after waking, for a long time he could not fully collect himself and make sense of what had happened to him. At last he realized that the overheated room was full of poisonous stove fumes and that he might have died. Yet the drunken peasant still lay there snoring; the candle had melted down and was about to go out. Mitya cried out and staggered through the passage into the watchman’s room. The man soon woke, but on hearing about the fumes in the other room, although he went to deal with them, he took the news with a strange indifference that astonished and offended Mitya.

<!-- B08-C02-P017 -->
“But he’s dead, he’s dead, and then… what then?” Mitya cried to him in a frenzy.

<!-- B08-C02-P018 -->
They flung the doors wide, opened the window, and opened the chimney damper. Mitya dragged in a bucket of water from the passage, first wetted his own head, then found a rag, dipped it in the water, and laid it on Pointer’s head. The watchman continued to treat the whole incident almost contemptuously, and after opening the window said sullenly, “That’ll do,” and went back to bed, leaving Mitya a lighted iron lantern. Mitya fussed over the drunken man, overcome by the fumes, for half an hour, continually wetting his head. He seriously intended to stay awake all night, but exhausted himself and somehow sat down for just a moment to catch his breath. His eyes closed instantly; then, without knowing it, he at once stretched out on the bench and fell into a dead sleep.

<!-- B08-C02-P019 -->
He woke terribly late. It was already about nine in the morning. The sun shone brightly through the hut’s two little windows. Yesterday’s curly-haired peasant sat on the bench, already dressed in his long coat. In front of him stood a fresh samovar and a fresh bottle. The old bottle from yesterday had been emptied, and the new one was more than half gone. Mitya jumped up and realized at once that the accursed peasant was drunk again, hopelessly, irretrievably drunk. He stared at him for a minute with bulging eyes. The peasant, meanwhile, glanced at him silently and slyly, with an insulting composure, even a kind of contemptuous arrogance, as it seemed to Mitya. He rushed over to him.

<!-- B08-C02-P020 -->
“Excuse me, you see… I… you’ve probably heard from the watchman here, in that room: I’m Lieutenant Dmitri Karamazov, son of old Karamazov, whose grove you’re bargaining to buy…”

<!-- B08-C02-P021 -->
“You’re lying!” the peasant suddenly said, firmly and calmly, biting off the words.

<!-- B08-C02-P022 -->
“How am I lying? You know Fyodor Pavlovich, don’t you?”

<!-- B08-C02-P023 -->
“I don’t know any Fyodor Pavlovich of yours,” said the peasant, working his tongue laboriously.

<!-- B08-C02-P024 -->
“The wood, you’re bargaining for his wood. Do wake up, come to your senses. Father Pavel of Ilyinskoye brought me here… You wrote to Samsonov, and he sent me to you…” Mitya was gasping.

<!-- B08-C02-P025 -->
“L-lying!” Pointer said again, biting off the word.

<!-- B08-C02-P026 -->
Mitya’s feet went cold.

<!-- B08-C02-P027 -->
“Have mercy, this is no joke! Perhaps you’re a little drunk. After all, you can speak, you can understand… otherwise… otherwise I don’t understand anything!”

<!-- B08-C02-P028 -->
“You’re a dyer!”

<!-- B08-C02-P029 -->
“For pity’s sake, I’m Karamazov, Dmitri Karamazov. I have a proposition for you… a profitable proposition… a very profitable one… concerning the wood, precisely.”

<!-- B08-C02-P030 -->
The peasant stroked his beard gravely.

<!-- B08-C02-P031 -->
“No, you took the contract and turned out a scoundrel. You’re a scoundrel!”

<!-- B08-C02-P032 -->
“I assure you, you’re mistaken!” Mitya wrung his hands in despair. The peasant kept stroking his beard, then suddenly narrowed his eyes slyly.

<!-- B08-C02-P033 -->
“No, you show me this: show me a law that says you’re allowed to play dirty tricks, d’you hear! You’re a scoundrel, understand?”

<!-- B08-C02-P034 -->
Mitya stepped back darkly, and suddenly it was as if “something struck me on the forehead,” as he later put it. In an instant a kind of illumination occurred in his mind: “a beacon flared up, and I understood everything.” He stood stupefied, wondering how he, an intelligent man after all, could have fallen for such stupidity, got himself into such an adventure, and kept at it for almost an entire day, fussing over this Pointer, wetting his head… “Well, the man’s drunk, blind drunk, and he’ll go on this binge another week—what is there to wait for? What if Samsonov sent me here on purpose? What if she… Oh God, what have I done!..”

<!-- B08-C02-P035 -->
The peasant sat looking at him and chuckling. On another occasion Mitya might have killed the fool in his rage; now he himself had become as weak as a child. Quietly he went over to the bench, picked up his coat, put it on without a word, and left the room. In the other room he did not find the watchman; nobody was there. He took fifty kopecks in small change from his pocket and laid them on the table for his night’s lodging, the candle, and the trouble he had caused. Outside the hut he saw nothing but forest all around. He set off at random, unable even to remember which way to turn from the hut, right or left: hurrying there with the priest the night before, he had paid no attention to the road. There was no desire for revenge in his soul against anyone, even Samsonov. He walked along the narrow forest path senselessly, lost, with his “idea lost,” paying no attention at all to where he was going. A child he met could have overpowered him, so suddenly had his soul and body lost their strength. Somehow, however, he got out of the forest: bare, harvested fields suddenly appeared, stretching beyond sight. “What despair, what death all around!” he repeated, still walking on and on.

<!-- B08-C02-P036 -->
He was rescued by passersby: a driver was taking an elderly merchant along the country road. When they drew level, Mitya asked the way, and it turned out that they too were going to Volovya. They came to an arrangement and took Mitya along. Some three hours later they arrived. At Volovya station Mitya immediately ordered post horses for town, then suddenly realized that he was impossibly hungry. While the horses were being harnessed, they made him some fried eggs. He ate them all in an instant, ate the whole large chunk of bread, ate the sausage they found, and drank three glasses of vodka. The food restored his strength and spirits, and his soul brightened again. He flew along the road, urging the driver on, and suddenly devised a new and now “infallible” plan for obtaining “that accursed money” before evening that very day. “And to think, just think, that a human life is being ruined for the sake of this paltry three thousand!” he exclaimed contemptuously. “I’ll settle it today!” And if it had not been for the unceasing thought of Grushenka, and whether anything had happened to her, he might have become quite cheerful again. But the thought of her drove itself into his soul every moment like a sharp knife. At last they arrived, and Mitya immediately ran to Grushenka.

[^b08-c02-n001]: “Pointer” translates a hunting-dog nickname. Gorstkin resents being called by it.
