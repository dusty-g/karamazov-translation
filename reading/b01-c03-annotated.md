# Chapter III — A Second Marriage and a Second Set of Children

*Part I · Book I: The Story of One Little Family*

<!-- B01-C03-P001 -->

Having got four-year-old Mitya off his hands, Fyodor Pavlovich married again
very soon afterward. This second marriage lasted some eight years. He found this second wife of his,
Sofya Ivanovna, also a very young person, in another province, where he had gone on a small
contracting venture in partnership with some little Jew. Though Fyodor Pavlovich
caroused and drank and made a nuisance of himself, he never stopped attending to the investment of
his capital and always managed his little dealings successfully, though, of course, almost always
rather underhandedly. Sofya Ivanovna was one of the “little orphans,” without family from childhood,
the daughter of an obscure deacon, brought up in the wealthy house of her benefactress, guardian,
and tormentor, an old lady of rank, the widow of General Vorokhov. I don't know the details; I have
only heard that her ward, a gentle, unresentful girl who never answered back, was once cut down from
a noose she had hung on a nail in a closet—so hard did she find it to endure the whims and endless
reproaches of this apparently not unkind old woman, who was merely, out of idleness, the most
intolerable petty tyrant. Fyodor Pavlovich offered his hand; inquiries were made about him, and he
was sent packing; and it was then that, once again, as in his first marriage, he proposed carrying
the little orphan off. It is very, very possible that even she would never have married him for
anything if she had learned a few more details about him in time. But this happened in another
province; and what could a sixteen-year-old girl understand, except that it was better to throw
herself into the river than stay with her benefactress? And so the poor thing exchanged a
benefactress for a benefactor.

Fyodor Pavlovich did not receive a penny this time, because the
general's widow was angry, gave nothing, and cursed them both into the bargain; but this time he had
not expected to receive anything, and had been captivated only by the innocent girl's remarkable
beauty and, above all, by her innocent appearance, which had struck him, a sensualist whose depraved
taste had until then run only to coarse female beauty. “Those innocent little eyes slashed right
across my soul like a razor,” he used to say afterward, with his own nasty little giggle. In a
depraved man, however, even this may have been nothing more than sensual desire.

Having received no compensation, Fyodor Pavlovich stood on no ceremony with his wife, and taking
advantage of the fact that she had, so to speak, “wronged” him, and that he had all but “cut her down from the
noose,” taking advantage, too, of her phenomenal submissiveness and inability to answer back, he
actually trampled on the most ordinary decencies of marriage. Women of ill repute came to the house,
with his wife right there, and orgies were held. As a characteristic detail, let me mention that the
servant Grigory, a gloomy, stupid, and stubborn moralizer who had hated his former mistress,
Adelaida Ivanovna, this time took the new mistress's side, defended her, and quarreled with Fyodor
Pavlovich on her behalf in a way almost beyond what was permissible for a servant; once he even broke up an orgy
and drove out all the shameless women who had come, by force.

Later this unhappy young woman, cowed
from her earliest childhood, developed something like a nervous disorder of the sort that afflicts
women, most often peasant women in the villages, who were known on account of this illness as
*klikushi*.[^b01-c03-n002] With its terrible hysterical fits, the illness even caused her at times
to lose her reason. Nevertheless, she bore Fyodor Pavlovich two sons, Ivan and Alexei, the first in
the first year of the marriage and the second three years later. When she died, the little boy
Alexei was in his fourth year, and strange as it may seem, I know he remembered his mother for the
rest of his life—as though through a dream, of course.

After her death, almost exactly the same
thing happened to both boys as to the first, Mitya: their father utterly forgot and neglected them,
and they ended up with the very same Grigory, and in his cottage too. It was in the cottage that the
old petty tyrant, the general's widow, their mother's benefactress and guardian, found them. She was
still alive, and all that time, all eight years, she had been unable to forget the affront she had
suffered. All eight years she had received, through discreet channels, the most precise information
about how her “Sofya” was living, and on hearing how ill she was and what outrages surrounded her,
had said aloud to the dependent women in her household two or three times: “Serves her right. God
sent her that for her ingratitude.”

Exactly three months after Sofya Ivanovna's death, the general's widow
suddenly appeared in our town in person and went straight to Fyodor Pavlovich's home; she spent only
half an hour in the little town, but accomplished a great deal. It was evening. Fyodor Pavlovich,
whom she had not seen in all eight years, came out to meet her a little drunk. They tell how, the
instant she saw him, without any explanation, she gave him two magnificent, ringing slaps and yanked
his forelock downward three times, then, without adding a word, went straight to the two boys in the
cottage. Seeing at a glance that they were unwashed and in dirty underclothes, she at once gave
Grigory himself a slap as well and announced that she was taking both children home with her; then
she led them out just as they were, wrapped them in a traveling rug, put them in her carriage, and
took them away to her own town. Grigory bore this slap like a devoted slave, did not utter a single
rude word, and, as he escorted the old mistress to her carriage, bowed low from the waist and
solemnly declared that “God will reward you for the orphans.” “And you're still a blockhead!” the
general's widow shouted as she drove away. Fyodor Pavlovich, after thinking the whole business over,
decided it was a good thing, and later gave his formal consent to every provision for the children's
upbringing with the general's widow. As for the slaps he had received, he went around the whole town
telling people about them himself.

As it happened, the general's widow herself died soon afterward, though she
stipulated in her will that the two little ones should receive a thousand rubles each “for their
education, and all this money must be spent on them without fail, but on condition that it lasts
until they come of age, because even such a handout is more than enough for children of that sort,
and if anyone wishes to, let him open his own purse,” and so on and so forth. I have not read the
will myself, but I heard there really was something strange of this kind in it, expressed in a
highly peculiar way. The old woman's principal heir, however, proved to be an honest man, Yefim
Petrovich Polenov, the marshal of the nobility of that province.[^b01-c03-n003] After corresponding
with Fyodor Pavlovich and instantly divining that no money for the upbringing of his own children
could be got out of him (though he never refused outright, but merely dragged things out in such
cases, sometimes even overflowing with sentiment), he took a personal interest in the orphans and
grew especially fond of the younger one, Alexei, who even lived and grew up in his family for a long
time. I ask the reader to notice this from the very beginning. And if there was anyone the young men
had to thank, all their lives, for their upbringing and education, it was this Yefim Petrovich, a
most noble and humane man, such as one seldom meets. He kept intact for the little ones the thousand
rubles each had been left by the general's widow, so that by the time they came of age the sums had
grown with interest to two thousand each, and brought them up on his own money, certainly spending
far more than a thousand on each of them.

Again, I will not yet enter into a detailed account of
their childhood and youth, but will touch only on the most important circumstances. About the elder,
Ivan, however, I will say only that he grew up a rather gloomy, inwardly withdrawn boy, far from
timid, but seemingly aware, from as early as ten, that they were growing up, after all, in someone
else's family and on someone else's charity, and that they had the kind of father it was shameful
even to speak about, and so on and so forth. Very early, almost in infancy (so people said, at any
rate), this boy began to show some extraordinary and brilliant aptitude for learning. I don't know
precisely, but somehow it came about that he left Yefim Petrovich's family when he was scarcely
thirteen, transferring to one of the Moscow gymnasiums and boarding with a certain experienced
educator, famous at the time, who had been Yefim Petrovich's friend since childhood. Ivan himself
said afterward that it had all come about, so to speak, through Yefim Petrovich's “ardor for good
deeds”: he had been carried away by the idea that a boy of genius ought to be brought up by an
educator of genius. However, neither Yefim Petrovich nor the educator of genius was still alive when
the young man finished at the gymnasium and entered the university.

Since Yefim Petrovich had made poor arrangements, and payment of Ivan's own childhood inheritance,
bequeathed by the tyrannical general's widow and already increased by interest from one thousand
rubles to two thousand, was held up by various formalities
and delays entirely unavoidable in our country, the young man had a very hard time during his first
two years at the university, for throughout that time he was obliged to feed and support himself
while studying as well. It should be noted that he would not even attempt to correspond with his
father then—perhaps out of pride, out of contempt for him, or perhaps through cool, sensible
reasoning, which told him he would receive no support of the least significance from dear Papa. Be
that as it may, the young man was not at all at a loss and did manage to find work, first giving
lessons at twenty kopecks apiece, then running around to newspaper offices and
supplying little articles, ten lines long, about incidents in the streets, signed “Eyewitness.”
These little articles, they say, were always put together in such an interesting, piquant way that
they quickly caught on, and in this alone the young man showed his entire practical and intellectual
superiority to that numerous, perpetually needy and wretched portion of our students of both sexes
who, in the capital cities, habitually spend their days from morning to night wearing out the
doorsteps of newspapers and journals, unable to think of anything better than endlessly repeating
the same request for work translating from the French or making copies.

Once he had become acquainted with the newspaper offices, Ivan Fyodorovich maintained his
connections with them, and in
his last years at the university he began publishing highly accomplished reviews of books on various
specialized subjects, so that he even became known in literary circles. Yet it was only very
recently that, by chance, he had suddenly managed to draw particular attention from a much wider
circle of readers, so that a great many people noticed and remembered him all at once. It was a
rather curious affair.

Having already left the university and being about to go abroad on his two thousand rubles, Ivan
Fyodorovich suddenly published a strange article in one of the leading
newspapers, which attracted the attention even of nonspecialists—and, what was more, it dealt with a
subject apparently quite unfamiliar to him, for his degree was in the natural sciences. The article
concerned the question of ecclesiastical courts, which was then being raised
everywhere. After examining some opinions already put forward on the question, he
offered his own personal view. The chief thing was its tone and the remarkably unexpected
conclusion. And yet many in the church party decidedly took the author for one of their own. Then
suddenly, alongside them, not only the civil party but even the atheists themselves began applauding
in their turn. In the end, some perceptive people decided that the whole article was simply an
impudent farce and a mockery. I mention this affair particularly because the article in due course
found its way into our famous monastery just outside town, where there was a general interest in the
question of ecclesiastical courts that had arisen—found its way in and produced complete
bewilderment. On learning the author's name, they were also intrigued by the fact that he was a
native of our town and the son of “that very same Fyodor Pavlovich.” And then, suddenly, at just
this time, the author himself appeared among us.

Why had Ivan Fyodorovich come to us then? I remember asking myself that
question even at the time, with something almost like uneasiness. This fateful arrival, which set so
many consequences in motion, remained obscure to me for a long time afterward, almost always.
Generally speaking, it was strange that a young man so learned, so proud and cautious in appearance,
should suddenly turn up in such a disgraceful house, with such a father, who had ignored him all his
life, had neither known him nor remembered him, and who, though he would certainly never, on any
account or under any circumstances, have given his son money if he had asked, had nevertheless spent
his whole life afraid that these sons too, Ivan and Alexei, would one day come and ask for money.
And here the young man settles in this father's house, lives with him for one month and then
another, and the two could not get along better. This last fact particularly astonished not only me
but many others. Pyotr Alexandrovich Miusov, whom I have already mentioned, a distant relation of
Fyodor Pavlovich's through his first wife, happened to be with us again at that time, at his estate
just outside town, having arrived from Paris, where he had now settled for good. I remember that he
was the most astonished of all, once he had become acquainted with the young man, who interested him
enormously and with whom he sometimes sparred over matters of learning, not without some inward
pain. “He's proud,” he said of him to us then, “he'll always be able to earn a kopeck; he already
has the money to go abroad—what does he want here? It's clear to everyone that he hasn't come to his
father for money, because his father wouldn't give it to him in any case. He's no lover of wine or
debauchery, and yet the old man can't even manage without him, that's how well they get along!” It
was true; the young man even had a visible influence on the old man; the old man had almost begun,
at times, to seem to obey his son, though he was extremely headstrong, even maliciously so on occasion; he had
even begun, at times, to behave more decently...

Only later did it emerge that Ivan Fyodorovich had come partly at the request
of his elder brother, Dmitri Fyodorovich, and on his business. He first got to know and set eyes on
his brother at almost this same time, during this very visit, but had nevertheless begun
corresponding with him before coming from Moscow, over an important matter that concerned Dmitri
Fyodorovich more closely. What this matter was, the reader will learn fully and in detail in due
course. Even so, even when I knew of this particular circumstance, Ivan Fyodorovich still seemed
mysterious to me, and his arrival among us remained inexplicable.

I will add that Ivan Fyodorovich then appeared to be acting as a mediator and
peacemaker between his father and his elder brother, Dmitri Fyodorovich, who had started a great
quarrel with their father and even brought a formal lawsuit against him.

This little family, I repeat, had all come together then for the first time in
their lives, and some of its members were seeing one another for the first time in their lives. Only
the youngest son, Alexei Fyodorovich, had already been living in our town for about a year and had
thus arrived before either of his brothers. Now it is this Alexei whom I find hardest of all to
speak about in my present prefatory account, before bringing him onto the scene in the novel. But I
shall have to write a preface about him too, at least to explain one very strange point in advance,
namely: I am obliged to introduce my future hero to the reader, from the very first scene of his
novel, in a novice's cassock. Yes, he had already been living in our monastery for
about a year, and seemed to be preparing to shut himself away there for the rest of his life.

[^b01-c03-n002]: *Klikushi* was a Russian folk label for women whose fits, cries and altered behavior were understood in religious as well as medical terms.

[^b01-c03-n003]: The provincial marshal of the nobility was the elected head of a province’s noble assembly, an office in local administration rather than a military rank.
