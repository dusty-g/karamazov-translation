# Book X: Boys
## III. A Schoolboy

<!-- B10-C03-P001 -->
But Kolya was no longer listening. At last he could go. Once outside the gate he looked about, gave a little shiver, said, “Freezing!” and set off straight down the street, then turned right along a lane toward the market square. One house short of the square he stopped at a gate, took a whistle from his pocket, and blew it with all his strength, as though giving an agreed signal. He had no more than a minute to wait: a rosy-cheeked boy of about eleven suddenly darted out through the wicket, also wearing a warm, neat, even smart little coat. This was Smurov, a boy in the preparatory class—while Kolya Krasotkin was already two classes above him—the son of a well-to-do official, whose parents apparently forbade him to associate with Krasotkin, notorious as a reckless mischief-maker; so Smurov had evidently slipped out on the sly. If the reader has not forgotten, this Smurov was one of the group of boys who, two months earlier, had thrown stones across the ditch at Ilyusha, and was the one who had told Alyosha Karamazov about him.

<!-- B10-C03-P002 -->
“I’ve been waiting a whole hour for you, Krasotkin,” Smurov said with a resolute air, and the boys set off toward the square.

<!-- B10-C03-P003 -->
“I was delayed,” Krasotkin replied. “There were circumstances. Won’t you get a whipping for being with me?”

<!-- B10-C03-P004 -->
“Oh, come now, as if they whipped me! Is Perezvon with you too?”

<!-- B10-C03-P005 -->
“Perezvon too!”

<!-- B10-C03-P006 -->
“Are you taking him there?”

<!-- B10-C03-P007 -->
“Him too.”

<!-- B10-C03-P008 -->
“Oh, if only it were Zhuchka.”

<!-- B10-C03-P009 -->
“Zhuchka is out of the question. Zhuchka no longer exists. Zhuchka has vanished into the darkness of the unknown.”

<!-- B10-C03-P010 -->
“Oh, couldn’t we do this?” Smurov suddenly stopped. “Ilyusha says Zhuchka was shaggy too, the same gray, smoky color as Perezvon—couldn’t we say this is Zhuchka herself? Perhaps he’d believe it?”

<!-- B10-C03-P011 -->
“Schoolboy, abhor falsehood: that’s one. Even for a good cause: that’s two. Above all, I trust you haven’t announced my visit there.”

<!-- B10-C03-P012 -->
“Heaven forbid, I do understand. But Perezvon won’t comfort him,” Smurov sighed. “You know, his father, that captain, Scrubber, told us he’s bringing him a puppy today, a real Medelyan mastiff with a black nose. He thinks that will comfort Ilyusha, but will it really?”

<!-- B10-C03-P013 -->
“How is he himself—Ilyusha?”

<!-- B10-C03-P014 -->
“Oh, bad, bad! I think he’s got consumption. He’s quite conscious, only he keeps breathing and breathing—he isn’t breathing properly. The other day he asked them to help him walk about; they put his little boots on, and he started walking but kept falling. ‘Oh,’ he said, ‘I told you, Papa, my boots are bad, my old ones; they were awkward to walk in before, too.’ He thought the boots were making him fall down, but it was just weakness. He won’t live a week. Herzenstube comes to see him. They’re rich again now, they have plenty of money.”

<!-- B10-C03-P015 -->
“Scoundrels.”

<!-- B10-C03-P016 -->
“Who are scoundrels?”

<!-- B10-C03-P017 -->
“Doctors, and all that medical rabble, generally speaking and, of course, particularly. I reject medicine. A useless institution. Still, I’m investigating all this. But what’s all this sentimentality you’ve got going there? The whole class seems to be camping out there.”

<!-- B10-C03-P018 -->
“Not the whole class; about ten of our boys go there, all the time, every day. There’s nothing wrong with that.”

<!-- B10-C03-P019 -->
“What astonishes me in all this is Alexei Karamazov’s role: his brother goes on trial tomorrow or the next day for such a crime, and he has all this time to be sentimental with boys!”

<!-- B10-C03-P020 -->
“There’s no sentimentality about it at all. You’re going to make friends with Ilyusha again yourself.”

<!-- B10-C03-P021 -->
“Make friends again? What a ridiculous expression. In any case, I don’t allow anyone to analyze my actions.”

<!-- B10-C03-P022 -->
“How happy Ilyusha will be to see you! He has no idea you’re coming. Why, why did you refuse to come for so long?” Smurov suddenly exclaimed with feeling.

<!-- B10-C03-P023 -->
“My dear boy, that’s my business, not yours. I’m going of my own accord, because that is my will, while Alexei Karamazov dragged all of you there: there’s a difference. And how do you know? Perhaps I’m not going to make friends again at all. A stupid expression.”

<!-- B10-C03-P024 -->
“It wasn’t Karamazov at all, not him at all. Our boys just began going there on their own—of course, with Karamazov at first. And there was nothing of that sort, no nonsense. First one, then another. His father was terribly glad to see us. You know, he’ll simply go mad if Ilyusha dies. He can see Ilyusha is going to die. And he’s so glad we’ve made friends with Ilyusha again. Ilyusha asked about you, but didn’t add anything else. He asks, then goes quiet. And his father will go mad or hang himself. He used to behave like a madman even before. You know, he’s a noble man, and there was a mistake that time. It’s all that parricide’s fault, for beating him up then.”

<!-- B10-C03-P025 -->
“All the same, Karamazov is an enigma to me. I could have made his acquaintance long ago, but in certain cases I prefer to be proud. Besides, I’ve formed a certain opinion of him, which still needs to be tested and clarified.”

<!-- B10-C03-P026 -->
Kolya fell gravely silent; Smurov did too. Smurov, of course, stood in awe of Kolya Krasotkin and would not have dared think himself his equal. Now he was intensely interested, because Kolya had explained that he was going “of his own accord,” so there must certainly be some mystery in his having suddenly decided to go now, precisely today. They were crossing the market square, where a good many carts had come in that morning, and a great deal of poultry had been brought. The town women were selling ring-shaped rolls, thread, and so forth under their awnings. In our little town such Sunday gatherings are naively called fairs, and there are many such fairs in the course of a year. Perezvon ran along in the highest spirits, forever veering right and left to sniff at something somewhere. When he met other dogs, he sniffed them and let himself be sniffed with extraordinary willingness, observing all the canine rules.

<!-- B10-C03-P027 -->
“I like observing realism, Smurov,” Kolya suddenly began. “Have you noticed how dogs meet and sniff one another? There's some universal law of nature at work among them.”

<!-- B10-C03-P028 -->
“Yes, a funny one.”

<!-- B10-C03-P029 -->
“No, not funny—that’s where you’re wrong. Nothing in nature is funny, whatever human beings, with their prejudices, may think. If dogs could reason and criticize, they would certainly find just as much to laugh at, if not considerably more, in the social relations between human beings, their masters—if not considerably more. I repeat that because I’m firmly convinced there’s much more foolishness among us. That’s Rakitin’s idea, a remarkable idea. I’m a socialist, Smurov.”

<!-- B10-C03-P030 -->
“What’s a socialist?” Smurov asked.

<!-- B10-C03-P031 -->
“That’s when everyone is equal, everyone owns the same property in common, there’s no marriage, and religion and all the laws are whatever anyone pleases, and then all the rest of it. You haven’t grown up enough for that yet, it’s too early for you. Cold, though.”

<!-- B10-C03-P032 -->
“Yes. Twelve degrees of frost. Father looked at the thermometer earlier.”

<!-- B10-C03-P033 -->
“And have you noticed, Smurov, that in the middle of winter, at fifteen or even eighteen degrees of frost, it doesn’t seem as cold as it does now, for instance, at the beginning of winter, when a frost suddenly strikes without warning, like these twelve degrees now, especially when there isn’t much snow? It means people haven’t got used to it yet. With human beings everything is habit, in every respect, even in affairs of state and politics. Habit is the chief driving force. What a funny peasant, though.”

<!-- B10-C03-P034 -->
Kolya pointed to a tall peasant in a sheepskin coat, with a good-natured face, who stood beside his cart clapping his mittened hands against the cold. His long, light-brown beard was white with frost.

<!-- B10-C03-P035 -->
“The peasant’s beard is frozen!” Kolya shouted loudly and provocatively as he passed.

<!-- B10-C03-P036 -->
“Plenty of beards are frozen,” the peasant replied, calmly and sententiously.

<!-- B10-C03-P037 -->
“Don’t tease him,” Smurov said.

<!-- B10-C03-P038 -->
“It’s all right, he won’t get angry, he’s a good fellow. Goodbye, Matvei.”

<!-- B10-C03-P039 -->
“Goodbye.”

<!-- B10-C03-P040 -->
“Is your name really Matvei?”

<!-- B10-C03-P041 -->
“Matvei. Didn’t you know?”

<!-- B10-C03-P042 -->
“No; I said it at random.”

<!-- B10-C03-P043 -->
“Well, now. A schoolboy, are you?”

<!-- B10-C03-P044 -->
“A schoolboy.”

<!-- B10-C03-P045 -->
“Do they whip you, then?”

<!-- B10-C03-P046 -->
“Not exactly—well, sort of.”

<!-- B10-C03-P047 -->
“Does it hurt?”

<!-- B10-C03-P048 -->
“It certainly can!”

<!-- B10-C03-P049 -->
“Ah, life!” the peasant sighed from the bottom of his heart.

<!-- B10-C03-P050 -->
“Goodbye, Matvei.”

<!-- B10-C03-P051 -->
“Goodbye. You’re a nice lad, that’s what.”

<!-- B10-C03-P052 -->
The boys went on.

<!-- B10-C03-P053 -->
“That’s a good peasant,” Kolya said to Smurov. “I like talking to the common people, and I’m always happy to give them their due.”

<!-- B10-C03-P054 -->
“Why did you lie to him about us getting whipped?” Smurov asked.

<!-- B10-C03-P055 -->
“Well, I had to give him some comfort, didn’t I?”

<!-- B10-C03-P056 -->
“How?”

<!-- B10-C03-P057 -->
“You see, Smurov, I dislike being asked questions by people who don’t understand the first time. Some things can’t even be explained. In a peasant’s view, a schoolboy gets whipped and ought to get whipped: what sort of schoolboy is he if they don’t whip him? Suppose I suddenly told him they don’t whip us—he’d be upset. But you don’t understand that. You have to know how to talk to the common people.”

<!-- B10-C03-P058 -->
“Only please don’t tease anyone, or there’ll be trouble again, like that time with the goose.”

<!-- B10-C03-P059 -->
“Are you afraid?”

<!-- B10-C03-P060 -->
“Don’t laugh, Kolya, I swear I am. Father will be terribly angry. I’m strictly forbidden to go about with you.”

<!-- B10-C03-P061 -->
“Don’t worry, nothing will happen this time. Hello, Natasha!” he shouted to one of the women selling under an awning.

<!-- B10-C03-P062 -->
“What do you mean, Natasha? I’m Marya,” the woman, who was still far from old, answered shrilly.

<!-- B10-C03-P063 -->
“It’s a good thing you’re Marya. Goodbye.”

<!-- B10-C03-P064 -->
“You little scamp, hardly off the ground and carrying on like that!”

<!-- B10-C03-P065 -->
“No time, I have no time for you. Tell me next Sunday,” Kolya waved his arms, as though she had been pestering him and not he her.

<!-- B10-C03-P066 -->
“What have I got to tell you on Sunday? You started bothering me, not the other way round, you little nuisance!” Marya began shouting. “You ought to be whipped, that’s what. Everyone knows what a torment you are, that’s what!”

<!-- B10-C03-P067 -->
Laughter broke out among the other women selling from their stalls beside Marya, when suddenly, for no reason at all, an angry man of the shop-assistant sort darted out from under the arcade of the town shops. He was no local trader but one of the visitors, still young, wearing a long-skirted blue caftan and a peaked cap, with dark-blond curls and a long, pale, slightly pockmarked face. He was in a state of foolish excitement, and at once began shaking his fist at Kolya.

<!-- B10-C03-P068 -->
“I know you!” he exclaimed angrily. “I know you!”

<!-- B10-C03-P069 -->
Kolya looked at him intently. He could not recall when he might have had any clash with this man. But then, he had had so many clashes in the streets, he could hardly remember them all.

<!-- B10-C03-P070 -->
“Do you?” he asked ironically.

<!-- B10-C03-P071 -->
“I know you! I know you!” the townsman kept repeating like a fool.

<!-- B10-C03-P072 -->
“All the better for you. Well, I haven’t time. Goodbye!”

<!-- B10-C03-P073 -->
“What are you making trouble for?” the townsman shouted. “At it again, are you? I know you! At it again?”

<!-- B10-C03-P074 -->
“My good fellow, any trouble I’m making is none of your business just now,” said Kolya, stopping and continuing to examine him.

<!-- B10-C03-P075 -->
“How is it none of my business?”

<!-- B10-C03-P076 -->
“It simply isn’t.”

<!-- B10-C03-P077 -->
“Whose business is it, then? Whose? Well, whose?”

<!-- B10-C03-P078 -->
“It’s Trifon Nikitich’s business now, my good fellow, not yours.”

<!-- B10-C03-P079 -->
“What Trifon Nikitich?” The young man stared at Kolya in foolish astonishment, though still just as heated. Kolya gravely measured him with his eye.

<!-- B10-C03-P080 -->
“Have you been to the Ascension?” he suddenly asked, sternly and insistently.

<!-- B10-C03-P081 -->
“What Ascension? What for? No, I haven’t,” the young man said, somewhat taken aback.

<!-- B10-C03-P082 -->
“Do you know Sabaneyev?” Kolya continued, still more sternly and insistently.

<!-- B10-C03-P083 -->
“What Sabaneyev? No, I don’t.”

<!-- B10-C03-P084 -->
“Well, to hell with you, then!” Kolya suddenly snapped; turning sharply to the right, he strode rapidly on his way, as though scorning to speak to such a blockhead, who did not even know Sabaneyev.

<!-- B10-C03-P085 -->
“Stop! Hey! What Sabaneyev?” the young man recovered himself, all worked up again. “What was he talking about?” He suddenly turned to the women, staring at them stupidly.

<!-- B10-C03-P086 -->
The women laughed.

<!-- B10-C03-P087 -->
“Crafty lad,” said one.

<!-- B10-C03-P088 -->
“What—what Sabaneyev did he mean?” the young man kept repeating frantically, waving his right hand.

<!-- B10-C03-P089 -->
“That must be Sabaneyev who worked for the Kuzmichevs, that must be it,” one woman suddenly guessed.

<!-- B10-C03-P090 -->
The young man stared wildly at her.

<!-- B10-C03-P091 -->
“Kuz-mi-chev?” another woman repeated. “But how is he Trifon? He’s Kuzma, not Trifon, and the boy called him Trifon Nikitich, so it can’t be him.”

<!-- B10-C03-P092 -->
“Look, it’s not Trifon or Sabaneyev, it’s Chizhov,” a third woman suddenly put in; until then she had kept silent and listened seriously. “Alexei Ivanych, that’s his name. Chizhov, Alexei Ivanovich.”

<!-- B10-C03-P093 -->
“That’s right, it’s Chizhov,” a fourth woman insistently confirmed.

<!-- B10-C03-P094 -->
The bewildered young man looked from one to another.

<!-- B10-C03-P095 -->
“But why did he ask, why was he asking, good people?” he exclaimed, almost in despair now. “‘Do you know Sabaneyev?’ How the devil should I know what sort of Sabaneyev he is!”

<!-- B10-C03-P096 -->
“What a muddlehead you are! They’re telling you it’s not Sabaneyev but Chizhov, Alexei Ivanovich Chizhov, that’s who!” one of the women shouted at him impressively.

<!-- B10-C03-P097 -->
“What Chizhov? Well, what one? Tell me if you know.”

<!-- B10-C03-P098 -->
“The tall one with all that hair. Had a stall in the market last year.”

<!-- B10-C03-P099 -->
“And what the blazes do I want with your Chizhov, good people, eh?”

<!-- B10-C03-P100 -->
“How should I know what the blazes you want with Chizhov?”

<!-- B10-C03-P101 -->
“Who knows what you want with him?” another chimed in. “You ought to know yourself what you want with him, making all that racket. He was speaking to you, not us, you foolish man. Or do you really not know him?”

<!-- B10-C03-P102 -->
“Who?”

<!-- B10-C03-P103 -->
“Chizhov.”

<!-- B10-C03-P104 -->
“The devil take Chizhov, and you with him! I’ll thrash him, that’s what! He was making fun of me!”

<!-- B10-C03-P105 -->
“You’ll thrash Chizhov? More likely he’ll thrash you! You’re a fool, that’s what!”

<!-- B10-C03-P106 -->
“Not Chizhov, not Chizhov, you wicked, spiteful woman! I’ll thrash the boy, that’s what! Bring him here, bring him here, he was making fun of me!”

<!-- B10-C03-P107 -->
The women roared with laughter. Kolya was already striding far away, a triumphant expression on his face. Smurov walked beside him, looking back at the shouting group in the distance. He too was greatly amused, though still afraid of getting into trouble with Kolya.

<!-- B10-C03-P108 -->
“Which Sabaneyev did you ask him about?” he asked Kolya, already anticipating the answer.

<!-- B10-C03-P109 -->
“How should I know which one? They’ll keep that shouting up till evening now. I like stirring up fools in every stratum of society. There’s another blockhead standing there, that peasant. They say, ‘Nothing is stupider than a stupid Frenchman,’ but a Russian face gives itself away too. Doesn’t that fellow have fool written all over his face—that peasant there, eh?”

<!-- B10-C03-P110 -->
“Leave him alone, Kolya, let’s walk past.”

<!-- B10-C03-P111 -->
“Not for anything, I’m in full swing now. Hey! Hello, peasant!”

<!-- B10-C03-P112 -->
The burly peasant walking slowly past, who must already have had a drink, had a round, simple-looking face and a beard streaked with gray. He raised his head and looked at the boy.

<!-- B10-C03-P113 -->
“Well, hello, if you’re not joking,” he replied unhurriedly.

<!-- B10-C03-P114 -->
“And if I am joking?” Kolya laughed.

<!-- B10-C03-P115 -->
“If you’re joking, then joke, God be with you. No harm in it, that’s allowed. A joke’s always allowed.”

<!-- B10-C03-P116 -->
“Forgive me, my good fellow, I was joking.”

<!-- B10-C03-P117 -->
“Well, God forgive you.”

<!-- B10-C03-P118 -->
“But do *you* forgive me?”

<!-- B10-C03-P119 -->
“Most certainly I do. Run along.”

<!-- B10-C03-P120 -->
“Well, now, perhaps you’re a clever peasant after all.”

<!-- B10-C03-P121 -->
“Cleverer than you,” the peasant unexpectedly replied, just as gravely as before.

<!-- B10-C03-P122 -->
“I doubt it,” said Kolya, somewhat taken aback.

<!-- B10-C03-P123 -->
“I mean what I say.”

<!-- B10-C03-P124 -->
“Well, perhaps you are.”

<!-- B10-C03-P125 -->
“That’s right, my lad.”

<!-- B10-C03-P126 -->
“Goodbye, peasant.”

<!-- B10-C03-P127 -->
“Goodbye.”

<!-- B10-C03-P128 -->
“There are different sorts of peasants,” Kolya remarked to Smurov after a short silence. “How was I to know I’d come up against a clever one? I’m always ready to acknowledge intelligence among the common people.”

<!-- B10-C03-P129 -->
In the distance the cathedral clock struck half past eleven. The boys hurried, covering the still fairly long stretch to Staff Captain Snegiryov’s lodgings quickly and almost without speaking. Twenty paces from the house Kolya stopped and told Smurov to go ahead and ask Karamazov to come out to him.

<!-- B10-C03-P130 -->
“We must sniff each other out first,” he remarked to Smurov.

<!-- B10-C03-P131 -->
“But why call him out?” Smurov began to object. “Just go in, they’ll be terribly glad to see you. Why get acquainted out here in the frost?”

<!-- B10-C03-P132 -->
“I know perfectly well why I need him out here in the frost,” Kolya cut him short despotically—which he dearly loved doing with these “little ones”—and Smurov ran to carry out the order.
