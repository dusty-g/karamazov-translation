# Book VII: Alyosha

## I. The Odor of Corruption

<!-- B07-C01-P001 -->
The body of the deceased Father Zosima, a priest-monk of the Great Schema, was prepared for burial according to the prescribed rite. As is well known, the bodies of monks and monks of the Schema are not washed. “When one of the monks departs unto the Lord,” says the Great Book of Needs, “the appointed monk”—that is, the monk assigned to this duty—“wipes his body with warm water, first making with a sponge”—that is, a Greek sponge—“the sign of the cross upon the brow of the deceased, upon his breast, his hands, his feet, and his knees, and nothing more.” Father Paisius himself performed all this for the deceased. After wiping the body, he dressed it in monastic clothing and wrapped it in the mantle, making cuts in the mantle, as the rule required, so that he could wrap it in the form of a cross. On his head he placed the cowl with its eight-pointed cross. The cowl was left open, while the face of the deceased was covered with a black liturgical veil. An icon of the Savior was placed in his hands. Thus prepared, he was laid toward morning in the coffin, which had been made ready long before. They intended to leave the coffin in his cell for the whole day—in the first, larger room, the very room where the late elder had received the brethren and lay visitors. Since the deceased had been a priest-monk of the Great Schema, priest-monks and monk-deacons were to read the Gospel over him, rather than the Psalter.

Father Joseph began reading immediately after the memorial service. Father Paisius, who had volunteered to read afterward throughout the day and night, was still very busy and anxious, as was the superior of the hermitage: something unusual had suddenly begun to appear, and was becoming more and more evident, both among the monastery brethren and among the laypeople arriving in crowds from the monastery guesthouses and the town—a quite unheard-of, even “unseemly,” excitement and impatient expectation. Both the superior and Father Paisius did all they could to calm those caught up in this restless agitation. Once it was fully daylight, people even began arriving from town with their sick, especially children, as though they had deliberately waited for this very moment, evidently hoping for an immediate power of healing that, they believed, could not be long in manifesting itself. Only now did it become clear how completely everyone in our parts had come to regard the elder, even in his lifetime, as an unquestionable and great saint. And those arriving were by no means all from the common people. These great expectations among the faithful, displayed so hastily and openly, even impatiently and almost as a demand, seemed to Father Paisius an undoubted temptation. Though he had foreseen it long before, the reality surpassed what he had expected. Meeting monks who were caught up in the excitement, Father Paisius even began to rebuke them. “To expect something great in this fashion, and so immediately,” he said, “is a frivolity possible only among people of the world, and unbecoming in us.” But few listened, and Father Paisius observed this with anxiety, despite the fact that he himself—if we are to recall everything truthfully—though indignant at these over-impatient expectations and finding them frivolous and vain, was secretly, inwardly, in the depths of his soul, waiting for almost the same thing as these excited people; he could not help admitting it to himself. All the same, certain encounters particularly troubled him, stirring deep misgivings through some foreboding of his own.

Among the crowd pressing into the dead man's cell he noticed, for example, Rakitin and the distant visitor, the monk from Obdorsk, who was still staying at the monastery, with an inward revulsion for which he immediately reproached himself. For some reason Father Paisius suddenly found both of them suspicious—though they were not the only ones who might have been noticed in this light. Of all the excited people, the monk from Obdorsk was by far the busiest. He could be seen everywhere, in every corner: everywhere he asked questions, everywhere he listened, everywhere he whispered with a peculiarly mysterious air. His expression was one of extreme impatience, as though he were already irritated that what he expected was taking so long to happen. As for Rakitin, it later emerged that he had come to the hermitage so early on a special commission from Madame Khokhlakov. This kind but weak-willed woman, who could not herself be admitted to the hermitage, had scarcely woken and learned of the death when she was seized by such urgent curiosity that she immediately dispatched Rakitin in her place, instructing him to observe everything and send her written reports at once, roughly every half hour, of whatever happened. She considered Rakitin a most devout and faithful young man—so well did he know how to get along with everyone and present himself to each as they wished him to be, whenever he saw the slightest advantage for himself in doing so.

The day was clear and bright, and many of the visiting pilgrims gathered around the graves of the hermitage, most of which clustered around the church, though others lay scattered throughout the grounds. As Father Paisius walked about the hermitage, he suddenly remembered Alyosha, whom he had not seen for a long time, almost since the night before. He had no sooner remembered him than he caught sight of him in the farthest corner of the hermitage, beside the fence, sitting on the gravestone of a monk long dead and renowned for his ascetic labors. Alyosha sat with his back to the hermitage and his face toward the fence, as though hiding behind the monument. Coming close, Father Paisius saw that he had covered his face with both hands and was weeping silently but bitterly, his whole body shaking with sobs. Father Paisius stood over him for a little while.

<!-- B07-C01-P002 -->
“Enough, my dear son, enough, my friend,” he said at last, with deep feeling. “What is it? Rejoice, don't weep. Don't you know that this is the greatest of all his days? Where he is now, at this very moment—only think of that!”

<!-- B07-C01-P003 -->
Alyosha looked up at him for a moment, uncovering his face, swollen with tears like a small child's, but at once turned away without a word and covered it with both hands again.

<!-- B07-C01-P004 -->
“Well, perhaps you're right,” Father Paisius said thoughtfully. “Perhaps you should weep. Christ has sent you these tears.” “These tender tears of yours will only bring your soul rest and your dear heart joy,” he added to himself as he walked away, thinking lovingly of Alyosha. He did, however, walk away rather quickly, for he felt that if he kept looking at him he might begin to weep himself. Time passed, meanwhile, and the monastery services and memorial prayers for the deceased continued in due order. Father Paisius relieved Father Joseph at the coffin once again and resumed the reading of the Gospel. But before three o'clock that afternoon, the thing I mentioned at the end of the previous book had occurred, something so unexpected by anyone here and so contrary to the hopes of all that, I repeat, the detailed and petty story of this incident is remembered to this day with extraordinary vividness in our town and throughout the surrounding district. Here let me add once more, on my own behalf: it is almost repugnant to me to recall this petty, scandalous event, which was in itself utterly insignificant and natural, and I would certainly have left it out of my account altogether had it not affected the soul and heart of the principal, though still future, hero of my story, Alyosha, so powerfully and in such a particular way, creating a sort of turning point and upheaval in his soul that shook his mind but also strengthened it once and for all, for his whole life and toward a definite end.

<!-- B07-C01-P005 -->
And so, to the story. When, before dawn, they laid the elder's body, prepared for burial, in the coffin and carried it into the first room, his former reception room, the question arose among those beside the coffin whether they ought to open the windows. But this question, raised casually and in passing by someone, remained unanswered and almost unnoticed—unless some of those present did notice it, though only inwardly, as meaning that to expect decay and an odor of corruption from the body of such a man was sheer absurdity, deserving pity, if not a smile of derision, at the questioner's lack of faith and frivolity. For they expected precisely the opposite. And soon after noon something began that those coming and going at first acknowledged only silently, to themselves, each visibly afraid to communicate to anyone the thought beginning to form in his mind; but by three o'clock that afternoon it had become so clear and undeniable that the news flew instantly through the whole hermitage and all the pilgrims visiting it, immediately reached the monastery and astonished everyone there, and finally, in a very short time, reached the town and agitated everyone in it, believers and unbelievers alike. The unbelievers rejoiced, and as for the believers, some among them rejoiced even more than the unbelievers themselves, for “people love the downfall and disgrace of the righteous,” as the late elder himself had said in one of his teachings.

The fact was that an odor of corruption had begun to come from the coffin, gradually at first but more and more noticeably; by three o'clock it was all too unmistakable, and still steadily growing stronger. It had been a long time since there had been such a scandal at our monastery—indeed, no one could remember its like in all the monastery's past—as the grossly unrestrained scandal that broke out immediately after this event even among the monks themselves, something that on any other occasion would have been quite impossible. Afterward, even many years later, some of our more reasonable monks, recalling that day in detail, wondered in horror how the scandal could have gone so far. For monks of most righteous lives, whose righteousness had been plain to all, God-fearing elders, had died before, and from their humble coffins too had come an odor of corruption, arising naturally as from any dead body; yet this had caused no scandal, nor even the slightest agitation.

There were, of course, some among those who had died long ago whose memory was still alive in the monastery and whose remains, according to tradition, had shown no sign of decay. This had affected the brethren with a mysterious tenderness and remained in their memory as something beautiful and miraculous, a promise of still greater glory to come from those tombs, if only the time should come by God's will. Especially cherished among these was the memory of the elder Job, who had lived to the age of a hundred and five, a renowned ascetic, a great practitioner of fasting and silence, who had died long ago, back in the second decade of the present century. His grave was shown with particular and extraordinary reverence to all pilgrims on their first visit, with mysterious references to certain great hopes. (This was the very grave where Father Paisius had found Alyosha sitting that morning.) Besides this elder, dead so long ago, the same memory remained alive of the great father and priest-monk of the Schema, the elder Varsonofy, who had died comparatively recently—the very man from whom Father Zosima had received the eldership and whom, in his lifetime, all the pilgrims visiting the monastery had regarded simply as a holy fool. Tradition held that both these men had lain in their coffins as if alive, that they had been buried entirely untouched by decay, and that their faces had even seemed to grow radiant in the coffin. Some even insisted on remembering a distinct fragrance coming from their bodies. Yet even with these impressive memories, it would still be difficult to explain the immediate cause of so frivolous, absurd, and malicious an outbreak beside the elder Zosima's coffin.

For my part, I think that much else came together here, many different causes acting at once. One of them, for example, was that very entrenched hostility to the institution of elders as a harmful innovation, still deeply concealed in the minds of many monks in the monastery. And then, of course, above all, there was envy of the dead man's holiness, so firmly established in his lifetime that it seemed almost forbidden to question it. For although the late elder had drawn many people to him, less through miracles than through love, and had built around himself a whole world, as it were, of people who loved him, nevertheless, and indeed all the more for that, this very thing had also created those who envied him and, in time, bitter enemies, open and secret, not only among the monastery's inhabitants but even among people of the world. He had never harmed anyone, for example, and yet: “Why do they consider him so holy?” This one question, repeated over time, finally opened up a whole abyss of the most insatiable malice. That is why I think so many people were immeasurably delighted when they smelled the odor of corruption from his body, especially so soon—for not even a day had passed since his death—while among those devoted to the elder who had revered him until then, there were immediately some who almost took the event as a personal insult and injury. The affair developed as follows.

<!-- B07-C01-P006 -->
As soon as decay began to reveal itself, one could tell from the mere appearance of the monks entering the dead man's cell what they had come for. A man would come in, stand a short while, and hurry out to confirm the news to the others waiting in a crowd outside. Some of those waiting shook their heads sorrowfully, but others no longer even wished to conceal the joy shining openly in their spiteful eyes. No one rebuked them anymore; no voice spoke up for what was good. This was strange, for those devoted to the late elder were still the majority in the monastery; but evidently the Lord himself had allowed the minority to gain the upper hand for a time on this occasion. Soon laypeople too began appearing in the cell as the same sort of spies, mostly the more educated visitors. Few of the common people came in, though many had gathered at the hermitage gates. What is certain is that the influx of lay visitors increased greatly after three o'clock, precisely because of the scandalous news. People who might not have come that day at all, who had had no intention of coming, now came expressly for this, including some persons of considerable rank. Outward decorum, however, had not yet been disturbed, and Father Paisius went on reading the Gospel aloud firmly and distinctly, with a stern face, as though he had noticed nothing of what was happening, though he had long since noticed something unusual. But now voices began reaching even him, at first very quiet, then gradually firmer and bolder. “It seems God's judgment is different from man's!” Father Paisius suddenly heard. The first to say this aloud was a layman, a town official, already elderly and, so far as was known of him, very devout; but in saying it aloud he merely repeated what the monks had long been whispering in one another's ears. They had long since uttered this hopeless pronouncement, and worst of all, with almost every minute a kind of triumph became more evident in it and grew stronger. Soon, however, decorum itself began to break down, as though everyone felt somehow entitled to break it. “And how could this have happened?” some of the monks said, at first as though regretfully. “His body was small and wasted, nothing but skin and bones. Where could a smell come from?” “Then God meant to give a sign,” others hastily added, and their opinion was immediately accepted without dispute, for it was pointed out once more that even if the smell had come naturally, as from any dead sinner, it should still have come later, not in such obvious haste—after a day at least—whereas “this one has outstripped nature.” So this could only be God and his deliberate finger. He had meant to give a sign. The argument struck home irresistibly.

The gentle priest-monk Father Joseph, the librarian and a favorite of the deceased, tried to object to some of the slanderers that “it isn't like this everywhere,” that the necessary incorruptibility of the bodies of the righteous was no dogma of Orthodoxy, but merely an opinion; that even in the most Orthodox lands, on Mount Athos, for instance, an odor of corruption did not cause such distress, and the chief sign that the saved had been glorified was considered to be not the body's incorruptibility but the color of the bones after the bodies had lain for many years in the earth and even decayed there. “And if the bones are found to be yellow as wax, that is the chief sign that the Lord has glorified the righteous dead man; but if they are found to be black instead of yellow, it means the Lord has not deemed him worthy of such glory. That is how it is on Athos, that great place where Orthodoxy has been preserved unbroken from ancient times in the most radiant purity,” Father Joseph concluded. But the humble father's words carried no conviction and even met with mockery. “All that is learning and innovation. There's no need to listen,” the monks decided to themselves. “We keep to the old ways here. There are plenty of innovations these days—must we copy them all?” others added. “We've had no fewer holy fathers than they have. They're under the Turk there and they've forgotten everything. Their Orthodoxy has been muddied for a long time, and they don't even have bells,” the most derisive put in. Father Joseph withdrew in sorrow, all the more because he himself had expressed his opinion with little firmness, as though he scarcely believed it himself. But he foresaw with dismay that something very ugly was beginning and that even disobedience itself was raising its head.

Little by little, after Father Joseph, all the voices of reason fell silent too. Somehow it happened that everyone who had loved the late elder and accepted the institution of elders with tender obedience suddenly became terribly afraid of something, and when they met they could only look timidly into one another's faces. The enemies of eldership as an innovation, meanwhile, proudly raised their heads. “The late elder Varsonofy gave off no odor at all, only a sweet fragrance,” they recalled with malicious pleasure. “But he didn't earn that through eldership; he was righteous in himself.”

After this, condemnation and even outright accusations rained down on the newly deceased elder. “He taught falsely. He taught that life is a great joy, instead of humility in tears,” said some of the most muddleheaded. “He had a fashionable faith. He didn't believe in material fire in hell,” added others still more muddleheaded. “He wasn't strict in his fasting. He allowed himself sweets, ate cherry preserves with his tea, loved them dearly—the ladies used to send them to him. Is drinking tea any business for a monk of the Schema?” came from others full of envy. “He sat there in his pride,” the most malicious recalled cruelly. “He thought himself a saint. They went down on their knees before him, and he accepted it as his due.” “He abused the sacrament of confession,” the fiercest opponents of eldership added in vicious whispers, even some of the oldest monks, most austere in their devotions, true practitioners of fasting and silence, who had kept silent during the dead man's lifetime but had suddenly opened their mouths now. This was terrible, for their words had a powerful influence on the younger monks whose convictions were not yet settled. The visitor from Obdorsk, the little monk from Saint Sylvester's, listened most attentively to all this, sighing deeply and nodding his head. “No, it seems Father Ferapont judged rightly yesterday,” he thought to himself. And just then Father Ferapont appeared, as though he had come out expressly to deepen the upheaval.

<!-- B07-C01-P007 -->
I have already mentioned that he rarely left his little wooden cell in the apiary, that for long periods he did not even appear in church, and that this was allowed him as a supposed holy fool, without his being bound by the rule common to everyone. To tell the whole truth, however, all this was allowed him partly out of necessity. For it somehow seemed almost shameful to insist on burdening so great a practitioner of fasting and silence, a man who prayed day and night and even fell asleep on his knees, with the common rule if he did not wish to submit to it himself. “He's holier than all of us, and what he does is harder than what the rule requires,” the monks would have said. “And if he doesn't go to church, that means he knows for himself when to go. He has his own rule.” To avoid this likely murmuring and scandal, Father Ferapont was left in peace. As everyone already knew, Father Ferapont intensely disliked the elder Zosima; and now the news suddenly reached him too, in his little cell, that “God's judgment, it seems, is different from man's, and he has even outstripped nature.” Presumably one of the first to run and tell him was the visitor from Obdorsk, who had visited him the day before and left him in terror. I have also mentioned that Father Paisius, standing firm and unshakable as he read over the coffin, though he could neither hear nor see what was happening outside the cell, had infallibly anticipated the essentials in his heart, for he knew his surroundings through and through. He was not dismayed, but awaited fearlessly whatever else might happen, looking piercingly toward the outcome of the disturbance that already stood before his mind's eye. Suddenly his ear was struck by an extraordinary noise in the entryway, now plainly violating decorum. The door swung wide open, and Father Ferapont appeared on the threshold. Behind him, as was observed and indeed could be clearly seen from the cell, many monks who had accompanied him were crowded at the foot of the porch, with laypeople among them. His followers, however, did not enter or even climb the porch steps. They had stopped and were waiting to see what Father Ferapont would say and do next, for they sensed, even with a certain fear despite all their boldness, that he had come with a purpose. Stopping on the threshold, Father Ferapont raised his arms, and from beneath his right arm peered the sharp, curious little eyes of the visitor from Obdorsk, the only one who had been unable to contain himself and had run up the steps after Father Ferapont out of sheer curiosity. The others, on the contrary, had all shrunk still farther back in sudden fear the moment the door crashed open. Raising his arms on high, Father Ferapont suddenly cried out:

<!-- B07-C01-P008 -->
“Cast out I shall, cast out!” And at once, turning to each of the four directions in turn, he began making the sign of the cross with his hand over the walls and all four corners of the cell. Those who had followed Father Ferapont understood this action at once, for they knew that he always did it wherever he went in, and would neither sit down nor say a word before he had driven out the unclean power.

<!-- B07-C01-P009 -->
“Satan, depart! Satan, depart!” he repeated with each sign of the cross. “Cast out I shall, cast out!” he cried again. He wore his coarse cassock, belted with a rope. Beneath his hempen shirt his bare chest showed, covered with gray hair. His feet were entirely bare. As soon as he began waving his arms, the heavy penitential chains he wore beneath his cassock began to shake and clank. Father Paisius broke off his reading, stepped forward, and stood waiting before him.

<!-- B07-C01-P010 -->
“Why have you come, venerable father? Why do you disturb our good order? Why do you trouble the humble flock?” he said at last, looking sternly at him.

<!-- B07-C01-P011 -->
“Wherefore art thou come? What dost thou seek? How dost thou believe?” Father Ferapont shouted, playing the holy fool. “I've hastened here to drive out these guests of yours, the filthy devils. I'm looking to see how many you've gathered without me. I mean to sweep them out with a birch broom.”

<!-- B07-C01-P012 -->
“You drive out the unclean one, yet perhaps you serve him yourself,” Father Paisius continued fearlessly. “And who can say of himself, ‘I am holy’? Can you, father?”

<!-- B07-C01-P013 -->
“I am foul, not holy. I won't sit in an armchair and crave worship for myself like an idol!” Father Ferapont thundered. “Nowadays people are destroying the holy faith. The dead man, your saint here”—he turned to the crowd and pointed a finger at the coffin—“denied the devils. Gave a purge for devils. And so they've bred in your corners like spiders. And today he's stinking himself. In this we see a great sign from the Lord.”

<!-- B07-C01-P014 -->
And something of the sort had indeed happened once in Father Zosima's lifetime. One of the monks had begun dreaming of the unclean power and finally seeing it while awake. When in the greatest terror he confided this to the elder, the elder advised unceasing prayer and stricter fasting. But when this too failed to help, he advised him, without abandoning prayer and fasting, to take a certain medicine. Many were scandalized by this at the time and talked among themselves, shaking their heads—above all Father Ferapont, to whom certain detractors had immediately hurried with the news of this “extraordinary” prescription by the elder in so exceptional a case.

<!-- B07-C01-P015 -->
“Depart, father!” Father Paisius said commandingly. “It is not men who judge, but God. Perhaps the ‘sign’ we see here is one that neither you nor I nor anyone has the power to understand. Depart, father, and do not trouble the flock!” he repeated insistently.

<!-- B07-C01-P016 -->
“He didn't observe the fasts required by his Schema, and that's why the sign has come. It is plain, and it's a sin to hide it!” the fanatic persisted, carried away by his zeal without understanding. “He was seduced by candy; the ladies brought it in their pockets. He took his pleasure in tea, sacrificed to his belly, filling it with sweets, and his mind with haughty thoughts... And so he has suffered disgrace...”

<!-- B07-C01-P017 -->
“Your words are frivolous, father!” Father Paisius raised his voice too. “I marvel at your fasting and ascetic labors, but your words are frivolous, as though spoken by a fickle, foolish youth in the world. Depart, father, I command you!” Father Paisius thundered at last.

<!-- B07-C01-P018 -->
“Oh, I'll depart!” Father Ferapont said, seemingly somewhat disconcerted, but not relinquishing his bitterness. “You're learned men! Your great intelligence has raised you above my nothingness. I came here barely able to read, and here I've forgotten even what I knew. The Lord God himself has shielded me, little as I am, from your wisdom...”

<!-- B07-C01-P019 -->
Father Paisius stood over him, waiting steadfastly. Father Ferapont was silent for a moment, then suddenly grew mournful and, putting his right palm to his cheek, said in a singsong voice as he looked at the dead elder's coffin:

<!-- B07-C01-P020 -->
“Tomorrow they'll sing ‘Helper and Protector’ over him—that most glorious canon—but when I drop dead, over me it'll be no more than ‘What Sweetness in This Life’—just a little sticheron,”[^b07-c01-n001] he said tearfully and with regret. “They have grown proud and exalted themselves! This place is empty!” he suddenly shrieked like a madman, and with a wave of his hand turned quickly and hurried down the porch steps. The crowd waiting below wavered. Some followed him at once, but others lingered, for the cell was still open, and Father Paisius had come out onto the porch after Father Ferapont and stood watching. But the old man, now thoroughly worked up, had not yet finished. After going some twenty paces he suddenly turned toward the setting sun, raised both arms above him, and, as though his legs had been cut from under him, collapsed on the ground with a tremendous cry:

<!-- B07-C01-P021 -->
“My Lord has conquered! Christ has triumphed as the sun goes down!” he shouted frenziedly, raising his arms toward the sun; then, falling facedown on the earth, he sobbed aloud like a small child, his whole body shaking with tears and his arms spread out across the ground. Now everyone rushed to him; cries rang out, answering sobs... A sort of frenzy seized them all.

<!-- B07-C01-P022 -->
“There's a holy man! There's a righteous man!” voices cried out, no longer timidly. “There's the man who ought to sit as elder!” others added, now with bitterness.

<!-- B07-C01-P023 -->
“He won't sit as elder... He'll refuse it himself... He won't serve that accursed innovation... He won't copy their foolishness,” other voices immediately took up. It is hard to imagine where this might have ended, but at that very moment the bell rang, calling them to the service. Everyone at once began crossing themselves. Father Ferapont too got up and, shielding himself with the sign of the cross, went off to his cell without looking back, still crying out, though now his words were quite incoherent. A few people started after him, but most began to disperse, hurrying to the service. Father Paisius handed the reading over to Father Joseph and came down the steps. The fanatics' frenzied cries could not shake him, yet suddenly his heart grew sad and ached in a particular way over something, and he became aware of it. He stopped and suddenly asked himself, “Why this sorrow of mine, even to the point of despondency?” And to his surprise he realized at once that this sudden sorrow apparently arose from a very small and particular cause: among the agitated people who had just crowded around the entrance to the cell he had noticed Alyosha, and he remembered that at the very sight of him he had immediately felt something like a pain in his heart. “Can this young man mean so much to my heart already?” he suddenly asked himself in surprise. At that moment Alyosha happened to be passing him, as though hurrying somewhere, but not toward the church. Their eyes met. Alyosha quickly looked away and lowered his eyes to the ground, and from the young man's appearance alone Father Paisius guessed what a powerful change was taking place within him at that moment.

<!-- B07-C01-P024 -->
“Have you too been shaken?” Father Paisius suddenly cried. “Can you too be among those of little faith?” he added sorrowfully.

<!-- B07-C01-P025 -->
Alyosha stopped and gave Father Paisius an uncertain sort of look, but quickly looked away again and again lowered his eyes to the ground. He stood sideways, without turning to face the man who questioned him. Father Paisius watched attentively.

<!-- B07-C01-P026 -->
“Where are you hurrying, then? The bell is calling us to the service,” he asked again, but again Alyosha gave no answer.

<!-- B07-C01-P027 -->
“Are you leaving the hermitage? How can you go without asking, without a blessing?”

<!-- B07-C01-P028 -->
Alyosha suddenly gave a crooked smile and raised his eyes with a strange, very strange look to the father questioning him, the man to whom his former guide, the former master of his heart and mind, his beloved elder, had entrusted him as he lay dying; then suddenly, still without answering, he waved his hand, seemingly unconcerned even with showing respect, and walked quickly toward the gates, out of the hermitage.

<!-- B07-C01-P029 -->
“You'll come back yet!” Father Paisius whispered, watching him go with sorrowful astonishment.
