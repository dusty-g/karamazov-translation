# Book X: Boys
## V. At Ilyusha’s Bedside

<!-- B10-C05-P001 -->
The room we already know, where our acquaintance the retired Staff Captain Snegiryov lived with his family, was stifling and crowded with visitors just then. Several boys were sitting with Ilyusha this time, and though they were all as ready as Smurov to deny that Alyosha had brought them together and made peace between them, that was what had happened. His whole art in the matter had consisted of bringing them to Ilyusha one by one, without any “slobbering affection,” as though quite unintentionally, by chance. This had brought Ilyusha immense relief in his suffering. The almost tender friendship and concern shown him by all these boys, his former enemies, had moved him deeply. Only Krasotkin was missing, and that weighed terribly on his heart. If anything in Ilyushechka's bitter memories was bitterest of all, it was this whole episode with Krasotkin, once his only friend and protector, whom he had attacked with a knife. That was also what the clever little boy Smurov thought—the first to come and make peace with Ilyusha. But when Smurov had vaguely hinted to Krasotkin that Alyosha wanted to call on him “about something,” Krasotkin had cut him short at once and blocked the approach, instructing Smurov to tell “Karamazov” immediately that he knew for himself how to act, that he asked no one's advice, and that if he went to see the sick boy he himself would know when to go, because he had “his own plans.” That had been about two weeks before this Sunday. It was why Alyosha had not called on him as he had intended. Still, though he waited, he sent Smurov to Krasotkin again, and then once more. On both those occasions Krasotkin answered with the most impatient and emphatic refusal, sending word to Alyosha that if he came to fetch him himself he would never visit Ilyusha at all, and that they were to stop pestering him. Even Smurov had not known until this very last day that Kolya had decided to go to Ilyusha that morning. Only the evening before, as he said goodbye to Smurov, had Kolya abruptly told him to wait at home for him the next morning, because they were going together to the Snegiryovs; but he must not dare tell anyone he was coming, since he wanted to arrive unexpectedly. Smurov obeyed. His hope that Kolya would bring the lost Zhuchka was based on a remark Krasotkin had once let fall in passing: they were “all asses if they couldn't find a dog, provided she was still alive.” But when Smurov waited for a suitable moment and timidly hinted at his guess about the dog, Krasotkin suddenly became furiously angry. “What kind of ass do you take me for, going all over town looking for other people's dogs when I've got my own Perezvon? And how can anyone imagine a dog could swallow a pin and stay alive? Slobbering affection, that's all!”

<!-- B10-C05-P002 -->
Meanwhile, for nearly two weeks now, Ilyusha had scarcely left his little bed in the corner by the icons. He had not been to school since the very day he met Alyosha and bit his finger. In fact, he had fallen ill that same day, though for another month he could still occasionally get out of bed and make his way about the room or entryway. At last he lost all his strength and could no longer move without his father's help. His father watched over him in trembling anxiety, had given up drinking altogether, and was almost out of his mind with fear that his boy would die. Often, especially after leading him round the room by the arm and settling him back in bed, he would suddenly run into a dark corner of the entryway, lean his forehead against the wall and begin to sob in great, shaking bursts, stifling his voice so Ilyushechka would not hear him.

<!-- B10-C05-P003 -->
When he returned to the room, he would usually try to amuse and comfort his precious boy, telling him fairy tales, funny stories, or mimicking the various ridiculous people he happened to have met; he even imitated animals and their comical howls and cries. But Ilyusha very much disliked his father's clowning and contortions. Though he tried not to show his distress, the boy knew with an aching heart that his father was humiliated in society, and “the scrubber” and that “terrible day” were always, inescapably in his thoughts. Ninochka, Ilyushechka's quiet, gentle sister who could not walk, disliked her father's antics too. (As for Varvara Nikolaevna, she had long since gone to Petersburg to attend courses.) But half-witted Mama found them very entertaining and laughed with all her heart whenever her husband began acting something out or making funny gestures. It was the only way to cheer her up; the rest of the time she grumbled incessantly and complained that everyone had forgotten her, that nobody respected her, that they mistreated her, and so on and so on. In the last few days, however, she too had suddenly seemed to change entirely. She often looked toward Ilyusha's corner and grew thoughtful. She became much less talkative, much quieter, and if she began to cry, she cried softly so no one would hear. The staff captain observed this change in her with bitter bewilderment. At first the boys' visits had displeased and irritated her, but gradually the children's cheerful shouts and stories began to entertain her too, and in the end she grew so fond of them that she would have been terribly desolate had they stopped coming. When the children told stories or began to play, she laughed and clapped her hands. She would call some of them over and kiss them. She grew particularly fond of Smurov. As for the staff captain, from the very beginning the appearance in his rooms of children who came to cheer Ilyusha had filled his soul with rapturous joy, and even with the hope that Ilyusha would stop fretting now and perhaps recover all the sooner. Until quite recently, for all his fear for Ilyusha, he had never doubted for a moment that his boy would suddenly get well. He welcomed the little visitors with reverence, hovered about them, waited on them, was ready to carry them on his back, and even began doing so in earnest; but Ilyusha disliked those games, and they were abandoned. He began buying treats for them, little gingerbreads and nuts, providing tea and spreading bread and butter. It should be mentioned that throughout this time he never ran short of money. He had accepted those two hundred rubles from Katerina Ivanovna exactly as Alyosha had predicted. Then, after learning more about their circumstances and Ilyusha's illness, Katerina Ivanovna had visited their rooms herself, met the whole family, and even managed to charm the staff captain's half-witted wife. Since then her generosity had never failed them, and the staff captain himself, crushed by the terrifying thought that his boy would die, had forgotten his former pride and humbly accepted charity. All this time, at Katerina Ivanovna's request, Doctor Herzenstube had been visiting the sick boy regularly, every other day; but his visits did little good, and he dosed him horribly with medicines. Today, however, this Sunday morning, the staff captain was expecting a new doctor, a visitor from Moscow, where he was considered a celebrity. Katerina Ivanovna had expressly summoned him from Moscow at great expense—not for Ilyushechka, but for another purpose, which will be explained later, in its proper place. Since he was here, however, she had asked him to visit Ilyushechka too, and the staff captain had been notified beforehand. But he had no inkling that Kolya Krasotkin was coming, although he had long been wishing that this boy, for whom his Ilyushechka pined so painfully, would come at last. At the very moment Krasotkin opened the door and appeared in the room, everyone—the staff captain and the boys—was gathered round the sick boy's bed, examining the tiny Medelyan mastiff puppy that had just been brought in. It had been born only yesterday, but the staff captain had arranged for it a week earlier, to amuse and comfort Ilyushechka, who was still grieving for the vanished and surely by now dead Zhuchka. Ilyusha had heard three days before that he was to be given a little dog, and no ordinary one but a real Medelyan—which of course was terribly important. Though his sensitivity and delicacy made him show pleasure in the gift, everyone, both his father and the boys, clearly saw that the new dog had perhaps only stirred more strongly in his little heart the memory of the unhappy Zhuchka he had tormented. The puppy lay wriggling beside him, and with a pained smile he stroked it with his thin, pale, wasted little hand. You could even see that he liked the puppy, but... Zhuchka was still missing, it still wasn't Zhuchka. If only he could have Zhuchka and the puppy together, then his happiness would be complete!

“Krasotkin!” suddenly cried one of the boys, the first to see Kolya enter. There was a visible stir; the boys moved apart to either side of the bed, suddenly revealing Ilyushechka in full view. The staff captain rushed to meet Kolya.

<!-- B10-C05-P005 -->
“Come in, come in... our dear guest!” he babbled. “Ilyushechka, Mr. Krasotkin has come to see you...”

<!-- B10-C05-P006 -->
But Krasotkin, after a quick handshake, immediately demonstrated his extraordinary knowledge of social proprieties. First and foremost he turned to the staff captain's wife, seated in her armchair—just then she was terribly displeased, grumbling that the boys were blocking her view of Ilyusha's bed and would not let her see the new dog—and made her an exceedingly polite bow, scraping his foot. Then he turned to Ninochka and gave her, as a lady, the same bow. This polite behavior made an unusually favorable impression on the ailing lady.

<!-- B10-C05-P007 -->
“You can see at once that he's a well-brought-up young man,” she declared loudly, spreading her hands. “Unlike our other visitors, who arrive riding on one another.”

<!-- B10-C05-P008 -->
“But how, Mama dear, on one another? How do you mean?” the staff captain murmured affectionately, though a little anxious on “Mama's” account.

<!-- B10-C05-P009 -->
“That's how they come riding in. One climbs onto another's shoulders in the entryway and rides into a respectable family. What sort of visitor is that?”

<!-- B10-C05-P010 -->
“But who, Mama dear, who came in that way? Who?”

<!-- B10-C05-P011 -->
“Why, this boy rode in on this boy today, and that one on that one...”

<!-- B10-C05-P012 -->
But Kolya was already standing by Ilyusha's bed. The sick boy had visibly paled. He raised himself on the bed and looked long and intently at Kolya. Kolya had not seen his former little friend for about two months, and now he suddenly stopped before him in utter dismay. He had never imagined he would see such a thin, yellow little face, such eyes, burning with fever and seeming to have grown terribly large, such thin little hands. With sorrowful astonishment he watched how deeply and rapidly Ilyusha breathed, how parched his lips were. He stepped toward him, offered his hand, and said, almost completely at a loss:

<!-- B10-C05-P013 -->
“Well, old fellow... how are you getting on?”

<!-- B10-C05-P014 -->
But his voice broke, his easy manner failed him, his face suddenly twitched, and something trembled about his lips. Ilyusha gave him a pained smile, still unable to say a word. Kolya suddenly raised his hand and, for some reason, passed his palm over Ilyusha's hair.

<!-- B10-C05-P015 -->
“It's all right!” he murmured softly, perhaps to encourage him, perhaps without knowing himself why he said it. They were silent again for a minute.

<!-- B10-C05-P016 -->
“What's that you've got, a new puppy?” Kolya suddenly asked in the most unfeeling voice.

<!-- B10-C05-P017 -->
“Ye-e-es!” Ilyusha answered in a long, breathless whisper.

<!-- B10-C05-P018 -->
“A black nose. That means he'll be fierce, one of those dogs you have to chain up,” Kolya observed gravely and firmly, as though the whole point were the puppy and its black nose. The real point was that he was still trying with all his might to master his feelings, so as not to cry like a “little one,” and still could not master them. “When he grows up you'll have to keep him on a chain. I know about these things.”

<!-- B10-C05-P019 -->
“He'll be enormous!” cried one of the boys in the crowd.

<!-- B10-C05-P020 -->
“Of course, he's a Medelyan! Enormous, as big as this, as big as a calf!” several young voices suddenly chimed in.

<!-- B10-C05-P021 -->
“As big as a calf, a real calf, sir,” the staff captain sprang forward. “I made a point of finding one like that, the very fiercest, and his parents are enormous too, and the fiercest dogs you could imagine, this high from the floor... Do sit down, sir, here on Ilyusha's bed, or here on the bench. Welcome, our dear guest, our long-awaited guest... Have you come with Alexei Fyodorovich, sir?”

<!-- B10-C05-P022 -->
Krasotkin sat on the bed at Ilyusha's feet. He might have prepared some easy conversational opening on the way, but now he had completely lost the thread.

<!-- B10-C05-P023 -->
“No... with Perezvon... I've got this dog now, Perezvon. A Slavic name. He's waiting out there... I'll whistle, and he'll fly in. I've brought a dog too,” he suddenly turned to Ilyusha. “Remember Zhuchka, old fellow?” The sudden question struck him like a blow.

<!-- B10-C05-P024 -->
Ilyushechka's little face twisted. He looked at Kolya in anguish. Alyosha, standing by the door, frowned and tried to signal to Kolya discreetly not to mention Zhuchka, but Kolya did not notice, or chose not to notice.

<!-- B10-C05-P025 -->
“Where is... Zhuchka?” Ilyusha asked in a broken little voice.

<!-- B10-C05-P026 -->
“Well, my boy, your Zhuchka—phew! Your Zhuchka's gone!”

<!-- B10-C05-P027 -->
Ilyusha said nothing, but looked long and intently at Kolya again. Catching Kolya's eye, Alyosha again signaled with all his might, but Kolya once more looked away, pretending not to have noticed this time either.

<!-- B10-C05-P028 -->
“Ran off somewhere and vanished. How could she not vanish after a snack like that?” Kolya cut into him mercilessly, though meanwhile he himself seemed for some reason to be growing breathless. “But I've got Perezvon... A Slavic name... I've brought him to see you...”

<!-- B10-C05-P029 -->
“Don't!” Ilyushechka suddenly said.

<!-- B10-C05-P030 -->
“No, no, you must, you absolutely must see him... It'll amuse you. I brought him specially... just as shaggy as she was... May I call my dog in here, madam?” he suddenly asked Mrs. Snegiryov, in a state of excitement that was by now quite incomprehensible.

<!-- B10-C05-P031 -->
“Don't, don't!” Ilyusha cried in a voice broken with grief. Reproach blazed in his eyes.

<!-- B10-C05-P032 -->
“Perhaps you might, sir...” The staff captain suddenly sprang up from the chest by the wall where he had sat down. “Perhaps... some other time, sir...” he faltered. But Kolya, hurrying and insisting beyond restraint, suddenly shouted to Smurov, “Smurov, open the door!” And the moment he did, Kolya blew his whistle. Perezvon came flying into the room.

<!-- B10-C05-P033 -->
“Jump, Perezvon! Beg! Beg!” Kolya shouted, leaping to his feet, and the dog rose on his hind legs and stretched upright in front of Ilyusha's bed. Then something happened that no one had expected: Ilyusha shuddered, suddenly thrust his whole body forward, bent toward Perezvon, and stared at him as though his heart had stopped.

<!-- B10-C05-P034 -->
“That's... Zhuchka!” he suddenly cried in a little voice cracked with suffering and happiness.

<!-- B10-C05-P035 -->
“Who did you think it was?” Krasotkin shouted at the top of his clear, happy voice, and bending down, he put his arms round the dog and lifted him toward Ilyusha.

<!-- B10-C05-P036 -->
“Look, old fellow, see the blind eye and the nick in the left ear? Exactly the marks you told me about. That's how I found him! I found her then, soon afterward. She didn't belong to anybody, she didn't belong to anybody!” he explained, turning quickly toward the staff captain, his wife and Alyosha, then back to Ilyusha. “She was in the Fedotovs' backyard, she'd settled there, but they didn't feed her, and she was a stray, she'd strayed in from the country... So I found her... You see, old fellow, that means she didn't swallow your piece of bread. If she'd swallowed it she certainly would have died, of course she would! So she must have managed to spit it out, since she's alive now. You just didn't notice her spit it out. She spat it out, but pricked her tongue all the same, and that's why she yelped. She ran off yelping, and you thought she'd swallowed the whole thing. She must have yelped terribly, because the skin inside a dog's mouth is very tender... more tender than a person's, much more tender!” Kolya exclaimed wildly, his flushed face radiant with delight.

<!-- B10-C05-P037 -->
Ilyusha could not speak at all. He stared at Kolya with his great, terribly bulging eyes, his mouth open, white as a sheet. Had the unsuspecting Krasotkin only known how painfully, how fatally such a moment might affect the sick boy's health, he would never have dared play the trick he had played. But perhaps Alyosha was the only one in the room who understood this. As for the staff captain, he seemed to have become the youngest child of all.

<!-- B10-C05-P038 -->
“Zhuchka! So this is Zhuchka?” he cried blissfully. “Ilyushechka, it's Zhuchka, your Zhuchka! Mama, it's Zhuchka!” He was almost crying.

<!-- B10-C05-P039 -->
“And I never guessed!” Smurov exclaimed ruefully. “Oh, that Krasotkin! I said he'd find Zhuchka, and he has!”

<!-- B10-C05-P040 -->
“He has!” someone else echoed joyfully.

<!-- B10-C05-P041 -->
“Well done, Krasotkin!” rang out a third young voice.

<!-- B10-C05-P042 -->
“Well done, well done!” all the boys shouted, and began to clap.

<!-- B10-C05-P043 -->
“But wait, wait!” Krasotkin struggled to shout them down. “I'll tell you how it happened—the point is how it happened, nothing else! I found him, dragged him home and hid him at once, locked up the house and wouldn't show him to anyone until the very last day. Only Smurov found out two weeks ago, but I convinced him it was Perezvon and he didn't guess. In the meantime I taught Zhuchka everything: just look, just look at the tricks he knows! That's why I taught him, so I could bring him to you all trained and polished, old fellow. Here, old fellow, look at your Zhuchka now! Haven't you got a piece of beef somewhere? He'll show you a trick that'll make you fall over laughing—a little beef, a piece, surely you have some?”

<!-- B10-C05-P044 -->
The staff captain rushed through the entryway into the landlady's quarters, where his family's food was also cooked. Kolya, desperately hurrying so as not to waste precious time, shouted to Perezvon, “Die!” The dog immediately spun round, lay on his back, and froze motionless with all four paws in the air. The boys laughed; Ilyusha watched with his former pained smile, but the one who most enjoyed Perezvon's dying was “Mama.” She burst out laughing at the dog, snapped her fingers and called:

<!-- B10-C05-P045 -->
“Perezvon, Perezvon!”

<!-- B10-C05-P046 -->
“He won't get up for anything, not for anything!” Kolya cried triumphantly, with justifiable pride. “The whole world could shout, but if I call he'll jump up in an instant! Ici, Perezvon!”

<!-- B10-C05-P047 -->
The dog sprang up and began jumping about, yelping with joy. The staff captain ran in with a piece of boiled beef.

<!-- B10-C05-P048 -->
“Is it hot?” Kolya asked quickly and businesslike as he took the piece. “No, it's not hot. Dogs don't like hot food. Now everyone watch! Ilyushechka, look—look, look, old fellow, why aren't you looking? I've brought him, and he won't look!”

<!-- B10-C05-P049 -->
The new trick consisted of placing a delicious morsel of beef on the very nose of the dog, who stood motionless with his muzzle stretched out. The unfortunate animal had to stand with the piece on his nose for as long as his master ordered, without moving or stirring, even for half an hour. But Perezvon was kept waiting only the briefest moment.

<!-- B10-C05-P050 -->
“Catch!” Kolya shouted, and in an instant the piece flew from Perezvon's nose into his mouth. The audience, of course, expressed delighted amazement.

<!-- B10-C05-P051 -->
“Can it really be, can it really be that you stayed away all this time just to train the dog?” Alyosha exclaimed, unable to keep the reproach from his voice.

<!-- B10-C05-P052 -->
“Exactly!” Kolya cried with perfect innocence. “I wanted to show him off in all his glory!”

<!-- B10-C05-P053 -->
“Perezvon! Perezvon!” Ilyusha suddenly began snapping his thin little fingers, beckoning to the dog.

<!-- B10-C05-P054 -->
“Why bother? Let him jump onto the bed himself. Ici, Perezvon!” Kolya slapped the bed with his palm, and Perezvon shot up beside Ilyusha like an arrow. Ilyusha immediately put both arms round his head, and Perezvon promptly licked his cheek in return. Ilyushechka pressed close to him, stretched out on his bed, and hid his face from everyone in the dog's shaggy fur.

<!-- B10-C05-P055 -->
“Lord, Lord!” exclaimed the staff captain. Kolya sat down on Ilyusha's bed again.

<!-- B10-C05-P056 -->
“Ilyusha, I can show you something else. I've brought you a little cannon. Remember, I told you about it before, and you said, ‘Oh, how I'd like to see it!’ Well, I've brought it now.”

<!-- B10-C05-P057 -->
And Kolya hurriedly took his little bronze cannon out of his bag. He was in such a hurry because he was so happy himself: at another time he would have waited for the effect produced by Perezvon to subside, but now he rushed ahead, scorning all restraint. “You're happy already, so here's some more happiness!” He himself was quite intoxicated.

<!-- B10-C05-P058 -->
“I spotted this little thing a long time ago at the official Morozov's—for you, old fellow, for you. He had it standing there doing nothing; he'd got it from his brother. I traded him a book for it, from Papa's bookcase: *A Relative of Mohammed, or Healing Foolishness*. The book's a hundred years old, a wild thing, published in Moscow before there was any censorship. Morozov's keen on that sort of thing. He even thanked me...”

<!-- B10-C05-P059 -->
Kolya held the cannon out in his hand so everyone could see and enjoy it. Ilyusha raised himself up, still keeping his right arm round Perezvon, and gazed in delight at the toy. The excitement reached its height when Kolya announced that he had gunpowder too, and that they could fire it right away, “if it wouldn't disturb the ladies.” “Mama” immediately asked to have a closer look at the toy, and it was handed to her at once. She was enormously taken with the little bronze cannon on its wheels and began rolling it about on her lap. Asked for permission to fire it, she gave her fullest consent, though she did not understand what she was being asked. Kolya showed them the powder and shot. The staff captain, as a former military man, took charge of loading it himself, putting in the tiniest quantity of powder, but asking that the shot be left for another time. They set the cannon on the floor, pointed into an empty space, pressed three grains of powder into the touchhole and lit them with a match. It went off magnificently. Mama gave a start, but immediately laughed for joy. The boys watched in silent triumph, but the most blissful of all was the staff captain as he looked at Ilyusha. Kolya picked up the little cannon and immediately gave it to Ilyusha, together with the shot and powder.

<!-- B10-C05-P060 -->
“It's for you, for you! I've had it ready for a long time,” he repeated once more, brimming with happiness.

<!-- B10-C05-P061 -->
“Oh, give it to me! No, give the little cannon to me instead!” Mama suddenly began begging, like a little child. Her face filled with painful anxiety at the fear that they would not give it to her. Kolya was embarrassed. The staff captain grew agitated.

<!-- B10-C05-P062 -->
“Mama dear, Mama dear!” He rushed up to her. “The cannon is yours, yours, but let Ilyusha keep it, because it was given to him. It's just the same as being yours; Ilyushechka will always let you play with it. You can share it, share it...”

<!-- B10-C05-P063 -->
“No, I don't want to share it. No, I want it all for myself, not Ilyusha's,” Mama went on, quite ready now to burst into tears.

<!-- B10-C05-P064 -->
“Mama, take it, here, take it!” Ilyusha suddenly cried. “Krasotkin, may I give it to Mama?” he asked Krasotkin pleadingly, as though afraid he might be offended at having his present given to someone else.

<!-- B10-C05-P065 -->
“Certainly!” Krasotkin agreed at once, and taking the cannon from Ilyusha, he handed it to Mama himself with the most polite of bows. She even wept with tenderness.

<!-- B10-C05-P066 -->
“Ilyushechka, darling, here's someone who loves his mama!” she exclaimed tenderly, and immediately began rolling the cannon about on her lap again.

<!-- B10-C05-P067 -->
“Mama dear, let me kiss your hand,” her husband said, springing over to her and carrying out his intention at once.

<!-- B10-C05-P068 -->
“And here's another very sweet young man, this kind boy!” the grateful lady said, pointing to Krasotkin.

<!-- B10-C05-P069 -->
“And now I'll bring you as much gunpowder as you like, Ilyusha. We make it ourselves now. Borovikov found out the formula: twenty-four parts saltpeter, ten sulfur and six birch charcoal, grind it all together, pour in water, mix it into a paste and rub it through a drumskin—and there's your gunpowder.”

<!-- B10-C05-P070 -->
“Smurov's already told me about your powder, but Papa says it isn't real gunpowder,” Ilyusha answered.

<!-- B10-C05-P071 -->
“What do you mean, not real?” Kolya blushed. “Ours burns. Still, I don't know...”

<!-- B10-C05-P072 -->
“No, sir, I meant nothing,” the staff captain suddenly sprang forward, looking guilty. “It's true I said that real gunpowder isn't made that way, but that doesn't matter, sir, this way will do too.”

<!-- B10-C05-P073 -->
“I don't know, you know better. We lit some in a stone pomade jar; it burned splendidly, all of it burned, with only the tiniest bit of soot left. But that was only the paste; if you rub it through a skin... Still, you know better, I don't know... And Bulkin's father whipped him for our gunpowder, did you hear?” he suddenly asked Ilyusha.

<!-- B10-C05-P074 -->
“I heard,” Ilyusha answered. He was listening to Kolya with boundless interest and pleasure.

<!-- B10-C05-P075 -->
“We'd made a whole bottle of powder, and he kept it under the bed. His father saw it. It could blow up, he said. And he whipped him then and there. He wanted to complain about me at school. Now he won't let him see me; nobody's allowed to see me now. Smurov isn't allowed either. I'm notorious with everyone; they say I'm ‘reckless,’” Kolya smiled contemptuously. “It all started here with the railway.”

<!-- B10-C05-P076 -->
“Oh, we've heard about that adventure of yours too!” exclaimed the staff captain. “How did you manage to lie there? Can you really have felt no fear at all, lying under the train? Were you frightened, sir?”

<!-- B10-C05-P077 -->
The staff captain was fawning terribly on Kolya.

<!-- B10-C05-P078 -->
“N-not particularly!” Kolya replied carelessly. “It was that damned goose that did most damage to my reputation here,” he turned back to Ilyusha. But although he affected carelessness as he spoke, he still could not master himself and kept seeming to falter in his chosen tone.

<!-- B10-C05-P079 -->
“Oh, I've heard about the goose too!” Ilyusha laughed, beaming all over. “They told me, but I didn't understand. Did the magistrate really try you?”

<!-- B10-C05-P080 -->
“A completely brainless affair, absolutely trivial, which as usual they made a mountain out of here,” Kolya began easily. “I was walking through the square one day, just as they'd driven in some geese. I stopped to look at them. Suddenly a local fellow, Vishnyakov—he works as a messenger for the Plotnikovs now—looked at me and said, ‘What are you staring at the geese for?’ I looked at him: a stupid, round mug, a fellow of twenty. You know, I never turn away from the common people. I like being with the people... We've lost touch with the people—that's an axiom—you appear to be laughing, Karamazov?”

<!-- B10-C05-P081 -->
“No, God forbid, I'm listening very attentively,” Alyosha replied with the most innocent expression, and the suspicious Kolya immediately took heart.

<!-- B10-C05-P082 -->
“My theory, Karamazov, is clear and simple,” he immediately began rushing on again with renewed delight. “I believe in the people and am always glad to do them justice, but without spoiling them in the least; that's a sine qua...[^b10-c05-n001] Yes, but I was talking about the goose. So I turned to this fool and answered, ‘I'm wondering what the goose is thinking.’ He looked at me quite stupidly. ‘And what is the goose thinking?’ he said. ‘Well, look,’ I said, ‘there's a cart full of oats. Oats are spilling out of a sack, and the goose has stretched its neck right under the wheel to peck at the grain—see?’ ‘I see that very well,’ he said. ‘Well then,’ I said, ‘if you moved that very cart forward just a little now, would the wheel cut through the goose's neck or not?’ ‘It certainly would,’ he said, grinning from ear to ear, completely won over. ‘Well, come on then, my lad,’ I said, ‘let's do it.’ ‘Let's,’ he said.
