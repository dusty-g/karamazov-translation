# Chapter I — Fyodor Pavlovich Karamazov

*Part I · Book I: The Story of One Little Family*

<!-- B01-C01-P001 -->
Alexei Fyodorovich Karamazov was the third son of a landowner in our district, Fyodor Pavlovich Karamazov, so well known in his day—and still remembered here—for his tragic and murky death, which occurred exactly thirteen years ago and which I will describe in its proper place. For now, I will say only this about the “landowner,” as we called him here, though he had scarcely ever lived on his estate: he was a strange type, though one met with rather often, a man who was not only vile and debauched but muddleheaded as well—one of those muddleheaded men, however, who are very good at managing their own little financial affairs, and apparently nothing else.

Fyodor Pavlovich, for instance, began with almost nothing. He was a landowner on the smallest scale, running from one person's table to another for his dinner, angling to become a hanger-on; yet at his death he turned out to have as much as a hundred thousand rubles in ready money. And all the while, throughout his life, he remained one of the most muddleheaded eccentrics in our entire district. Let me repeat: this was not stupidity; most of these eccentrics are quite intelligent and cunning. It was precisely muddleheadedness, and of some peculiar, national kind at that.

He married twice and had three sons: the eldest, Dmitri Fyodorovich, by his first wife, and the other two, Ivan and Alexei, by his second. Fyodor Pavlovich's first wife came from the fairly wealthy and distinguished noble family of the Miusovs, who also owned land in our district. I won't try to explain at length just how a girl with a dowry, who was beautiful as well, and moreover one of those spirited, intelligent young women so common among us in the present generation, though they had already begun appearing in the previous one, could marry such a worthless “puny little wretch,” as everyone called him then.

After all, I myself knew a young woman, back in the “romantic” generation before last, who spent several years mysteriously in love with a certain gentleman whom she could, incidentally, have married at any time without the least trouble. Yet she ended by inventing insurmountable obstacles for herself and, on a stormy night, throwing herself from a high, cliff-like bank into a rather deep and fast-flowing river, where she died entirely on account of her own whims, solely to resemble Shakespeare's Ophelia. Indeed, had this cliff, which she had picked out and grown fond of so long before, been less picturesque, had there been only a prosaic, flat riverbank in its place, the suicide might never have happened at all. This is a true occurrence, and one must suppose that quite a few such occurrences, or others of the same sort, have taken place in our Russian life over the last two or three generations.

Adelaida Ivanovna Miusova's action was likewise, without doubt, an echo of outside influences, and likewise the agitation of captive thought.[^b01-c01-n009] Perhaps she wanted to assert women's independence, to defy social conventions, to defy the despotism of her relatives and family; and her obliging imagination persuaded her, let us say for just one moment, that despite his rank of hanger-on, Fyodor Pavlovich was still one of the boldest and most mocking men of that age of transition to everything better, whereas he was merely a malicious buffoon, and nothing more. The affair had the added spice of an elopement, which greatly tempted Adelaida Ivanovna. As for Fyodor Pavlovich, even his social position at the time had left him well prepared for adventures of this kind, since he passionately wished to make his way in the world by any means whatever; attaching himself to a good family and getting hold of a dowry was very tempting.

As for mutual love, there seems to have been none at all, on either the bride's side or his, despite Adelaida Ivanovna's beauty. So this may have been the only instance of its kind in the life of Fyodor Pavlovich, a man of the most voracious sensuality all his days, ready in an instant to cling to any skirt at all, if only it beckoned him. And yet this woman alone aroused no particular passion in him.

Immediately after the elopement, Adelaida Ivanovna saw at once that she felt only contempt for her husband, and nothing more. The consequences of the marriage thus became apparent with extraordinary speed. Although her family came to terms with what had happened quite soon and gave the runaway bride her dowry, the couple began a life of utter disorder and endless scenes. People said that the young wife showed incomparably more nobility and elevation of spirit in all this than Fyodor Pavlovich, who, as is now known, filched all her money at one go, as much as twenty-five thousand rubles, the moment she received it, so that from then on those little thousands might as well have sunk into the water as far as she was concerned. As for the small village and quite fine town house that also came to her as a dowry, he tried for a long time, with all his might, to have them transferred to his own name by drawing up some suitable legal document. And he would certainly have succeeded: she would have given in simply out of contempt and disgust for him, so to speak, which his shameless extortion and begging aroused in her every minute—out of sheer weariness of spirit, just to make him leave her alone. Fortunately, Adelaida Ivanovna's family intervened and reined in the grasping fellow.

It is known for certain that there were frequent fights between the couple, but according to the story handed down, it was not Fyodor Pavlovich who did the beating; it was Adelaida Ivanovna, a passionate, bold, dark-complexioned, impatient woman gifted with remarkable physical strength. Finally she left the house and ran away from Fyodor Pavlovich with a seminarian-teacher who was all but dying of poverty, leaving three-year-old Mitya on Fyodor Pavlovich's hands.

Fyodor Pavlovich promptly installed a whole harem in the house and took to the wildest drinking; during the intermissions he traveled over almost the entire province, tearfully complaining to one and all about Adelaida Ivanovna, who had deserted him, and revealing details of his married life that a husband ought to have been far too ashamed to reveal. Above all, he seemed to enjoy, and even feel flattered by, playing his ridiculous role of the wronged husband before everyone, even embellishing the details of the wrong he had suffered. “You'd think you'd been awarded a rank, Fyodor Pavlovich, you're so pleased with yourself, despite all your sorrow,” the mockers told him. Many even added that he was happy to appear in a new guise as a buffoon, and that he deliberately pretended not to notice his own comic position in order to get a bigger laugh.

Who knows, though—perhaps it was naivety on his part. At last he managed to pick up the trail of his runaway wife.

The poor woman turned out to be in Petersburg, where she had moved with her seminarian and thrown herself wholeheartedly into the fullest possible emancipation. Fyodor Pavlovich immediately began bustling about and getting ready to go to Petersburg—what for? Of course, he had no idea himself. Really, he might have gone then; but once he had made up his mind, he promptly considered himself especially entitled, to fortify himself for the journey, to plunge once more into boundless drinking. And it was just then that his wife's family received news of her death in Petersburg.

Somehow she had died quite suddenly, somewhere in an attic—of typhus, according to some accounts; supposedly of starvation, according to others. Fyodor Pavlovich was drunk when he learned of his wife's death; they say he ran down the street and began shouting, joyfully raising his hands to heaven, “Now lettest thou…”[^b01-c01-n003] According to others, he sobbed like a little child, so much that, they say, even with all the disgust he inspired, you could not help feeling sorry for him at the sight.

It may very well be that both happened: that he rejoiced in his release and wept for the woman who had released him—all at once. In most cases, people—even villains—are far more naive and simplehearted than we generally suppose. And so are we ourselves.

[^b01-c01-n003]: The opening of Simeon’s prayer in Luke 2:29, known as the *Nunc dimittis* and sung at Orthodox Vespers. The familiar English continuation is “thy servant depart in peace.”

[^b01-c01-n009]: This phrase echoes Mikhail Lermontov’s “Do Not Trust Yourself” (1839), whose opening warns a young dreamer against mistaking troubled feeling for inspiration.
