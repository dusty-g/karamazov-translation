# Book XII: A Judicial Error
## VI. The Prosecutor’s Speech: A Study of Character

<!-- B12-C06-P001 -->
Ippolit Kirillovich began his speech for the prosecution shaking all over with a nervous tremor, a cold, sickly sweat on his forehead and temples, his whole body alternating between chills and heat. He described it himself afterward. He considered this speech his *chef d’œuvre*, the *chef d’œuvre* of his entire life, his swan song. True, nine months later he died of a virulent consumption, so that, as it turned out, he would indeed have been entitled to compare himself to a swan singing its last song, had he foreseen his end. Into this speech he put his whole heart and all the intelligence he possessed, unexpectedly revealing that both civic feeling and the “accursed” questions had lain hidden within him, at least insofar as our poor Ippolit Kirillovich could contain them. Above all, his words carried weight because they were sincere: he sincerely believed the defendant guilty; he accused him neither to order nor merely in the discharge of his office, and in calling for “retribution” he really did tremble with the desire to “save society.” Even the ladies in our audience, ultimately hostile to Ippolit Kirillovich, admitted the extraordinary impression he made. He began in a cracked, faltering voice, but very soon it strengthened and rang through the whole hall, remaining so until the end of his speech. The moment he finished, however, he almost fainted.

“Gentlemen of the jury,” the prosecutor began, “the present case has resounded throughout Russia. Yet what, one might ask, is there to wonder at? What is there to find so particularly horrifying? For us, for us above all? We are so accustomed to all this! That is our horror: dark deeds like these have almost ceased to horrify us! It is our familiarity with them that ought to horrify us, not an isolated atrocity committed by one individual or another. Where, then, are the causes of our indifference, our barely lukewarm response to such affairs, to such signs of the times foretelling an unenviable future? In our cynicism, in the premature exhaustion of mind and imagination in our society, still so young and yet so untimely decrepit? In our moral principles, shaken to their foundations, or finally in the possibility that we have no such moral principles at all? I do not resolve these questions; nevertheless, they are agonizing, and every citizen not merely ought but is obliged to suffer from them. Our fledgling, still timid press has nonetheless already rendered society certain services, for without it we should never have learned with anything approaching completeness of those horrors of unbridled will and moral degradation which it continually reports in its pages to everyone, no longer only to those who visit the halls of the new public courts granted us in the present reign. And what do we read almost every day? Oh, continually we read of things beside which even this case pales and seems almost commonplace. But most important of all, so many of our Russian, our national criminal cases testify to something general, to some common calamity that has made its home among us, and which, as a universal evil, is already difficult to fight. Here a brilliant young officer of high society, scarcely beginning his life and career, vilely, in silence, without the slightest remorse, stabs to death a petty official who had in part been his benefactor, and the man’s servant woman, in order to steal his own promissory note and, along with it, the official’s remaining money: ‘It will come in handy for my fashionable pleasures and my future career.’ Having stabbed them both, he leaves, placing pillows beneath the heads of the two corpses. There a young hero, covered in crosses for bravery, murders on the highway, like a brigand, the mother of his commander and benefactor, and in recruiting his accomplices assures them that ‘she loves him like her own son, and will therefore follow all his advice and take no precautions.’ Let him be a monster; but now, in our time, I no longer dare say that he is merely an isolated monster. Another man may not do the stabbing, yet think and feel exactly as he does, be just as dishonorable in his soul. In silence, alone with his conscience, perhaps he asks himself: ‘What is honor, after all, and are ties of blood merely a prejudice?’ Perhaps people will cry out against me, saying that I am sick, hysterical, slandering monstrously, raving, exaggerating. Let them, let them—and God, how gladly I would be the first to welcome it! Oh, do not believe me, regard me as a sick man, but remember my words all the same: even if only a tenth, only a twentieth of what I say is true, even then it is terrible! Look, gentlemen, look at how our young people shoot themselves: oh, without the slightest Hamlet-like question about ‘What will there be beyond?’ without a trace of such questions, as though that whole article concerning our spirit and all that awaits us beyond the grave had long since been erased from their nature, buried and covered with sand. Look, finally, at our depravity, our sensualists. Fyodor Pavlovich, the unfortunate victim in the present trial, is almost an innocent infant beside some of them. And yet we all knew him; ‘he lived among us’… Yes, perhaps one day our own leading minds and those of Europe will study the psychology of Russian crime, for the subject deserves it. But that study will take place sometime later, at leisure, when all the tragic disorder of our present moment has receded farther into the distance, so that it can be viewed more intelligently and impartially than people such as myself, for instance, can view it. For now we either recoil in horror, or pretend to, while in fact savoring the spectacle as connoisseurs of strong, eccentric sensations that stir our cynically lazy idleness; or, finally, like little children, we wave the terrible phantoms away with our hands and bury our heads in a pillow until the dreadful vision passes, only to forget it immediately afterward in merriment and games. But someday we too must begin to live soberly and thoughtfully, we too must look at ourselves as a society, we too must make sense of something in our common affairs, or at least begin to do so. A great writer of the preceding age, at the close of his greatest work, portraying all Russia as a spirited Russian troika racing toward an unknown destination, exclaims: ‘Ah, troika, birdlike troika, who invented you!’[^b12-c06-n001] And in proud rapture he adds that all nations respectfully stand aside before the troika galloping headlong. Well, gentlemen, let them, let them stand aside, respectfully or otherwise; but in my sinful opinion, that artist of genius ended in this way either in a fit of childishly innocent idealism or simply from fear of the censorship of his day. For if one harnesses only his own heroes to his troika, the Sobakeviches, Nozdryovs, and Chichikovs, whoever one puts in the driver’s seat, one will get nowhere worthwhile with horses like those! And those are only the old horses, a far cry from today’s; ours are something else again…”

Here Ippolit Kirillovich’s speech was interrupted by applause. The liberal interpretation of the Russian troika had pleased the audience. Admittedly, only two or three bursts of handclapping escaped, so that the presiding judge did not even find it necessary to threaten to “clear the hall,” but merely looked sternly toward the applauders. Yet Ippolit Kirillovich was encouraged: no one had ever applauded him before! For so many years people had refused to listen to the man, and suddenly he had the chance to speak his mind to all Russia!

“Indeed,” he continued, “what is this Karamazov family, which has suddenly earned such unhappy fame throughout the whole of Russia? Perhaps I exaggerate too much, but it seems to me that in the picture of this little family certain basic elements common to our modern educated society seem to glimmer—oh, not all its elements, and only in microscopic form, ‘like the sun in a little drop of water,’ yet something has been reflected, something has found expression. Look at this unfortunate, unbridled, depraved old man, this ‘father of a family,’ whose existence ended so unhappily. A hereditary nobleman who began his career as a penniless dependent, who through an unlooked-for and unexpected marriage seized a little capital in dowry; at first a petty rogue and a flattering buffoon, with the beginnings of intellectual abilities that were, indeed, far from negligible, and above all a moneylender. With the years—that is, as his little capital grows—he gains confidence. His abasement and fawning disappear, leaving only a mocking, malicious cynic and sensualist. His spiritual side is utterly obliterated, while his thirst for life is immense. It comes to this: he sees nothing in life beyond sensual pleasure, and teaches his children accordingly. Any spiritual duties as a father—none. He laughs at them; he brings up his little children in the back yard and is glad when they are taken away. He even forgets them altogether. The old man’s entire moral code is *après moi le déluge*.[^b12-c06-n002] Everything contrary to the very idea of a citizen: complete, even hostile separation from society. ‘Let the whole world burn, as long as I alone am comfortable.’ And he is comfortable, he is entirely satisfied, he longs to live another twenty or thirty years like this. He cheats his own son, and with the son’s own money, the inheritance from his mother that he refuses to give him, he steals his mistress from him—from his own son. No, I have no intention of surrendering the defendant’s defense to the highly talented counsel who has come from Petersburg. I too will speak the truth; I too understand the sum of indignation he accumulated in his son’s heart. But enough, enough of this unfortunate old man; he has received his recompense. Let us remember, however, that he is a father, and one of the fathers of our day. Shall I offend society by saying that he is even one of many contemporary fathers? Alas, so many contemporary fathers merely do not express themselves as cynically as he did, being better bred and better educated, while essentially their philosophy is almost the same. But let me be a pessimist, let me. We have already agreed that you will forgive me. Let us agree beforehand: do not believe me, do not believe me; I shall speak, and you need not believe. Yet let me speak my mind all the same, and do not forget everything I say. Now, however, here are the children of this old man, this father of a family. One is before us in the dock; everything I have to say about him lies ahead. Of the others I shall speak only in passing. The elder of these others is one of our modern young men, brilliantly educated, with a fairly powerful mind, yet already believing in nothing, rejecting and dismissing much, far too much in life, exactly like his parent. We have all heard him; he was received cordially in our society. He did not conceal his opinions—quite the contrary, entirely the contrary—which emboldens me to speak of him now with some frankness, naturally not as a private individual, but only as a member of the Karamazov family. Yesterday, here on the outskirts of town, a sickly idiot closely involved in the present case died by his own hand: Smerdyakov, a former servant and possibly an illegitimate son of Fyodor Pavlovich. During the preliminary investigation he told me with hysterical tears how this young Karamazov, Ivan Fyodorovich, had terrified him with his spiritual lawlessness. ‘According to him, everything in the world is permitted, whatever it may be, and nothing must be forbidden from now on—that’s what he kept teaching me.’ It seems that this proposition he had been taught finally drove the idiot mad, although his epilepsy and the whole terrible catastrophe that had broken over their household naturally contributed to his mental disorder. But one very, very curious observation flashed out of this idiot, one that would have done credit to a far more intelligent observer, which is precisely why I have mentioned all this. ‘If there is one son,’ he told me, ‘whose character is more like Fyodor Pavlovich’s than the others, it is him, Ivan Fyodorovich!’ At that observation I shall break off the portrait I have begun, considering it indelicate to go further. Oh, I do not wish to draw further conclusions and croak like a raven of nothing but ruin for a young life. Even today, here in this hall, we saw that the direct power of truth still lives in his young heart, that feelings of family affection have not yet been stifled in him by unbelief and moral cynicism, acquired more by inheritance than through genuine suffering of thought. Then there is the other son—oh, still a youth, devout and humble, who, in contrast to his brother’s dark, corrupting worldview, seeks to attach himself, so to speak, to ‘the principles of the people,’ or to what is called by that abstruse phrase in certain theoretical corners of our thinking intelligentsia. He attached himself to a monastery, you see; he very nearly took monastic vows himself. In him, it seems to me, unconsciously and so early, there found expression that timid despair with which so many in our poor society now, frightened by its cynicism and depravity and mistakenly attributing all the evil to European enlightenment, throw themselves, as they say, upon the ‘native soil,’ into the maternal embrace of their own land, so to speak, like children frightened by phantoms, longing at the withered breast of an enfeebled mother merely to fall peacefully asleep, even to sleep their whole lives away, if only they need not see the horrors that frighten them. For my part I wish this kind and gifted young man all the best; I wish that his youthful idealism and striving toward the people’s principles may not later turn, as so often happens, into dark mysticism on the moral side and mindless chauvinism on the civic side—two qualities that threaten the nation with perhaps even greater evil than that premature corruption by a misunderstood and effortlessly acquired European enlightenment from which his elder brother suffers.”

Chauvinism and mysticism provoked another two or three bursts of clapping. Ippolit Kirillovich had certainly been carried away, and all this had little bearing on the case, quite apart from being rather unclear; but the consumptive, embittered man had wanted so badly to speak his mind for once in his life. People in our town afterward said that his description of Ivan Fyodorovich had been prompted by a rather indelicate feeling, because Ivan had once or twice put him in his place during public arguments, and Ippolit Kirillovich remembered it and now wanted revenge. But I do not know whether one could draw that conclusion. In any event, all this had merely been an introduction; his speech then became more direct and closer to the matter at hand.

“But here is the third son of the father of a modern family,” Ippolit Kirillovich continued. “He is in the dock, before us. Before us too are his exploits, his life and deeds: the hour has come, and everything has unfolded, everything has been revealed. In contrast to his brothers’ ‘Europeanism’ and ‘principles of the people,’ he seems to represent spontaneous Russia—oh, not all of it, not all, and God forbid that it should be all! And yet there she is, our dear Russia; you can smell her, hear her, our little mother! Oh, we are spontaneous, we are good and evil in the most astonishing mixture; we love enlightenment and Schiller, and at the same time we rampage through taverns and tear the little beards off drunkards, our drinking companions. Oh, we too can be good and beautiful, but only when things are good and beautiful for ourselves. Indeed, we are even swept away—yes, swept away—by the noblest ideals, but only on condition that they attain themselves, fall onto our table from heaven, and, above all, come free, free, with nothing for us to pay. We hate paying, but we love receiving, and that applies to everything. Oh, give us, give us every blessing life can offer—every blessing, precisely; we will settle for nothing less—and above all place no restraint whatever on our temperament, and then we too will prove that we can be good and beautiful. We are not greedy, no; nevertheless, give us money, more, more, as much money as possible, and you will see how generously, with what contempt for filthy lucre, we scatter it in a single night of unrestrained revelry. And if we are given no money, we will show how we can get it when we really want to. But more of that later; let us follow the matter in order. First of all we have before us a poor, abandoned boy, ‘in the back yard with no boots,’ as our esteemed and respected fellow citizen, alas of foreign origin, put it earlier. Once more I repeat: I will yield the defendant’s defense to no one! I am both prosecutor and defender. Yes, we too are people, we too are human beings, and we too know how to weigh the effect on a character of the earliest impressions of childhood and the family nest. But now the boy is a youth, a young man, an officer; for violent behavior and a challenge to a duel, he is sent to one of the remote frontier towns of our blessed Russia. There he serves, and there he carouses; and of course a great ship needs a great voyage. We need means, gentlemen, means above all, and so, after lengthy disputes, he and his father agree on a final six thousand rubles, which are sent to him. Note that he gave a document, and a letter of his exists in which he virtually renounces the remainder and brings his dispute with his father over the inheritance to an end with those six thousand. Here he meets a young woman of lofty character and cultivation. Oh, I dare not repeat the details; you have only just heard them. Here is honor, here is self-sacrifice, and I fall silent. The image of a frivolous, dissolute young man nevertheless bowing before true nobility, before a higher ideal, flashed before us in a most sympathetic light. But then suddenly, here in this same courtroom, quite unexpectedly came the reverse side of the medal. Again, I dare not indulge in conjecture, and shall refrain from analyzing why this happened. Yet there were reasons why it happened. That same person, in tears of long-concealed indignation, tells us that he, he himself, was the first to despise her for her rash, perhaps unrestrained impulse, which was nevertheless lofty, nevertheless generous. On his face, her fiancé’s, first of all appeared the mocking smile she could not bear from him alone. Knowing he had already betrayed her—betrayed her in the conviction that henceforth she must endure everything from him, even his betrayal—knowing this, she deliberately offers him three thousand rubles, making it clear, all too clear, that she is offering him money with which to betray her: ‘Well, will you accept it? Are you cynical enough?’ she silently asks him with her judging, testing gaze. He looks at her, understands her thoughts perfectly—he himself admitted here before you that he understood everything—and simply appropriates those three thousand and squanders them in two days with his new beloved! Which are we to believe? The first legend, the impulse of lofty nobility that gives away its last means of subsistence and bows before virtue, or the reverse side of the medal, so repulsive? Ordinarily in life, when faced with two opposites, we must seek the truth between them; in the present case, that is literally untrue. Most likely, in the first instance he was sincerely noble, and in the second just as sincerely base. Why? Precisely because ours are broad, Karamazov natures—that is the point toward which I am leading—capable of encompassing every possible contradiction and contemplating both abysses at once: the abyss above us, the abyss of the highest ideals, and the abyss below us, the abyss of the lowest, most fetid degradation. Recall the brilliant thought expressed earlier by that young observer who has studied the whole Karamazov family deeply and at close range, Mr. Rakitin: ‘The sensation of baseness in their fall is as necessary to these unbridled, unrestrained natures as the sensation of the loftiest nobility.’ And it is true: they need precisely this unnatural mixture constantly and without interruption. Two abysses, two abysses, gentlemen, at one and the same moment—without them we are unhappy and unsatisfied, our existence incomplete. We are broad, broad as all our mother Russia; we can contain everything and live with everything! Incidentally, gentlemen of the jury, now that we have touched on these three thousand rubles, I shall permit myself to look a little ahead. Just imagine that he, a man of this character, having received that money, and in such a way, through such shame, such disgrace, such utter humiliation—just imagine that on that same day he supposedly managed to set aside half of it, sew it into a pouch, and then for an entire month had the strength to wear it around his neck despite every temptation and the most pressing needs! Neither in drunken tavern revels nor when he had to fly out of town to obtain money from God knows whom, money he desperately needed to carry his beloved away from the temptations offered by his rival, his father, did he dare touch that pouch. Why, if only to avoid leaving his beloved exposed to the temptations of the old man of whom he was so jealous, he ought to have opened his pouch and stayed at home as her unremitting watchman, awaiting the moment when she would finally tell him, ‘I am yours,’ so that he could fly away with her somewhere far from their present fatal surroundings. But no, he does not touch his talisman, and on what pretext? The original pretext, we have said, was precisely that when she told him, ‘I am yours, take me wherever you like,’ he would have the means to take her away. But this first pretext, by the defendant’s own account, paled before a second. As long, he says, as I carry this money on me, ‘I am a scoundrel, but not a thief,’ because I can always go to the fiancée I have wronged, and, laying before her this half of the entire sum I obtained from her by deceit, always say: ‘You see, I squandered half your money and thereby proved myself a weak and immoral man, a scoundrel if you like’—I am using the defendant’s own language—‘but though a scoundrel, I am no thief, because if I were a thief I would not have brought you this remaining half, but would have appropriated it too, as I did the first half.’ An astonishing explanation of the fact! This same violent but weak man, who could not resist the temptation to accept three thousand rubles in such disgrace, suddenly discovers such stoic firmness within himself that he carries thousands around his neck without daring to touch them! Is this remotely consistent with the character we are examining? No, and I shall permit myself to tell you how the real Dmitri Karamazov would have acted in such a case, even if he had actually decided to sew his money into a pouch. At the first temptation—say, to give some fresh pleasure to that same new beloved with whom he had already squandered the first half of the money—he would have unstitched his pouch and taken out, let us suppose, only a hundred rubles to begin with; for why must he return precisely half, that is, fifteen hundred? Fourteen hundred would be enough; it would come to the same thing: ‘A scoundrel, but not a thief, since I brought back at least fourteen hundred rubles, whereas a thief would have taken everything and brought back nothing.’ Then, a little later, he would have unstitched the pouch again and taken out a second hundred, then a third, then a fourth, and by the end of the month at the latest he would have taken out the next-to-last hundred: ‘Even if I bring back only one hundred, it comes to the same thing: a scoundrel, but not a thief. I squandered twenty-nine hundred, but still returned one hundred; a thief wouldn’t have returned even that.’ Finally, having squandered that next-to-last hundred, he would have looked at the last and said to himself: ‘It really isn’t worth bringing back just one hundred—let me squander that as well!’ That is how the real Dmitri Karamazov, the one we know, would have acted! The legend of the pouch, however, contradicts reality more completely than anything one could imagine. We can suppose anything but that. Yet we shall return to it.”

After setting out in order everything the examination had established about the property disputes and family relations between father and son, and concluding again and again that the available information offered not the slightest possibility of determining, in this matter of the division of the inheritance, who had cheated whom or who had inflated his claims against whom, Ippolit Kirillovich, speaking of the three thousand rubles that had lodged in Mitya’s mind as a fixed idea, referred to the medical testimony.
