# Book XII: A Judicial Error

## XIV. The Peasants Stood Up for Themselves

<!-- B12-C14-P001 -->
So Fetyukovich ended, and the audience’s delight this time burst out as irresistibly as a storm. It was unthinkable now to restrain it: women were weeping, many men were weeping too, even two dignitaries shed tears. The presiding judge submitted and even delayed ringing his bell: “To assail such enthusiasm would be to assail something sacred,” as our ladies exclaimed afterward. The orator himself was sincerely moved. And it was at just such a moment that our Ippolit Kirillovich rose once more to “exchange objections.” He was greeted with hatred: “What? What is this? He dares to object still?” the ladies murmured. But even if the ladies of the entire world had murmured, with the prosecutor’s own wife, Ippolit Kirillovich’s spouse, at their head, he could not have been restrained at that moment. He was pale, trembling with agitation; his first words, his first sentences, were even incomprehensible; he gasped, pronounced words badly, lost his way. Soon, however, he recovered. But I shall quote only a few sentences from this second speech.

<!-- B12-C14-P002 -->
“…We are reproached with having invented novels. But what has the defense offered except novel upon novel? The only thing missing was verse. Fyodor Pavlovich, awaiting his mistress, tears open the envelope and throws it onto the floor. We are even told what he said on this remarkable occasion. Is that not a poem? And where is the proof that he took out the money, who heard what he said? The feeble-minded idiot Smerdyakov, transformed into some Byronic hero avenging his illegitimate birth upon society—is that not a poem in the Byronic manner? And the son who forces his way in to his father, kills him, but at the same time does not kill him—that is not even a novel or a poem, but a sphinx posing riddles which it certainly cannot solve itself. If he killed him, then he killed him; but how can it be that if he killed him, he did not kill him—who can understand that? Then we are told that our rostrum is a rostrum of truth and sound ideas, and from this rostrum of ‘sound ideas’ comes an axiom, solemnly sworn, that to call the killing of a father parricide is nothing but prejudice! But if parricide is a prejudice, and if every child is to interrogate his father: ‘Father, why should I love you?’—what will become of us, what will become of the foundations of society, where will the family go? Parricide, you see, is merely a Moscow merchant’s wife’s ‘brimstone.’ The most precious, most sacred principles governing the purpose and future of Russian justice are presented perversely and frivolously, solely to achieve a goal, to secure the justification of what cannot be justified. ‘Oh, overwhelm him with mercy!’ the defense counsel exclaims, and the criminal wants nothing better; tomorrow everyone will see how overwhelmed he is! Indeed, is the defense counsel not too modest in demanding only the defendant’s acquittal? Why not demand the establishment of a scholarship in the parricide’s name, to immortalize his deed for posterity and the younger generation? The Gospel and religion are corrected: all that, we are told, is mysticism, while only we have true Christianity, tested now by the analysis of reason and sound ideas. And so a false image of Christ is raised before us! ‘With what measure ye mete, it shall be measured to you again,’ the defense counsel exclaims, and in the same instant deduces that Christ commanded us to measure by the measure with which others measure to us—and this from the rostrum of truth and sound ideas! We glance into the Gospel only on the eve of our speeches, to display our acquaintance with what is, after all, a rather original work, which may come in handy and serve to produce a certain effect as needed, everything according to need! But Christ commands precisely that we should not do this, that we should beware of doing this, because the wicked world does this, whereas we must forgive and turn our cheek, not repay in the same measure as those who wrong us. That is what our God taught us, not that forbidding children to kill their fathers is a prejudice. And let us not correct, from the rostrum of truth and sound ideas, the Gospel of our God, whom the defense counsel deigns to call merely a ‘crucified lover of mankind,’ in opposition to all Orthodox Russia, which cries out to him: ‘For thou art our God!…’”

<!-- B12-C14-P003 -->
Here the presiding judge intervened and checked the carried-away speaker, asking him not to exaggerate, to remain within proper bounds, and so on and so forth, as presiding judges usually say on such occasions. The courtroom, too, was restless. People stirred, even cried out indignantly. Fetyukovich did not even argue back; he merely came forward, put his hand to his heart, and in an injured voice spoke a few words full of dignity. He touched only lightly and mockingly once more upon “novels” and “psychology,” and at one point slipped in the remark: “Jupiter, you are angry, therefore you are wrong,” which drew approving chuckles from a great many people, since by now Ippolit Kirillovich looked nothing like Jupiter. Then, regarding the accusation that he was permitting the younger generation to kill their fathers, Fetyukovich observed with profound dignity that he would not even answer it. As for the “false image of Christ,” and his not having deigned to call Christ God but merely a “crucified lover of mankind,” which was “contrary to Orthodoxy and could not be said from a rostrum of truth and sound ideas,” Fetyukovich hinted at an “insinuation,” and at the fact that when preparing to come here he had at least expected this rostrum to be protected from accusations “dangerous to my person as a citizen and loyal subject…” But at these words the presiding judge checked him too, and Fetyukovich bowed and concluded his reply to a general murmur of approval throughout the courtroom. Ippolit Kirillovich, in our ladies’ opinion, had been “crushed forever.”

<!-- B12-C14-P004 -->
Then the defendant himself was given the floor. Mitya stood up, but said little. He was terribly exhausted, physically and spiritually. The air of independence and strength with which he had entered the courtroom that morning had almost vanished. He seemed to have gone through something that day that would stay with him for life, something that had taught him and brought him to understand a matter of great importance he had not understood before. His voice had grown weak; he no longer shouted as he had earlier. Something new could be heard in his words, something humbled, defeated, and bowed down.

<!-- B12-C14-P005 -->
“What can I say, gentlemen of the jury! My judgment has come; I sense God’s right hand upon me. An end to a dissolute man! But as though confessing to God, I tell you too: ‘Of my father’s blood—no, I am not guilty!’ For the last time I repeat: ‘I did not kill him.’ I was dissolute, but I loved goodness. Every moment I strove to reform, yet I lived like a wild beast. My thanks to the prosecutor; he told me much about myself that I did not know, but it is not true that I killed my father, the prosecutor is mistaken! My thanks to the defense counsel too; I wept as I listened to him, but it is not true that I killed my father, and he should not even have supposed it! And do not believe the doctors, I am in my right mind, only my soul is heavy. If you spare me, if you let me go, I shall pray for you. I shall become better, I give you my word, I give it before God. And if you condemn me, I shall break my sword over my own head, and having broken it, kiss the pieces![^b12-c14-n001] But spare me, do not deprive me of my God; I know myself: I shall rebel! My soul is heavy, gentlemen… spare me!”

<!-- B12-C14-P006 -->
He almost fell back into his seat; his voice failed, and he could barely get out the last sentence. Then the court proceeded to formulate the questions and began asking the parties for their submissions. But I shall not describe the details. At last the jurors rose to retire for deliberation. The presiding judge was very tired, and so his final instructions to them were very weak: “Be impartial, do not be influenced by the eloquent words of the defense, but nevertheless weigh the matter, remember that a great duty rests upon you,” and so on and so forth. The jurors withdrew, and there was a recess. People could stand up, walk about, exchange the impressions they had accumulated, get something to eat at the buffet. It was very late, already around one in the morning, but no one went home. Everyone was so tense and worked up that there was no thought of rest. Everyone waited with a sinking heart—though not everyone’s heart was sinking. The ladies were merely hysterically impatient, but their hearts were at ease: “Acquittal is inevitable.” They were all preparing for the spectacular moment of general enthusiasm. I admit that among the men in the courtroom, too, a great many were convinced that acquittal was inevitable. Some rejoiced, others frowned, and some simply looked crestfallen: they did not want an acquittal! Fetyukovich himself was firmly confident of success. People surrounded him, offered congratulations, sought his favor.

<!-- B12-C14-P007 -->
“There are,” he said in one group, as was reported afterward, “there are invisible threads linking the defense counsel to the jurors. They form and make themselves felt even during the speech. I felt them; they exist. The case is ours, rest assured.”

<!-- B12-C14-P008 -->
“But what will our peasants say now, I wonder?” said a frowning, fat, pockmarked gentleman, a landowner from just outside town, approaching a group of gentlemen in conversation.

<!-- B12-C14-P009 -->
“They’re not all peasants, you know. There are four officials.”

<!-- B12-C14-P010 -->
“Yes, the officials,” said a member of the zemstvo board, coming up.

<!-- B12-C14-P011 -->
“Do you know Nazaryev, Prokhor Ivanovich, that merchant with the medal, the juror?”

<!-- B12-C14-P012 -->
“What about him?”

<!-- B12-C14-P013 -->
“Sharp as they come.”

<!-- B12-C14-P014 -->
“But he never says anything.”

<!-- B12-C14-P015 -->
“He may say nothing, but that’s all the better. The Petersburg man teach him? He could teach all Petersburg. Twelve children, just think!”

<!-- B12-C14-P016 -->
“But really, can they possibly fail to acquit him?” one of our young officials was crying in another group.

<!-- B12-C14-P017 -->
“They’ll certainly acquit him,” came a decisive voice.

<!-- B12-C14-P018 -->
“It would be shameful, disgraceful not to acquit him!” the official exclaimed. “Suppose he killed him, but there are fathers and fathers! And besides, he was in such a frenzy… He really might only have swung the pestle and the man fell. The only bad thing is dragging the servant into it. That’s simply a ridiculous episode. In the defense counsel’s place I’d have said outright: he killed him, but he’s not guilty, and to hell with you!”

<!-- B12-C14-P019 -->
“That is what he did, only he didn’t say ‘to hell with you.’”

<!-- B12-C14-P020 -->
“No, Mikhail Semyonych, he very nearly did,” a third little voice put in.

<!-- B12-C14-P021 -->
“Really, gentlemen, didn’t they acquit that actress here during Lent, the one who cut her lover’s lawful wife’s throat?”

<!-- B12-C14-P022 -->
“But she didn’t finish cutting it.”

<!-- B12-C14-P023 -->
“All the same, all the same, she started!”

<!-- B12-C14-P024 -->
“And what about his words on children? Magnificent!”

<!-- B12-C14-P025 -->
“Magnificent.”

<!-- B12-C14-P026 -->
“Well, and the mysticism, what about the mysticism, eh?”

<!-- B12-C14-P027 -->
“Oh, enough about mysticism,” someone else cried. “Consider Ippolit, what his fate will be from this day on! Tomorrow his wife will scratch his eyes out over Mitenka.”

<!-- B12-C14-P028 -->
“Is she here?”

<!-- B12-C14-P029 -->
“Here? If she were here, she’d scratch them out here. She’s at home with toothache. Heh-heh-heh!”

<!-- B12-C14-P030 -->
“Heh-heh-heh!”

<!-- B12-C14-P031 -->
In a third group:

<!-- B12-C14-P032 -->
“They may actually acquit Mitenka, you know.”

<!-- B12-C14-P033 -->
“And then tomorrow he’ll smash up the whole Capital City tavern, go on drinking for ten days.”

<!-- B12-C14-P034 -->
“Oh, the devil!”

<!-- B12-C14-P035 -->
“The devil indeed, the devil had a hand in this; where else would he be but here?”

<!-- B12-C14-P036 -->
“Gentlemen, eloquence, granted. But surely we can’t go breaking our fathers’ heads with steelyards. Where will it end otherwise?”

<!-- B12-C14-P037 -->
“The chariot, the chariot, remember?”

<!-- B12-C14-P038 -->
“Yes, he turned a cart into a chariot.”

<!-- B12-C14-P039 -->
“And tomorrow a chariot into a cart, ‘as needed, everything as needed.’”

<!-- B12-C14-P040 -->
“A clever breed has sprung up. Is there any truth in our Russia, gentlemen, or none at all?”

<!-- B12-C14-P041 -->
But the bell rang. The jurors had deliberated for exactly an hour, neither more nor less. A profound silence fell as soon as the public was seated again. I remember the jurors entering the courtroom. At last! I shall not give the questions point by point; indeed, I have forgotten them. I remember only the answer to the presiding judge’s first and principal question, that is: “Did he commit premeditated murder for the purpose of robbery?” I do not remember the wording. Everything froze. The foreman of the jury, the youngest of the officials, proclaimed loudly and clearly into the courtroom’s deathly silence:

<!-- B12-C14-P042 -->
“Yes, guilty!”

<!-- B12-C14-P043 -->
And then it was the same on every count: guilty and guilty, without the slightest recommendation of leniency! No one had expected that; almost everyone had been sure of leniency, at least. The courtroom’s deathly silence remained unbroken, as though everyone had literally turned to stone—those who longed for conviction and those who longed for acquittal alike. But that was only in the first moments. Then terrible chaos broke out. A great many of the men proved to be very pleased. Some even rubbed their hands, making no attempt to conceal their joy. The dissatisfied seemed crushed; they shrugged, whispered among themselves, but still seemed unable to take it in. But my God, what happened to our ladies! I thought they would riot. At first they seemed unable to believe their ears. Then suddenly cries rang out across the courtroom: “What is this? What on earth is this?” They sprang from their seats. They must have imagined that the whole thing could immediately be changed and done over again. At that moment Mitya suddenly rose and shouted in a heartrending cry, stretching out his hands before him:

<!-- B12-C14-P044 -->
“I swear by God and his Last Judgment, I am not guilty of my father’s blood! Katya, I forgive you! Brothers, friends, spare the other woman!”

<!-- B12-C14-P045 -->
He did not finish, but burst into sobs that filled the courtroom, loud and terrible, in a voice that seemed not his own but new and unexpected, God only knows where it had suddenly come from. From the very back corner of the gallery above came a piercing woman’s cry: it was Grushenka. Earlier she had pleaded with someone, and they had let her back into the courtroom before the closing arguments began. Mitya was led away. Pronouncement of the sentence was postponed until the following day. The whole courtroom rose in confusion, but I no longer waited or listened. I remember only a few exclamations on the steps as I was leaving.

<!-- B12-C14-P046 -->
“He’ll get a taste of the mines for twenty years.”

<!-- B12-C14-P047 -->
“No less.”

<!-- B12-C14-P048 -->
“Yes, sir, our peasants stood up for themselves.”

<!-- B12-C14-P049 -->
“And finished off our Mitenka!”

End of the fourth and final part
