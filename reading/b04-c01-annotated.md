# Book IV: Strains
## I. Father Ferapont

<!-- B04-C01-P001 -->
Alyosha was woken early in the morning, before daybreak. The elder had awoken feeling very weak, though he wished to move from his bed to his armchair. His mind was perfectly clear; his face, though very tired, was serene, almost joyful, and his eyes were cheerful, welcoming, inviting. “I may not live through this day that has begun,” he told Alyosha. Then he asked to make his confession and receive communion at once. Father Paisius had always been his confessor. When both sacraments had been administered, the anointing began. The priest-monks gathered, and little by little the cell filled with monks from the hermitage. Meanwhile, day had broken. Monks began arriving from the monastery as well. When the service was over, the elder wished to take leave of them all, and kissed each one. Since the cell was so small, those who had come first went out to make room for others. Alyosha stood beside the elder, who had moved back to his armchair. He spoke and taught as much as he could; his voice, though weak, was still fairly steady. “I have been teaching you for so many years, which means I have been speaking aloud for so many years, that I seem to have acquired the habit of speaking, and of teaching you as I speak—so much so that keeping silent would almost be harder for me than speaking, dear fathers and brothers, even now, weak as I am,” he joked, looking tenderly at those crowded around him. Alyosha later remembered some of what he said then. But although he spoke distinctly, and in a fairly steady voice, his words were rather disconnected. He spoke of many things; it seemed he wanted to say everything, to say it all once more before the moment of death, everything he had left unsaid in his life, and not only to teach them, but as though he longed to share his joy and rapture with everyone and everything, to pour out his heart once more while he was alive...

<!-- B04-C01-P002 -->
“Love one another, fathers,” the elder taught them, as far as Alyosha later remembered. “Love God's people. We are no holier than those in the world because we have come here and shut ourselves within these walls. On the contrary, everyone who comes here has, by the very act of coming, acknowledged that he is worse than everyone in the world, worse than everyone and everything on earth... And the longer a monk then lives within his walls, the more deeply he must feel and recognize this. Otherwise there was no reason for him to come here at all. Only when he recognizes that he is not merely worse than everyone in the world, but guilty before all people, for everyone and everything, for all human sins, both the world's and each person's—only then will the purpose of our life together be fulfilled. For know, my dear ones, that each and every one of us is undoubtedly guilty for everyone and everything on earth, not merely through the world's common guilt, but each of us personally, for all people and for every person on this earth. This awareness is the crown of the monk's path, and of the path of every person on earth. For monks are not a different sort of human being; they are only what all people on earth ought to be. Only then would our hearts melt into a boundless love, embracing the universe, never satisfied. Only then will each of you be able to win the whole world through love and wash away the world's sins with your tears... Let each of you keep watch over his own heart; let each of you confess to himself unceasingly. Do not be afraid of your sin, even when you have recognized it, so long as there is repentance; but do not make bargains with God. Again I say: do not be proud. Do not be proud before the lowly, nor before the great. Do not hate those who reject you, disgrace you, abuse you, and slander you. Do not hate atheists, teachers of falsehood, materialists—even the wicked among them, and not only the good, for many of them are good, especially in our time. Remember them in your prayers in this way: Lord, save all those who have no one to pray for them; save also those who do not wish to pray to you. And add at once: I do not ask this out of pride, Lord, for I myself am more vile than everyone and everything... Love God's people. Do not let strangers carry off the flock, for if you fall asleep in your idleness and disdainful pride, and above all in your greed, they will come from every quarter and take your flock away. Explain the Gospel to the people unceasingly... Do not seek unjust gain... Do not love silver and gold; do not keep them... Have faith, and hold fast to the banner. Raise it high...”

<!-- B04-C01-P003 -->
The elder's speech was, however, more broken than it appears here, or in Alyosha's later account. At times he stopped speaking altogether, as though gathering his strength; he struggled for breath, yet seemed to be in a state of rapture. They listened with deep feeling, though many were astonished by his words and found them obscure... Later, they remembered all these words. When Alyosha happened to leave the cell for a moment, he was struck by the general excitement and expectation among the monks crowding inside and around it. Some waited almost anxiously, others solemnly. They all expected something immediate and great to happen as soon as the elder fell asleep in death. From one point of view, this expectation was almost frivolous, yet even the sternest elders were subject to it. The priest-monk and elder Paisius wore the sternest expression of all. Alyosha had left the cell only because Rakitin, who had arrived from town with a strange letter for him from Madame Khokhlakov, had mysteriously sent a monk to summon him. She had some curious news for Alyosha, news that came at a most opportune moment. The day before, among the devout women of the common people who had come to bow before the elder and receive his blessing, there had been an elderly woman from town, Prokhorovna, the widow of a noncommissioned officer. She had asked the elder whether she might have prayers said in church for the repose of her dear son Vasenka, as though he were dead, since his work had taken him far into Siberia, to Irkutsk, and she had heard nothing from him for a year. The elder had answered sternly, forbidding her to do so and calling such prayers akin to witchcraft. But then, forgiving her because she had not known better, he had added a word of comfort, “as though reading from the book of the future,” as Madame Khokhlakov put it in her letter: “Her son Vasya was undoubtedly alive, and would soon either come to her himself or send her a letter, and she should go home and wait for this. And what do you think?” Madame Khokhlakov went on in delight. “The prophecy has been fulfilled to the letter, and more than fulfilled.” The old woman had barely returned home when she was handed a letter from Siberia that was already waiting for her. And that was not all: in this letter, written on the road from Yekaterinburg, Vasya told his mother that he was coming back to Russia himself, accompanying an official, and that in about three weeks from her receipt of the letter “he hoped to embrace his mother.” Madame Khokhlakov urgently and fervently begged Alyosha to report this new “miracle of prophecy” to the abbot and all the brothers at once. “Everyone, everyone must know about it!” she exclaimed at the end of her letter. She had written hurriedly, in haste, and her excitement could be felt in every line. But there was nothing left for Alyosha to tell the brothers: they all knew everything already. When Rakitin sent a monk to fetch him, he had also instructed him “most respectfully to inform His Very Reverence Father Paisius that he, Rakitin, had a matter to discuss with him, but one of such importance that he dared not delay communicating it for a single minute, and that he humbly begged forgiveness for his presumption.” Since the monk had delivered Rakitin's request to Father Paisius before reaching Alyosha, all that remained for Alyosha, when he arrived and read the letter, was to pass it straight to Father Paisius as documentary evidence. And even this stern and skeptical man, though he frowned as he read the news of the “miracle,” could not entirely contain something he felt within. His eyes flashed, and suddenly a solemn, deeply moved smile appeared on his lips.

<!-- B04-C01-P004 -->
“What more may we see?” The words seemed to escape him.

<!-- B04-C01-P005 -->
“What more we may yet see, what more we may yet see!” the monks around him repeated. But Father Paisius frowned again and asked them all, at least for the present, not to speak of it to anyone, “until it is more fully confirmed, for worldly people are often frivolous, and this event might also have come about naturally,” he added cautiously, as though to clear his conscience, though he scarcely believed his own qualification, as his listeners saw very well. Within the hour, of course, the “miracle” was known throughout the monastery, and even to many of the laypeople who had come for the liturgy. The person most struck by it seemed to be the little visiting monk “from Saint Sylvester's,” from a small monastery at Obdorsk in the far North, who had arrived the day before. Yesterday, standing beside Madame Khokhlakov, he had bowed before the elder and, pointing to the lady's “healed” daughter, asked him with deep feeling, “How do you dare to do such things?”

<!-- B04-C01-P006 -->
The fact was that by now he was somewhat bewildered and hardly knew what to believe. Only the previous evening he had visited Father Ferapont in his separate cell beyond the monastery's apiary, and the encounter had shaken him, leaving an extraordinary and terrifying impression. This elder, Father Ferapont, was the same aged monk, the great practitioner of fasting and silence, whom we have already mentioned as an opponent of the elder Zosima, and above all of eldership, which he regarded as a harmful and frivolous innovation. He was an extremely dangerous opponent, despite the fact that, as a practitioner of silence, he hardly spoke a word to anyone. His chief danger lay in the many brothers who sympathized with him completely, and in the great number of visiting laypeople who revered him as a righteous man and a great ascetic, although they undoubtedly considered him a holy fool. But it was precisely his holy foolishness that captivated them. This Father Ferapont never visited the elder Zosima. Though he lived in the hermitage, they did not bother him much about its rules, again because he behaved outright as a holy fool. He was about seventy-five, if not older, and lived beyond the hermitage apiary, in a corner by the wall, in an old, almost collapsing wooden cell built there in ancient times, back in the previous century, for another great practitioner of fasting and silence, Father Jonah, who had lived to be a hundred and five. Many highly curious stories about his ascetic feats were still told in the monastery and the surrounding area. Some seven years earlier, Father Ferapont had finally managed to get himself installed in this same secluded little cell. It was really only a hut, but it looked very much like a chapel, since it contained an extraordinary number of donated icons, with donated lamps burning perpetually before them; Father Ferapont had been assigned, as it were, to tend and light these lamps. People said—and it was true—that he ate only two pounds of bread every three days, no more. The beekeeper who lived there at the apiary brought it to him every third day, but even to this beekeeper, who attended to him, Father Ferapont rarely spoke. These four pounds of bread, together with the small Sunday offering loaf that the abbot faithfully sent the blessed man every Sunday after the late liturgy, made up his entire weekly sustenance. The water in his mug was changed daily. He seldom appeared at the liturgy. Worshippers who came to see him sometimes found him spending the whole day in prayer, without rising from his knees or looking around. If he did speak to them, he was brief, abrupt, strange, and almost always rude. There were, however, very rare occasions when he too grew talkative with his visitors; usually he uttered only some strange remark that left the visitor thoroughly puzzled, and then, no matter how much he was begged, said nothing to explain it. He was not ordained, but only an ordinary monk. A very strange rumor circulated, though only among the most ignorant people, that Father Ferapont communicated with heavenly spirits and spoke only with them, which was why he kept silent with human beings. Following the directions of the beekeeper, himself a very taciturn and gloomy monk, the little monk from Obdorsk made his way through the apiary to the corner where Father Ferapont's cell stood. “He may speak to you, since you're a stranger, or you may get nothing out of him,” the beekeeper warned him. The monk approached in the greatest fear, as he later related himself. It was already quite late. On this occasion Father Ferapont was sitting on a low bench beside his cell door. A huge old elm rustled softly overhead. The evening chill was setting in. The monk from Obdorsk fell face down before the blessed man and asked for his blessing.

<!-- B04-C01-P007 -->
“Do you want me to fall on my face before you too, monk?” said Father Ferapont. “Arise!”

<!-- B04-C01-P008 -->
The monk got up.

<!-- B04-C01-P009 -->
“In blessing, be thou blessed. Sit beside me. Where've you blown in from?”

<!-- B04-C01-P010 -->
What astonished the poor monk most was that Father Ferapont, despite his undeniably severe fasting and his advanced age, still looked a strong old man: tall, standing straight and unbent, his face fresh and, though thin, healthy. He had undoubtedly retained considerable strength as well. His build was athletic. Despite his great age, he was not even entirely gray; the hair on his head and his beard, once jet-black, was still very thick. His eyes were gray, large, and shining, but so remarkably protruding that it was startling. He spoke with strongly rounded o's. He wore a long, reddish-brown peasant coat of coarse material formerly known as convict cloth, belted with a thick rope. His neck and chest were bare. Beneath the coat showed a shirt of the coarsest linen, almost black, which he wore for months without taking it off. They said he wore thirty pounds of penitential chains under his coat. On his bare feet were old shoes that were almost falling apart.

<!-- B04-C01-P011 -->
“From the small monastery at Obdorsk, from Saint Selivester's,” the visiting monk answered humbly, watching the hermit with his quick, curious little eyes, which were somewhat frightened too.

<!-- B04-C01-P012 -->
“Been to your Selivester's. Lived there. Is Selivester well?”

<!-- B04-C01-P013 -->
The monk faltered.

<!-- B04-C01-P014 -->
“Senseless creatures, you are! How do you keep the fast?”

<!-- B04-C01-P015 -->
“Our rule for meals follows the ancient rule of the hermitages: during the Forty Days, on Monday, Wednesday, and Friday no meal is set out. On Tuesday and Thursday the brothers have white bread, a boiled drink with honey, cloudberries or salted cabbage, and a mash of oat flour. On Saturday, white cabbage soup, pea noodles, and porridge with seed milk, all with oil. On Sunday, dried fish with the cabbage soup, and porridge. During Holy Week, from Monday until Saturday evening, for six days, we eat only bread and water and uncooked greens, and these in moderation; if possible, we do not take food every day, but as was prescribed for the first week. On Holy and Great Friday we eat nothing; likewise on Great Saturday we fast until the third hour, and then take a little bread and water and drink one cup of wine each. On Holy and Great Thursday we eat cooked food without oil, and drink wine, with dry food for the rest. For the Council at Laodicea speaks thus of Great Thursday: ‘It is not fitting to relax the fast on the Thursday of the last week of the Forty Days, and so dishonor all the Forty Days.’ That is our practice. But what is this beside yours, great father?” the monk added, growing bolder. “For all year round, even at Holy Easter, you live on bread and water alone, and the bread that lasts us two days lasts you a whole week. Truly, such great abstinence is a marvel.”

<!-- B04-C01-P016 -->
“And milk mushrooms?” Father Ferapont suddenly asked, pronouncing the initial g of *gruzdi*, mushrooms, with a breathy sound, almost like kh.

<!-- B04-C01-P017 -->
“Milk mushrooms?” the astonished monk repeated.

<!-- B04-C01-P018 -->
“Just so. I could leave their bread behind, with no need of it at all, go into the woods even, and live there on mushrooms or berries; but here they won't leave their bread behind, which means they're bound to the devil. Nowadays the filthy ones say there's no need to fast so much. Arrogant and filthy: that's their reasoning.”

<!-- B04-C01-P019 -->
“Oh, it's true,” sighed the monk.

<!-- B04-C01-P020 -->
“And did you see their devils?” Father Ferapont asked.

<!-- B04-C01-P021 -->
“Whose devils?” the monk asked timidly.

<!-- B04-C01-P022 -->
“I went up to the abbot's last year at Holy Pentecost. Haven't been since. I saw one sitting on a fellow's breast, hiding under his cassock, with just his little horns showing; another peeping out of a fellow's pocket, his eyes darting—afraid of me, he was; another had settled in a fellow's bowels, right in his filthy belly, and on one of them there was one hanging round his neck, clinging to him: he carries him about and can't see him.”

<!-- B04-C01-P023 -->
“You... can see them?” the monk asked.

<!-- B04-C01-P024 -->
“I tell you I can see them. See right through. As I was leaving the abbot's, I looked—and there was one hiding from me behind the door, a full-grown one, an arshin and a half tall or more, with a great thick tail, brown and long. And the tip of his tail happened to slip into the crack of the door. Well, I wasn't a fool: I slammed the door and caught his tail. How he squealed, how he thrashed! But I made the sign of the cross over him, three times, and crossed him to death. Died then and there, like a squashed spider. By now he must have rotted in the corner, stinking, but they can't see him, can't smell him. Haven't been there for a year. I'm revealing it only to you, as a foreigner.”

<!-- B04-C01-P025 -->
“Your words are terrible! And tell me, great and blessed father,” the monk ventured, growing bolder and bolder, “is it true—your great fame has reached even distant lands—that you are in constant communion with the Holy Spirit?”

<!-- B04-C01-P026 -->
“Flies down. Comes sometimes.”

<!-- B04-C01-P027 -->
“How does he fly down? In what form?”

<!-- B04-C01-P028 -->
“As a bird.”

<!-- B04-C01-P029 -->
“The Holy Spirit in the form of a dove?”

<!-- B04-C01-P030 -->
“That's the Holy Spirit; this is Holyspirit. Holyspirit's different. He can come down as other birds too: sometimes a swallow, sometimes a goldfinch, sometimes even a tit.”

<!-- B04-C01-P031 -->
“How do you tell him from a tit?”

<!-- B04-C01-P032 -->
“He speaks.”

<!-- B04-C01-P033 -->
“How does he speak? In what language?”

<!-- B04-C01-P034 -->
“Human.”

<!-- B04-C01-P035 -->
“And what does he say to you?”

<!-- B04-C01-P036 -->
“Today, for instance, he announced that a fool would visit and ask improper questions. You want to know too much, monk.”

<!-- B04-C01-P037 -->
“Your words are dreadful, most blessed and holy father,” the monk said, shaking his head. Yet a trace of disbelief appeared in his frightened little eyes.

<!-- B04-C01-P038 -->
“Do you see this tree?” Father Ferapont asked after a silence.

<!-- B04-C01-P039 -->
“I do, most blessed father.”

<!-- B04-C01-P040 -->
“To you it's an elm. To me it's another sight altogether.”

<!-- B04-C01-P041 -->
“What sight?” the monk asked, after waiting in vain for him to go on.

<!-- B04-C01-P042 -->
“It happens at night. See those two branches? At night, behold, Christ stretches out his arms to me and reaches for me with those hands. I see it plainly and tremble. Terrible, oh, terrible!”

<!-- B04-C01-P043 -->
“But what is terrible about it, if it is Christ himself?”

<!-- B04-C01-P044 -->
“He might seize me and carry me up.”

“Alive?”

<!-- B04-C01-P046 -->
“In the spirit and glory of Elijah—haven't you heard? He'll embrace me and carry me away...”

<!-- B04-C01-P047 -->
After this conversation, the monk from Obdorsk returned in considerable bewilderment to the cell assigned to him, which he was sharing with one of the brothers; nevertheless, his heart undoubtedly inclined more toward Father Ferapont than toward Father Zosima. Above all else, the monk from Obdorsk believed in fasting, and it was no wonder that so great a faster as Father Ferapont should “behold wondrous things.” His words were admittedly somewhat absurd, but only the Lord knew what was contained in those words, and all the fools for Christ's sake said and did things stranger still. As for the devil's pinched tail, he was wholeheartedly and gladly prepared to believe in it, not only figuratively but literally. Besides, even before coming to the monastery he had been strongly prejudiced against eldership, which he had known until then only by hearsay and, following many others, regarded as an outright harmful innovation. After spending a day at the monastery, he had already noticed the secret grumbling of some frivolous brothers who disapproved of eldership. Moreover, he was by nature a busy, darting sort of monk, with an immense curiosity about everything. That was why the great news of the elder Zosima's latest “miracle” left him so extremely bewildered. Alyosha later remembered how, among the monks crowding around the elder and his cell, the figure of the curious visitor from Obdorsk had kept flitting past him, darting from group to group, listening to everything and questioning everyone. But at the time he paid little attention to him; only afterward did it all come back... Besides, he had other things to think of: the elder Zosima, who had grown tired again and lain down in bed, suddenly remembered him, just as his eyes were closing, and asked for him. Alyosha came running at once. Only Father Paisius, the priest-monk Father Joseph, and Porfiry the novice were with the elder then. Opening his tired eyes and looking intently at Alyosha, the elder suddenly asked:

<!-- B04-C01-P048 -->
“Are your family waiting for you, my son?”

<!-- B04-C01-P049 -->
Alyosha hesitated.

<!-- B04-C01-P050 -->
“Don't they need you? Did you promise anyone yesterday that you would come today?”

<!-- B04-C01-P051 -->
“I promised... my father... my brothers... others too...”

<!-- B04-C01-P052 -->
“There, you see. You must go. Do not grieve. Know that I will not die without speaking my last word on earth in your presence. I will speak that word to you, my son; I will leave it to you. To you, my dear son, because you love me. But for now, go to those you have promised.”

<!-- B04-C01-P053 -->
Alyosha obeyed at once, though it was painful for him to leave. But the promise that he would hear the elder's last word on earth, and above all that it would be left, as it were, to him, Alyosha, shook his soul with rapture. He hurried, so that he could finish everything in town and return as soon as possible. Just then Father Paisius too gave him a word of counsel for his journey, which made a very powerful and unexpected impression on him. This happened after they had both left the elder's cell.

<!-- B04-C01-P054 -->
“Remember always, young man,” Father Paisius began, directly and without any preamble, “that secular learning, joining forces to become a great power, has taken apart—especially in the past century—everything heavenly bequeathed to us in the holy books, and after their merciless analysis the scholars of this world have been left with absolutely nothing of all that was once sacred. But they examined the parts and overlooked the whole, with a blindness that is astonishing. Yet the whole stands unshaken before their very eyes, as it did before, and the gates of hell shall not prevail against it. Has it not lived for nineteen centuries? Does it not live even now in the stirrings of individual souls and in the movements of the masses? It lives, unshaken as before, even in the souls of those very atheists who have demolished everything! For those who have renounced Christianity and rebel against it are themselves, in their essence, still shaped in that same image of Christ, and so they have remained, because until now neither their wisdom nor the ardor of their hearts has been able to create any higher image of humanity and human dignity than the one Christ revealed long ago. Whenever they have tried, they have produced only deformities. Remember this especially, young man, for your departing elder is sending you into the world. Perhaps, as you remember this great day, you will also remember my words, offered from my heart to guide you on your way, for you are young, and the temptations in the world are grievous, beyond your strength to bear. Now go, orphan.”

<!-- B04-C01-P055 -->
With this, Father Paisius blessed him. As he left the monastery, thinking over all these unexpected words, Alyosha suddenly understood that in this monk, until now so strict and stern with him, he was finding a new and unexpected friend, a new guide who loved him deeply—as though the dying elder Zosima had bequeathed him to Paisius. “Perhaps that really is what happened between them,” Alyosha suddenly thought. The unexpected, learned argument he had just heard—precisely this argument, and no other—was simply a sign of the warmth of Father Paisius's heart: already he was hurrying to arm the young mind as quickly as possible for its struggle with temptation, and to shelter the young soul entrusted to him behind the strongest defenses he could imagine.
