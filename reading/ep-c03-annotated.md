# Epilogue

## III. Ilyushechka’s Funeral. The Speech at the Stone

<!-- EP-C03-P001 -->
He was indeed late. They had been waiting for him, and had already decided to carry the pretty little coffin, covered with flowers, to the church without him. It was the coffin of Ilyushechka, the poor boy. He had died two days after Mitya’s sentence. Alyosha was met at the gate by the cries of the boys, Ilyusha’s friends. They had all been waiting impatiently for him and were glad he had finally come. About twelve of them had gathered, all with their satchels and schoolbags over their shoulders. “Papa will cry; stay with Papa,” Ilyusha had charged them as he was dying, and the boys had remembered. At their head was Kolya Krasotkin.

<!-- EP-C03-P002 -->
“I’m so glad you’ve come, Karamazov!” he exclaimed, holding out his hand to Alyosha. “It’s terrible in there. Truly, it’s painful to watch. Snegiryov isn’t drunk—we know for certain he hasn’t had anything to drink today—but he seems drunk... I’m always firm, but this is terrible. Karamazov, if I won’t be keeping you, just one more question before you go in?”

<!-- EP-C03-P003 -->
“What is it, Kolya?” Alyosha stopped.

<!-- EP-C03-P004 -->
“Is your brother innocent or guilty? Did he kill his father, or did the servant? Whatever you say, that’s how it will be. I haven’t slept for four nights because of this idea.”

<!-- EP-C03-P005 -->
“The servant killed him, and my brother is innocent,” Alyosha replied.

<!-- EP-C03-P006 -->
“That’s what I say too!” the boy Smurov suddenly shouted.

<!-- EP-C03-P007 -->
“Then he’ll perish as an innocent sacrifice for the truth!” Kolya exclaimed. “Though he’s been ruined, he’s happy! I could almost envy him!”

<!-- EP-C03-P008 -->
“What are you saying? How could you, and why?” Alyosha exclaimed in surprise.

<!-- EP-C03-P009 -->
“Oh, if only I too could sacrifice myself for the truth someday!” Kolya said enthusiastically.

<!-- EP-C03-P010 -->
“But not in a case like this, not with such disgrace, such horror!” said Alyosha.

<!-- EP-C03-P011 -->
“Of course... I’d like to die for all humanity, and as for disgrace, it doesn’t matter: let our names perish. I respect your brother!”

<!-- EP-C03-P012 -->
“So do I!” cried a voice from the crowd, suddenly and quite unexpectedly. It was the very boy who had once declared he knew who had founded Troy, and after shouting he blushed up to his ears, red as a peony, just as he had then.

<!-- EP-C03-P013 -->
Alyosha entered the room. Ilyusha lay in a blue coffin trimmed with white ruffles, his little hands folded and his eyes closed. The features of his wasted face had scarcely changed at all, and, strangely, there was almost no smell from the body. His expression was serious and seemed thoughtful. His hands, crossed over each other, were especially beautiful, as though carved from marble. Flowers had been placed in them, and the whole coffin was already decorated inside and out with flowers sent at first light by Lise Khokhlakov. More flowers had arrived from Katerina Ivanovna, and when Alyosha opened the door the staff captain was strewing these over his dear boy, a bunch of them in his trembling hands. He scarcely glanced at Alyosha as he came in; he did not want to look at anyone, even his weeping, deranged wife, his “Mama,” who kept trying to rise on her ailing legs and get a closer look at her dead boy. The children had lifted Ninochka in her chair and moved her right up to the coffin. She sat with her head pressed against it and must have been weeping quietly too. Snegiryov’s face looked animated, but bewildered, and at the same time hardened. There was something half-crazed about his gestures and the words that escaped him. “Little father, my dear little father!” he kept exclaiming as he looked at Ilyusha. Even when Ilyusha was alive, he had been in the habit of saying to him affectionately, “Little father, my dear little father!”

<!-- EP-C03-P014 -->
“Papa, give me some flowers too; take one from his little hand, that white one, and give it to me!” the deranged “Mama” begged between sobs. Whether she had taken such a fancy to the little white rose in Ilyusha’s hands, or wanted a flower from his hands as a keepsake, she became wildly agitated, reaching out for it.

<!-- EP-C03-P015 -->
“I won’t give anyone anything, nothing at all!” Snegiryov exclaimed heartlessly. “They’re his flowers, not yours. It’s all his, none of it’s yours!”

<!-- EP-C03-P016 -->
“Papa, give Mama a flower!” Ninochka suddenly raised her tear-soaked face.

<!-- EP-C03-P017 -->
“I won’t give anything, and least of all to her! She didn’t love him. She took his little cannon away from him that time, and he gave it to her as a pre-sent!” The staff captain suddenly sobbed aloud at the memory of how Ilyusha had given up his little cannon to his mother. The poor deranged woman dissolved in quiet tears, covering her face with her hands. At last, seeing that the father would not let the coffin leave him, though it was time to carry it out, the boys suddenly crowded closely around it and began lifting it.

<!-- EP-C03-P018 -->
“I don’t want him buried in the churchyard!” Snegiryov suddenly cried. “I’ll bury him by the stone, by our little stone! That’s what Ilyusha told me. I won’t let you carry him away!”

<!-- EP-C03-P019 -->
For the past three days he had kept saying he would bury him by the stone; but Alyosha, Krasotkin, the landlady, her sister, and all the boys intervened.

<!-- EP-C03-P020 -->
“Listen to what he’s thought up—burying him by that godforsaken stone, as though he’d hanged himself!” the old landlady said sternly. “There in the churchyard, there’s a cross over the ground. They’ll pray for him there. You can hear the singing from the church, and the deacon reads so clearly and distinctly that every word will reach him every time, just as though they were reading over his little grave.”

<!-- EP-C03-P021 -->
At last the staff captain waved his hands as if to say, “Take him wherever you like!” The children lifted the coffin, but as they carried it past his mother they stopped for a moment and lowered it so that she could say goodbye to Ilyusha. Suddenly seeing that dear little face so close, when for three days she had only looked at it from some distance, she began shaking all over and jerking her gray head hysterically back and forth above the coffin.

<!-- EP-C03-P022 -->
“Mama, make the sign of the cross over him, bless him, kiss him!” Ninochka called to her. But she went on jerking her head like a mechanical doll and suddenly, silently, her face twisted with searing grief, began beating her breast with her fist. The coffin was carried on. As it passed her, Ninochka pressed her lips for the last time to the lips of her dead brother. Leaving the house, Alyosha began to ask the landlady to look after those who remained, but she did not let him finish:

<!-- EP-C03-P023 -->
“Of course I’ll stay with them; we’re Christians too.” The old woman was weeping as she spoke.

<!-- EP-C03-P024 -->
It was not far to carry him to the church, three hundred paces at most. The day had turned clear and still; there was a frost, but not a severe one. The bell was still calling people to worship. Snegiryov hurried fussily and distractedly behind the coffin in his old short overcoat, scarcely more than a summer coat, bareheaded, with his old soft, wide-brimmed hat in his hands. He seemed beset by some worry he could not resolve: now he would reach out to support the head of the coffin, only hindering the bearers, now run around to the side, searching for a place for himself there. A flower fell into the snow, and he rushed to pick it up as though heaven knew what depended on its not being lost.

<!-- EP-C03-P025 -->
“The crust, we forgot the crust!” he suddenly exclaimed in terrible alarm. But the boys at once reminded him that he had already brought the bread crust earlier and that it was in his pocket. He promptly pulled it out and, reassured, grew calm.

<!-- EP-C03-P026 -->
“Ilyushechka asked for it, Ilyushechka,” he immediately explained to Alyosha. “He was lying there at night, and I was sitting beside him, and suddenly he told me: ‘Papa, when they’ve filled in my little grave, crumble a crust of bread over it so the sparrows will come. I’ll hear that they’ve come, and I’ll be happy I’m not lying there alone.’”

<!-- EP-C03-P027 -->
“That’s very good,” said Alyosha. “We must bring some often.”

<!-- EP-C03-P028 -->
“Every day, every day!” the staff captain babbled, seeming to come quite alive.

<!-- EP-C03-P029 -->
At last they reached the church and set the coffin in its center. All the boys gathered around it and stood there decorously throughout the service. The church was old and quite poor; many of the icons had no metal coverings at all, but somehow one prays better in such churches. During the liturgy Snegiryov seemed to quiet down a little, though at times the same unthinking, bewildered anxiety still broke through: he would go up to the coffin to straighten the covering or the paper crown on the forehead; then, when a candle fell from its holder, he suddenly rushed to put it back and fussed with it for a terribly long time. After that he calmed down and stood quietly at the head of the coffin with a dully anxious, puzzled face. After the Epistle he suddenly whispered to Alyosha, who was standing beside him, that the Epistle had not been read properly, but he did not explain what he meant. During the Cherubic Hymn he began to sing along, but did not finish; sinking to his knees, he pressed his forehead to the stone church floor and remained there for quite a long time. At last the funeral service began and candles were handed out. The distraught father started fussing again, but the tender, shattering funeral singing awakened and shook his soul. He seemed suddenly to shrink into himself and began sobbing in frequent short bursts, at first stifling the sound, and finally sobbing aloud. When they began to say their farewells and close the coffin, he flung his arms around it, as though to prevent them from covering Ilyushechka, and began kissing his dead boy on the lips, again and again, hungrily, without drawing away. At last they persuaded him, and had already led him down from the step when he suddenly stretched out his hand and snatched several flowers from the little coffin. He looked at them, and seemed struck by a new idea, so that for a moment he appeared to forget the chief thing. Little by little he seemed to sink into thought and no longer resisted when they lifted the coffin and carried it to the little grave. It was nearby, in the churchyard, right beside the church, an expensive plot; Katerina Ivanovna had paid for it. After the customary rite the gravediggers lowered the coffin. With his little bunch of flowers in his hands, Snegiryov leaned so far over the open grave that the boys, frightened, caught his coat and began pulling him back. But he seemed no longer to understand clearly what was happening. When they began filling in the grave, he suddenly pointed anxiously at the falling earth and even started saying something, but no one could make it out, and he himself abruptly fell silent. Then they reminded him that he must crumble the crust, and he grew terribly agitated, pulled it out, and began pinching off pieces and scattering them over the grave. “Come along then, little birds, come along, little sparrows!” he murmured anxiously. One of the boys remarked that the flowers in his hands made it awkward to break the bread, and suggested he give them to someone to hold for a moment. But he would not; he even grew suddenly frightened for his flowers, as though someone meant to take them from him altogether. Looking at the little grave and seeming to satisfy himself that everything had been done, the crumbs scattered, he suddenly turned, quite calmly, and trudged home. Yet his steps grew quicker and more hurried; he was hastening, almost running. The boys and Alyosha kept up with him.

<!-- EP-C03-P030 -->
“Flowers for Mama, flowers for Mama! We hurt Mama,” he suddenly began exclaiming. Someone called to him to put on his hat because it was cold now, but when he heard this he flung the hat into the snow as if in anger and kept saying, “I don’t want a hat, I don’t want a hat!” Smurov picked it up and carried it after him. Every one of the boys was crying, Kolya and the boy who had discovered Troy most of all; and though Smurov, with the captain’s hat in his hands, was crying terribly too, he still managed, almost without stopping his run, to snatch up a piece of brick that showed red against the snow on the path and fling it at a flock of sparrows flying swiftly past. Of course he missed, and went on running and crying. Halfway home Snegiryov suddenly stopped, stood for about half a minute as though struck by something, then abruptly turned back toward the church and began running to the grave they had left. But the boys immediately caught up with him and clung to him from all sides. Then, as though his strength had failed, as though struck down, he fell into the snow and, thrashing about, wailing and sobbing, began to cry, “Little father, Ilyushechka, my dear little father!” Alyosha and Kolya began helping him up, pleading with him and trying to soothe him.

<!-- EP-C03-P031 -->
“Captain, enough, a brave man has a duty to bear up,” Kolya murmured.

<!-- EP-C03-P032 -->
“You’ll spoil the flowers,” Alyosha said too, “and ‘Mama’ is waiting for them. She’s sitting there crying because you wouldn’t give her any of Ilyushechka’s flowers before. Ilyusha’s little bed is still there...”

<!-- EP-C03-P033 -->
“Yes, yes, to Mama!” Snegiryov suddenly remembered again. “They’ll put his little bed away, they’ll put it away!” he added, as if frightened that they really would put it away; he jumped up and again ran home. But it was not far now, and they all arrived together. Snegiryov flung open the door and cried to his wife, with whom he had quarreled so heartlessly earlier:

<!-- EP-C03-P034 -->
“Mama, my dear, Ilyushechka’s sent you flowers—your poor ailing little feet!” he cried, holding out the little bunch of flowers, frostbitten and broken when he had just thrashed about in the snow. But at that very moment he saw Ilyusha’s little boots in the corner before his bed, standing side by side where the landlady had just tidied them away: old, faded-brown, stiff little boots with patches. At the sight he raised his hands and rushed to them, fell to his knees, seized one little boot and, pressing his lips to it, began kissing it hungrily, crying, “Little father, Ilyushechka, my dear little father, where are your little feet?”

<!-- EP-C03-P035 -->
“Where have you taken him? Where have you taken him?” the deranged woman cried in a heartrending voice. Ninochka began sobbing too. Kolya ran out of the room, and the boys began to follow him. Finally Alyosha came out after them. “Let them cry,” he told Kolya. “There’s certainly no comforting them now. Let’s wait a little, then go back.”

<!-- EP-C03-P036 -->
“Yes, there’s no comfort, it’s terrible,” Kolya agreed. “You know, Karamazov,” he suddenly lowered his voice so that no one would hear, “I’m very sad, and if only we could bring him back to life, I’d give everything in the world!”

<!-- EP-C03-P037 -->
“Oh, so would I,” said Alyosha.

<!-- EP-C03-P038 -->
“What do you think, Karamazov—should we come here this evening? He’ll get drunk, you know.”

<!-- EP-C03-P039 -->
“Perhaps he will. Just you and I should come, that will be enough, and sit with them for an hour, with his mother and Ninochka. If we all come at once, it will bring everything back to them,” Alyosha advised.

<!-- EP-C03-P040 -->
“The landlady’s setting the table in there now—there’ll be a funeral meal, I suppose, and the priest’s coming. Should we go back there now, Karamazov, or not?”

<!-- EP-C03-P041 -->
“Certainly,” said Alyosha.

<!-- EP-C03-P042 -->
“It’s all so strange, Karamazov: such grief, and suddenly pancakes of all things—how unnatural it all is in our religion!”

<!-- EP-C03-P043 -->
“They’re having salmon too,” the boy who had discovered Troy suddenly remarked loudly.

<!-- EP-C03-P044 -->
“I’m seriously asking you, Kartashov, to stop interrupting with your foolish remarks, especially when no one is speaking to you or even wants to know whether you exist,” Kolya snapped at him irritably. The boy flared crimson, but did not dare answer. Meanwhile they were all wandering slowly along the path, when Smurov suddenly exclaimed:

<!-- EP-C03-P045 -->
“Here’s Ilyusha’s stone, where they wanted to bury him!”

<!-- EP-C03-P046 -->
They all stopped silently by the large stone. Alyosha looked at it, and the whole scene Snegiryov had once described to him came back to him at once: Ilyushechka weeping and embracing his father, crying, “Papa, Papa, how he humiliated you!” Something seemed to shake within his soul. Gravely and earnestly he looked around at all those dear, bright faces, the schoolboys who had been Ilyusha’s friends, and suddenly said:

<!-- EP-C03-P047 -->
“Gentlemen, I’d like to say a word to you here, in this very place.”

<!-- EP-C03-P048 -->
The boys gathered around him and at once fixed their eyes on him, intent and expectant.

<!-- EP-C03-P049 -->
“Gentlemen, we’ll soon be parting. For a little while yet I’ll be here with my two brothers, one of whom is going into exile, while the other lies near death. But soon I’ll leave this town, perhaps for a very long time. So we’ll be parting, gentlemen. Let’s agree here, by Ilyusha’s little stone, that we will never forget—first, Ilyushechka, and second, one another. Whatever happens to us later in life, even if we don’t meet again for twenty years, let’s still remember how we buried the poor boy we used to throw stones at—remember, there by the little bridge?—and whom we later all came to love so much. He was a fine boy, a kind and brave boy; he felt his father’s honor and the bitter insult to him, and rose up to defend him. So first of all, gentlemen, let’s remember him all our lives. And even if we’re occupied with the most important affairs, attain honors, or fall into some great misfortune, never forget how good it once felt to be here, all together, united in such a good and kind feeling, which perhaps made us better than we really are during this time of our love for the poor boy. My little doves—let me call you that, little doves, because now, at this moment, as I look at your kind, dear faces, you all look so much like those pretty blue-gray birds—my dear children, perhaps you won’t understand what I’m going to tell you, because I often speak very unclearly; but remember it all the same, and someday you’ll agree with my words. You must know that there is nothing higher or stronger or healthier or more useful for your future life than some good memory, especially one brought with you from childhood, from your parents’ home. People say a great deal to you about your education, but perhaps some beautiful, sacred memory like this, preserved from childhood, is the best education of all. If a person gathers many such memories to take into life, he is saved for his whole life. And even if only one good memory remains with us in our hearts, that too may someday serve to save us. Perhaps we’ll even become wicked later, unable to resist doing wrong; we’ll laugh at human tears and at people who say what Kolya cried out earlier, ‘I want to suffer for all people’—perhaps we’ll mock such people viciously. And yet, however wicked we become, which God forbid, when we remember how we buried Ilyusha, how we loved him in his last days, and how we spoke just now, as such friends, all together by this stone, even the cruelest and most mocking among us, if we become like that, still won’t dare laugh in his heart at how kind and good he was at this very moment! More than that, perhaps this memory alone will keep him from some great evil, and he’ll come to his senses and say, ‘Yes, I was kind then, brave and honest.’ Let him smile to himself; it doesn’t matter. People often laugh at what is kind and good; it’s only thoughtlessness. But I assure you, gentlemen, that as soon as he smiles, he’ll say in his heart, ‘No, I was wrong to smile, because this is something one mustn’t laugh at!’”

<!-- EP-C03-P050 -->
“That’s certainly how it will be, Karamazov! I understand you, Karamazov!” Kolya exclaimed, his eyes flashing. The boys stirred and wanted to call something out too, but restrained themselves, gazing intently and tenderly at the speaker.

<!-- EP-C03-P051 -->
“I say this in case we become bad,” Alyosha continued, “but why should we become bad, gentlemen? Isn’t that so? Let’s be kind first and foremost, then honest, and then—let’s never forget one another. I repeat that again. For my part, gentlemen, I give you my word that I won’t forget a single one of you; I’ll remember every face looking at me here and now, even thirty years from now. Earlier Kolya told Kartashov that supposedly we don’t want to know ‘whether he exists or not.’ But how could I forget that Kartashov exists, and that now he’s no longer blushing as he did when he discovered Troy, but looking at me with his fine, kind, cheerful eyes? Gentlemen, my dear gentlemen, let us all be generous and brave like Ilyushechka, intelligent, brave, and generous like Kolya—though he’ll be much more intelligent when he grows up—and let us be as shy, but as clever and dear, as Kartashov. But why am I talking about just those two! From now on all of you are dear to me, gentlemen; I’ll take all of you into my heart, and I ask you to take me into yours as well! And who has brought us together in this good, kind feeling, which we shall always remember now, all our lives, and mean to remember—who but Ilyushechka, that kind boy, that dear boy, a boy dear to us forever and ever! Let’s never forget him; may his memory live forever, a good memory in our hearts, now and forever and ever!”

<!-- EP-C03-P052 -->
“Yes, yes, forever, forever!” all the boys cried in their ringing voices, their faces full of tenderness.

<!-- EP-C03-P053 -->
“Let’s remember his face, his clothes, his poor little boots, his little coffin, his unhappy, sinful father, and how bravely he stood up alone against the whole class for his sake!”

<!-- EP-C03-P054 -->
“We will, we’ll remember!” the boys cried again. “He was brave, he was kind!”

<!-- EP-C03-P055 -->
“Oh, how I loved him!” Kolya exclaimed.

<!-- EP-C03-P056 -->
“Oh, children, my dear friends, don’t be afraid of life! How good life is when you do something good and true!”

<!-- EP-C03-P057 -->
“Yes, yes,” the boys repeated in delight.

<!-- EP-C03-P058 -->
“Karamazov, we love you!” a voice burst out uncontrollably, apparently Kartashov’s.

<!-- EP-C03-P059 -->
“We love you, we love you,” all the others took it up. Tears shone in many eyes.

<!-- EP-C03-P060 -->
“Hurrah for Karamazov!” Kolya proclaimed ecstatically.

<!-- EP-C03-P061 -->
“And eternal memory to the dead boy!”[^ep-c03-n001] Alyosha added again with feeling.

<!-- EP-C03-P062 -->
“Eternal memory!” the boys took up once more.

<!-- EP-C03-P063 -->
“Karamazov!” Kolya cried. “Does religion really and truly say that we’ll all rise from the dead and come back to life, and see one another again, and everyone, and Ilyushechka?”

<!-- EP-C03-P064 -->
“We shall certainly rise, we shall certainly see one another, and happily, joyfully tell one another everything that has happened,” Alyosha replied, half laughing, half in rapture.

<!-- EP-C03-P065 -->
“Oh, how good that will be!” burst from Kolya.

<!-- EP-C03-P066 -->
“Well, now let’s finish our speeches and go to his funeral meal. Don’t be troubled that we’ll be eating pancakes. It’s an ancient, enduring custom, and there’s something good in it,” Alyosha laughed. “Come, let’s go! Here we go now, hand in hand.”

<!-- EP-C03-P067 -->
“And forever like this, all our lives, hand in hand! Hurrah for Karamazov!” Kolya cried ecstatically once more, and once more all the boys took up his cry.

[^ep-c03-n001]: “Eternal memory” is a prayer sung at the close of Orthodox funeral and memorial services, asking God to remember the departed eternally.
