# Book II, Chapter VII
## A Seminarian on the Make

*Part I · Book II: An Inappropriate Gathering*

<!-- B02-C07-P001 -->
Alyosha led his elder into the little bedroom and seated him on the bed. The room was very small, with only the necessary furniture; the narrow iron bed had nothing but felt in place of a mattress. In a corner, beside the icons, stood a lectern with a cross and the Gospel book on it. The elder sank onto the bed, exhausted; his eyes shone, and he was breathing with difficulty. Once seated, he looked intently at Alyosha, as though pondering something.

<!-- B02-C07-P002 -->
“Go, my dear, go. Porfiry is all I need here, and you must hurry. They need you there. Go to Father Abbot and help serve dinner.”

<!-- B02-C07-P003 -->
“Give me your blessing to stay here,” Alyosha said pleadingly.

<!-- B02-C07-P004 -->
“They need you more there. There is no peace there. You can serve and be of use. If the demons rise up, say a prayer. And know, my son”—the elder liked to call him that—“that your place will not be here in the days to come, either. Remember this, young man. As soon as God grants me leave to depart this life, leave the monastery. Leave it altogether.”

<!-- B02-C07-P005 -->
Alyosha shuddered.

<!-- B02-C07-P006 -->
“What's the matter? Your place is not here for now. I give you my blessing for a great obedience in the world. You have much wandering still ahead of you. And you will have to marry, you will. You must endure everything before you return. And there will be much work to do. But I have no doubts about you, and that is why I send you. Christ is with you. Keep him, and he will keep you. You will see great sorrow, and in that sorrow you will be happy. Here is my charge to you: seek happiness in sorrow. Work, work without ceasing. Remember my words from this day on, for though I shall speak with you again, not only my days but my hours are numbered.”

<!-- B02-C07-P007 -->
Strong emotion showed again in Alyosha's face. The corners of his mouth trembled.

<!-- B02-C07-P008 -->
“What is it now?” The elder smiled gently. “Let those in the world weep over their dead; here we rejoice for a father who is departing. We rejoice and pray for him. Leave me now. I must pray. Go, and hurry. Stay close to your brothers. And not just one of them, but both.”

<!-- B02-C07-P009 -->
The elder raised his hand in blessing. There could be no arguing, much as Alyosha wanted to stay. He also wanted to ask a question, and it was almost on his lips: “What did that bow to the ground before my brother Dmitri signify?” But he did not dare ask. He knew the elder would have explained it himself, without being asked, if he could have. Evidently, then, he did not wish to. That bow had shaken Alyosha terribly; he believed blindly that it held a mysterious meaning. Mysterious, and perhaps dreadful. When he passed beyond the hermitage enclosure to reach the monastery in time for the start of dinner at the abbot's—only to serve at table, of course—his heart suddenly contracted painfully, and he stopped where he stood: he seemed to hear the elder's words again, foretelling his own approaching death. Whatever the elder foretold, and with such precision, must certainly come to pass; Alyosha had an unshakable faith in that. But how was he to go on without him, never seeing him, never hearing him? And where was he to go? He tells me not to cry and to leave the monastery—Lord! Alyosha had not felt such anguish for a long time. He hurried through the wood that separated the hermitage from the monastery and, unable even to bear his thoughts, so heavily did they weigh on him, began looking at the ancient pines on either side of the woodland path. The walk was short, five hundred paces at most; at this hour there should have been no one to meet, but suddenly, at the first bend in the path, he caught sight of Rakitin. He was waiting for someone.

<!-- B02-C07-P010 -->
“Are you waiting for me?” Alyosha asked as he came up to him.

<!-- B02-C07-P011 -->
“For you, precisely,” Rakitin said with a smirk. “Hurrying to Father Abbot's. I know; he's giving a dinner. There hasn't been a spread like it since he entertained the bishop and General Pakhatov, remember? I won't be there, but off you go and pass the sauces. Tell me one thing, Alexei: what does this dream signify? That's what I wanted to ask.”

<!-- B02-C07-P012 -->
“What dream?”

<!-- B02-C07-P013 -->
“That bow to the ground before your dear brother Dmitri Fyodorovich. And the way he banged his forehead!”

<!-- B02-C07-P014 -->
“You mean Father Zosima?”

<!-- B02-C07-P015 -->
“Yes, Father Zosima.”

<!-- B02-C07-P016 -->
“His forehead?”

<!-- B02-C07-P017 -->
“Ah, I've spoken disrespectfully! Well, let it be disrespectful. So, what does this dream signify?”

<!-- B02-C07-P018 -->
“I don't know what it means, Misha.”

<!-- B02-C07-P019 -->
“I knew he wouldn't explain it to you. Nothing very mysterious about it, of course; you might think it was just the usual pious nonsense. But the trick was done on purpose. Now all the sanctimonious types in town will start talking and spread it through the province: ‘What does this dream signify?’ In my opinion, the old man really does have second sight: he's sniffed out a crime. There's a stink about your family.”

<!-- B02-C07-P020 -->
“What crime?”

<!-- B02-C07-P021 -->
Rakitin plainly had something he wanted to say.

<!-- B02-C07-P022 -->
“There'll be a crime in that dear family of yours. It'll happen between your dear brothers and your wealthy dear papa. So Father Zosima banged his forehead just in case of future developments. Then, whatever happens: ‘Ah, the holy elder foretold it, he prophesied it!’ Though what sort of prophecy is there in banging your forehead? No, they'll say, it was an emblem, an allegory, and the devil knows what else! They'll make him famous, they'll remember: he foresaw the crime, he marked out the criminal. It's always like that with holy fools: they cross themselves before a tavern and throw stones at a church. Your elder's the same: drives a righteous man away with a stick and bows at a murderer's feet.”

<!-- B02-C07-P023 -->
“What crime? What murderer? What are you saying?” Alyosha stopped dead, and Rakitin stopped too.

<!-- B02-C07-P024 -->
“What murderer? As if you didn't know! I'll bet you've already thought about it yourself. That's an interesting point, by the way. Listen, Alyosha, you always tell the truth, though you're always trying to sit on two chairs at once: have you thought about it or haven't you? Answer me.”

<!-- B02-C07-P025 -->
“I have,” Alyosha answered quietly. Even Rakitin was taken aback.

<!-- B02-C07-P026 -->
“What? Have you really thought about it too?” he cried.

<!-- B02-C07-P027 -->
“I… I wouldn't say I'd thought about it,” Alyosha murmured. “But when you began talking about it just now in that strange way, it seemed to me that I'd thought about it myself.”

<!-- B02-C07-P028 -->
“You see—and how clearly you put it—you see? Watching Papa and dear brother Mitenka today, you thought of a crime? So I'm not mistaken, am I?”

<!-- B02-C07-P029 -->
“But wait, wait,” Alyosha broke in anxiously. “What makes you see all this?.. Why are you so interested in it? That's the first thing.”

<!-- B02-C07-P030 -->
“Two separate questions, but natural ones. I'll answer each in turn. Why do I see it? I wouldn't see anything if I hadn't suddenly understood Dmitri Fyodorovich, your brother, today, understood him completely, all at once, just as he is. Some single trait let me grasp the whole man at once. With these utterly honorable but lustful men, there's a line you mustn't cross. Otherwise—otherwise he'll stick a knife even in his own papa. And Papa's a drunken, dissolute lecher who's never known when to stop at anything. Neither of them will hold back, and bang, into the ditch they both go…”

<!-- B02-C07-P031 -->
“No, Misha, no. If that's all, you've reassured me. It won't come to that.”

<!-- B02-C07-P032 -->
“Then why are you shaking all over? Do you know something? Mitenka may be an honest man—he's stupid, but honest—but he's a sensualist. That defines him, that's his whole inner nature. His father has passed his vile sensuality on to him. It's only you I wonder at, Alyosha: how can you be a virgin? You're a Karamazov too! Sensuality in your family has reached a fever pitch. And now these three sensualists are watching one another… with knives tucked into their boots. Three heads have come together, and you may be the fourth.”

<!-- B02-C07-P033 -->
“You're mistaken about that woman. Dmitri… despises her,” Alyosha said with a sort of shudder.

<!-- B02-C07-P034 -->
“Grushenka? No, my friend, he doesn't despise her. Once he's openly traded his fiancée for her, he doesn't despise her. There's… there's something here, my friend, you can't understand yet. A man falls in love with some beauty, with a woman's body, or even with just one part of a woman's body—a sensualist can understand that—and he'll give his own children for her, sell his father and mother, Russia and his native land; an honest man, he'll go and steal; a gentle man, he'll cut someone's throat; a faithful man, he'll betray. Pushkin, the singer of women's little feet, sang of those feet in verse; others don't sing, but can't look at a woman's feet without going into convulsions. And it isn't only feet… Contempt is no help here, my friend, even if he does despise Grushenka. He despises her, but can't tear himself away.”

<!-- B02-C07-P035 -->
“I understand that,” Alyosha suddenly blurted out.

<!-- B02-C07-P036 -->
“Really? So you really do understand it, if you blurt out that you do at the first word,” Rakitin said with malicious delight. “You let that slip, it came out before you knew it. All the more valuable a confession: so you're familiar with the subject already, you've already thought about it, about sensuality. Ah, you virgin! You're a quiet one, Alyoshka, you're a saint, I grant you, but you're a quiet one, and the devil knows what you haven't thought about already, the devil knows what you know! A virgin, and you've already gone so deep—I've been watching you a long time. You're a Karamazov yourself, a Karamazov through and through, so breeding and selection must count for something. A sensualist on your father's side, a holy fool on your mother's. Why are you trembling? Am I telling the truth? You know what? Grushenka asked me: ‘Bring him to me’—meaning you—‘and I'll pull his little cassock off.’ And how she asked! Bring him, bring him! I just wondered: what makes her so curious about you? She's an extraordinary woman too, you know!”

<!-- B02-C07-P037 -->
“Give her my regards and tell her I won't come,” Alyosha said with a crooked smile. “Finish what you began, Mikhail; then I'll tell you what I think.”

<!-- B02-C07-P038 -->
“What is there to finish? It's all clear. It's the same old tune, my friend. If even you have a sensualist inside you, what about your brother Ivan, born of the same mother? He's a Karamazov too. That's the whole Karamazov question: sensualists, money-grubbers, and holy fools! Your brother Ivan is publishing his little theological articles as a joke for the time being, with some utterly idiotic ulterior purpose no one knows, while he's an atheist himself and admits his own baseness—this brother of yours, Ivan. Besides, he's stealing brother Mitya's fiancée for himself, and it looks as though he'll get her. And how: with Mitenka's own consent, because Mitenka is handing over his fiancée himself, just to be free of her and hurry off to Grushenka. And all this with all his nobility and selflessness, mark that. Those are the very people who bring the worst disasters! The devil himself couldn't make you out after that: a man admits his own baseness and plunges straight into it! Listen to the rest: now Mitenka's old rascal of a father is getting in his way. He's suddenly gone mad over Grushenka; he drools just looking at her. She's the sole reason he made such a scandal in the cell just now, only because Miusov dared call her a dissolute creature. He's worse than a tomcat in love. Before this, she just worked for him here for wages, helping with some shady little dealings involving the taverns, but now he's suddenly realized, had a good look, gone wild, and started pestering her with proposals—not honorable ones, of course. And won't they collide on that path, dear papa and dear son! But Grushenka won't have either one; for now she keeps dodging and teasing them both, watching to see which will pay off better. Because although she could grab plenty of money from Papa, he won't marry her, and perhaps in the end he'll turn Jew and snap his purse shut. In that case Mitenka has his value too: he has no money, but he might marry her. Yes, sir, he might marry her! Jilt his fiancée, the incomparable beauty Katerina Ivanovna, rich, of noble birth, a colonel's daughter, and marry Grushenka, the former kept woman of a nasty old merchant, that debauched peasant and mayor, Samsonov. All this really could end in a criminal collision. And that's what your brother Ivan is waiting for; then he'll be sitting pretty: he'll get Katerina Ivanovna, whom he's pining for, and grab her sixty thousand in dowry. For a penniless nobody like him, that's a very tempting start in life. And mark this: he won't just avoid hurting Mitya, he'll put him in his debt for life. I know for a fact that only last week Mitenka himself was shouting in a tavern, drunk, with the Gypsy girls, that he wasn't worthy of his fiancée Katenka, but his brother Ivan—now, he was worthy. And Katerina Ivanovna certainly won't refuse a charmer like Ivan Fyodorovich in the end; she's wavering between the two of them already. What has this Ivan done to bewitch you all, that you're all in awe of him? He's laughing at the lot of you: here I sit pretty, he says, feasting at your expense.”

<!-- B02-C07-P039 -->
“How do you know all this? Why are you so certain?” Alyosha suddenly asked sharply, frowning.

<!-- B02-C07-P040 -->
“And why do you ask now, afraid of my answer before you've heard it? That means you agree I've told the truth.”

<!-- B02-C07-P041 -->
“You don't like Ivan. Ivan won't be tempted by money.”

<!-- B02-C07-P042 -->
“Really? And Katerina Ivanovna's beauty? It isn't only money, though sixty thousand is tempting.”

<!-- B02-C07-P043 -->
“Ivan looks higher. Thousands won't tempt Ivan. He isn't looking for money or peace of mind. Perhaps he's looking for suffering.”

<!-- B02-C07-P044 -->
“What sort of dream is this now? Oh, you… noblemen!”

<!-- B02-C07-P045 -->
“Ah, Misha, his soul is in turmoil. His mind is captive. There's a great, unresolved idea inside him. He's one of those people who don't need millions; they need to resolve an idea.”

<!-- B02-C07-P046 -->
“Literary theft, Alyoshka. You're paraphrasing your elder. What a riddle Ivan's set you all!” Rakitin shouted with open malice. Even his face changed, and his lips twisted. “And it's a stupid riddle, there's nothing to solve. Use your brains and you'll understand. His article is ridiculous and absurd. And did you hear his stupid theory earlier: ‘If there's no immortality of the soul, there's no virtue either, so everything is permitted.’ And brother Mitenka, by the way, remember how he shouted, ‘I'll remember that!’ A tempting theory for scoundrels… I'm being abusive, that's stupid… not for scoundrels, but for schoolboy braggarts with their ‘unsolvable depths of thought.’ A little boaster, and it all comes down to: ‘On the one hand, one cannot but admit, and on the other, one cannot but acknowledge!’ His whole theory is vile! Humanity will find the strength within itself to live for virtue, even without believing in the immortality of the soul! It will find it in the love of liberty, equality, fraternity…”

<!-- B02-C07-P047 -->
Rakitin had grown heated and could hardly contain himself. But suddenly he stopped, as though remembering something.

<!-- B02-C07-P048 -->
“Enough of that.” His smile was even more crooked than before. “What are you laughing at? Do you think I'm a vulgarian?”

<!-- B02-C07-P049 -->
“No, it never even occurred to me to think you vulgar. You're intelligent, but… never mind, it was stupid of me to smile. I understand how you can get carried away, Misha. Your vehemence made me guess that you have feelings for Katerina Ivanovna yourself. I've suspected it for a long time, my friend, and that's why you dislike my brother Ivan. Are you jealous of him?”

<!-- B02-C07-P050 -->
“And jealous of her money too? Why don't you add that?”

<!-- B02-C07-P051 -->
“No, I won't add anything about money. I won't insult you.”

<!-- B02-C07-P052 -->
“I believe you because you've said so, but damn you and your brother Ivan all over again! None of you can understand that a man can dislike him very much without Katerina Ivanovna coming into it. And why the devil should I like him? He condescends to abuse me, doesn't he? Why shouldn't I have the right to abuse him?”

<!-- B02-C07-P053 -->
“I've never heard him say anything about you, good or bad; he doesn't talk about you at all.”

<!-- B02-C07-P054 -->
“Well, I heard that the day before yesterday at Katerina Ivanovna's he tore me to shreds—that's how interested he was in your humble servant. So who's jealous of whom, my friend, I don't know! He was pleased to express the opinion that if I don't agree to a career as an archimandrite in the very near future and don't decide to take monastic vows, I'll certainly go to Petersburg and join one of the big literary journals, certainly its criticism department, write for ten years or so, and eventually get the journal transferred to my name. Then I'll go on publishing it myself, and certainly along liberal and atheist lines, with a tinge of socialism, even a little gloss of socialism, while keeping my ears open—which means, essentially, playing both sides and pulling the wool over fools' eyes. According to your dear brother's interpretation, my career ends like this: the tinge of socialism won't prevent me from putting the subscription money in a current account and investing it when the opportunity comes, under the guidance of some little Jew, until I've built a substantial house in Petersburg, moved the editorial offices into it, and filled the other floors with tenants. He even picked the place for the house: by the New Stone Bridge over the Neva, which they say is being planned in Petersburg, from the Liteiny side to the Vyborg side…”

<!-- B02-C07-P055 -->
“Oh, Misha, perhaps it'll all come true just like that, down to the very last word!” Alyosha suddenly cried, unable to help himself, smiling merrily.

<!-- B02-C07-P056 -->
“So you're taking up sarcasm too, Alexei Fyodorovich.”

<!-- B02-C07-P057 -->
“No, no, I'm joking, forgive me. I have something quite different on my mind. But wait: who could have told you all these details, and who could you have heard them from? You couldn't have been at Katerina Ivanovna's yourself when he was talking about you?”

<!-- B02-C07-P058 -->
“I wasn't, but Dmitri Fyodorovich was, and I heard it with my own ears from Dmitri Fyodorovich himself. That is, if you like, he wasn't speaking to me; I overheard it—unavoidably, of course—because I was sitting in Grushenka's bedroom and couldn't come out the whole time Dmitri Fyodorovich was in the next room.”

<!-- B02-C07-P059 -->
“Oh yes, I'd forgotten, she's a relative of yours…”

<!-- B02-C07-P060 -->
“A relative? Grushenka is a relative of mine?” Rakitin suddenly cried, going red all over. “Have you lost your mind? Something wrong with your brains?”

<!-- B02-C07-P061 -->
“Why? Isn't she a relative? That's what I heard…”

<!-- B02-C07-P062 -->
“Where could you have heard that? No, you gentlemen Karamazovs put on airs as though you belonged to some grand and ancient noble family, while your father used to run from table to table playing the buffoon for other people's dinners and was lucky to be allowed a place in the kitchen. Granted, I'm only a priest's son, a louse beside you noblemen, but don't insult me so cheerfully and recklessly. I have my honor too, Alexei Fyodorovich. I cannot be related to Grushenka, a common whore, kindly understand that, sir!”

<!-- B02-C07-P063 -->
Rakitin was extremely irritated.

<!-- B02-C07-P064 -->
“Forgive me, for God's sake, I never could have imagined—and besides, how is she a whore? Is she… that sort of woman?” Alyosha suddenly blushed. “I tell you, I'd heard she was a relative. You visit her often, and you told me yourself that you aren't having a love affair with her… I never thought that you, of all people, despised her so much! Does she really deserve it?”

<!-- B02-C07-P065 -->
“If I visit her, I may have reasons of my own, and that's enough for you. As for relations, your dear brother, or even your father himself, is more likely to saddle you with her as a relative than me. Well, here we are. You'd better go to the kitchen. Ah! What's going on here, what's this? Are we late? They can't have finished dinner so soon! Have the Karamazovs been up to more mischief? That must be it. There's your father, with Ivan Fyodorovich behind him. They've broken loose from the abbot's. There's Father Isidor calling something after them from the porch. And your father's shouting and waving his arms too—swearing, I suppose. Well, well, there's Miusov driving off in the carriage, see him going? And there's the landowner Maximov running—there's been a scandal; so there was no dinner! Surely they haven't beaten up the abbot? Or perhaps they've been beaten up themselves? That would serve them right!..”

<!-- B02-C07-P066 -->
Rakitin's exclamations were not without cause. There had indeed been a scandal, unheard-of and unexpected. It had all happened “on an inspiration.”
