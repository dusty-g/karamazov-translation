# The Brothers Karamazov

## A Novel in Four Parts with an Epilogue

## From the Author

<!-- FM-PRE-P001 -->
As I begin the life of my hero, Alexei Fyodorovich Karamazov, I find myself somewhat perplexed. To be precise: although I call Alexei Fyodorovich my hero, I know perfectly well that he is by no means a great man, and so I foresee the inevitable questions: What is so remarkable about your Alexei Fyodorovich that you have chosen him as your hero? What has he done? Who knows him, and for what? Why should I, the reader, spend my time studying the facts of his life?

<!-- FM-PRE-P002 -->
The last question is the most fatal, for I can only answer it by saying, “Perhaps you will see for yourself from the novel.” But what if they read the novel and fail to see, and will not agree that my Alexei Fyodorovich is remarkable? I say this because, to my sorrow, I foresee it. He is remarkable to me, but I very much doubt whether I can prove it to the reader. The thing is, he may be a man of action, but a man of action who remains hard to define or make out. Still, it would be strange to demand clarity of people at a time like ours. One thing, perhaps, is fairly certain: he is a strange man, even an eccentric. But strangeness and eccentricity tend to count against a man rather than entitle him to attention, especially when everyone is trying to bring the separate particulars together and find at least some common meaning amid the general confusion. An eccentric, in most cases, is a separate particular, something apart. Isn't that so?

<!-- FM-PRE-P003 -->
Now, if you disagree with that last proposition and answer, “No,” or “Not always,” I may take heart about the significance of my hero, Alexei Fyodorovich. For it is not merely that an eccentric is “not always” a separate particular, something apart; on the contrary, it sometimes happens that he, perhaps, carries within him the very heart of the whole, while all the other people of his age have, for some reason, been torn away from it for a time by some passing gust of wind…

<!-- FM-PRE-P004 -->
I would not, however, have embarked on these thoroughly uninteresting and vague explanations, but would simply have begun without a preface: if people like it, they will read it anyway. The trouble is that I have one life to tell, but two novels. The principal novel is the second,[^fm-n002] dealing with my hero's activity in our own time—in this very moment we are living through. The first novel, however, took place thirteen years ago, and is almost not even a novel, but merely a moment from my hero's early youth. I cannot dispense with this first novel, because much in the second would be incomprehensible without it. But this makes my original difficulty still more complicated: if even I, the biographer himself, think that one novel might be too much for such a modest and as yet undefined hero, what must it look like for me to come forward with two, and how am I to explain such presumption on my part?

<!-- FM-PRE-P005 -->
Unable to find my way to a resolution of these questions, I resolve to pass them by without resolving anything. Of course, the perceptive reader guessed long ago that this was where I was heading from the start, and has only been annoyed with me for wasting fruitless words and precious time. To this I can give a precise answer: I wasted fruitless words and precious time, first, out of politeness, and second, out of cunning: at least, I could say, I had given some sort of warning in advance. Still, I am actually glad that my novel has divided of its own accord into two stories, “with the essential unity of the whole preserved”: having read the first story, readers can decide for themselves whether the second is worth beginning. Of course, no one is bound to anything; you can abandon the book after two pages of the first story and never open it again. But there are readers so scrupulous that they will insist on reading to the end so as not to go wrong in reaching an impartial judgment; all Russian critics, for example, are like that. Well, I feel easier where those readers are concerned: for all their care and conscientiousness, I am still giving them a perfectly legitimate excuse to abandon the story after the novel's first episode. Well, that is the whole preface. I entirely agree that it is unnecessary, but since it has already been written, let it stay. And now to work.

[^fm-n001]: Anna Grigoryevna Dostoevskaya was Dostoevsky’s second wife.

[^fm-n002]: The second novel was a planned continuation of The Brothers Karamazov that Dostoevsky never wrote.
