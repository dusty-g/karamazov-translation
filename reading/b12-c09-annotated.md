# Book XII: A Judicial Error

## IX. Psychology at Full Steam. The Galloping Troika. The Prosecutor’s Finale

<!-- B12-C09-P001 -->
Having reached this point in his speech, Ippolit Kirillovich—who had evidently chosen that strictly chronological method of presentation so beloved of nervous orators, who deliberately seek rigid boundaries to restrain their own impatient enthusiasm—Ippolit Kirillovich dwelt at particular length on the “former” and “indisputable” lover, and offered several thoughts on the subject that were interesting in their way. “Karamazov, insanely jealous of everyone, suddenly seems to collapse and vanish before the ‘former’ and ‘indisputable’ lover. This is all the stranger because previously he had paid almost no attention to this new danger approaching him in the person of an unexpected rival. But he had kept imagining that it was still so far away, and Karamazov always lives only in the present moment. Probably he even regarded him as a fiction. But the instant his sick heart understood that perhaps this woman had concealed this new rival, had deceived him earlier that day, precisely because this rival who had flown back to her was anything but a fantasy or a fiction to her, because he meant everything to her, her whole hope in life—the instant he understood this, he submitted. Well, gentlemen of the jury, I cannot pass over this sudden trait in the defendant’s soul, a trait he would have seemed utterly incapable of displaying: an irresistible need for truth, for respect for a woman, for recognition of the rights of her heart suddenly made itself felt—and when? At the very moment when he had stained his hands with his father’s blood on her account! It is also true that at that moment the blood he had shed had already cried out for vengeance, for he, who had destroyed his own soul and his whole earthly destiny, must involuntarily have felt and asked himself: ‘What am I, and what can I mean now to her, to this creature I love more than my own soul, beside this “former” and “indisputable” lover, repentant and returned to the woman he once ruined, with renewed love, honorable proposals, a promise of a reborn and now happy life? And I, wretch that I am, what can I give her now, what can I offer?’ Karamazov understood all this, understood that his crime had closed every road to him and that he was merely a criminal condemned to death, not a man with a life before him! This thought crushed and annihilated him. And at once he settled on one frenzied plan which, given Karamazov’s character, could not fail to strike him as the sole and fatal escape from his terrible situation. That escape was suicide. He runs for the pistols he has pawned to the official Perkhotin, and at the same time, on the way, as he runs, he snatches out of his pocket all his money, for which he has just spattered his hands with his father’s blood. Oh, now he needs money more than anything: Karamazov is dying, Karamazov is shooting himself, and people will remember it! We are a poet, after all; we have not burned our life like a candle at both ends for nothing. ‘To her, to her—and there, oh, there I shall give a feast for all the world, such as there has never been, so they will remember and talk about it for years. Amid wild shouts, frenzied gypsy songs and dances, we shall raise a glass and congratulate the adored woman on her new happiness, and then—there and then, at her feet, we shall shatter our skull before her eyes and execute ourselves! Someday she will remember Mitya Karamazov, she will see how Mitya loved her, she will pity Mitya!’ Much theatrical effect, romantic frenzy, wild Karamazov abandon and sentimentality—and something else too, gentlemen of the jury, something that cries out in his soul, beats unceasingly in his brain, and poisons his heart to death. That something is conscience, gentlemen of the jury, its judgment, its terrible remorse! But the pistol will reconcile everything; the pistol is the only way out, there is no other, and afterward—I do not know whether Karamazov thought at that moment about ‘what will be there,’ or whether Karamazov can think like Hamlet about what comes afterward. No, gentlemen of the jury, they have their Hamlets, but we, for the present, still have our Karamazovs!”

Here Ippolit Kirillovich unfolded a most detailed picture of Mitya’s preparations, the scene at Perkhotin’s, at the shop, with the drivers. He cited a mass of words, sayings, and gestures, all confirmed by witnesses, and the picture had a tremendous effect on his listeners’ convictions. Above all, it was the accumulation of facts that told. The guilt of this man, frantically floundering and no longer taking any care for himself, appeared irresistible. “He had no reason left to take care of himself,” said Ippolit Kirillovich. “Two or three times he almost confessed everything, all but hinted at it, and merely stopped short of finishing”—here followed the witnesses’ testimony. “He even shouted to the driver on the road: ‘Do you know you’re carrying a murderer?’ But he could not finish saying it yet: he had first to reach the village of Mokroye and complete his poem there. Yet what awaits the unfortunate man? The fact is that almost from his first moments in Mokroye he sees, and finally understands completely, that his ‘indisputable’ rival may be anything but indisputable now, and that his congratulations on their new happiness and his toast are neither wanted nor accepted. But you already know the facts, gentlemen of the jury, from the examination in court. Karamazov’s triumph over his rival proved incontestable, and here—oh, here an entirely new phase began in his soul, indeed the most terrible phase of all that this soul has endured or ever will endure! We may positively affirm, gentlemen of the jury,” Ippolit Kirillovich exclaimed, “that outraged nature and a criminal heart avenge themselves more fully than any earthly justice! More than that: justice and earthly punishment even lighten nature’s punishment, are even necessary to the criminal’s soul at such moments to save it from despair, for I cannot imagine the horror and the moral suffering of Karamazov when he learned that she loved him, that for his sake she was rejecting her ‘former’ and ‘indisputable’ lover, that it was him, him, ‘Mitya,’ whom she was calling to a renewed life with her, promising him happiness—and when? When everything was already over for him and nothing was possible any longer! Incidentally, I shall make in passing one observation of great importance to us, to explain the true nature of the defendant’s position at that time: this woman, his love, had remained inaccessible to him right up to that last moment, even to the very instant of his arrest, passionately desired but unattainable. But why, why did he not shoot himself then and there, why did he abandon his settled intention and even forget where his pistol was? It was precisely this passionate thirst for love, and the hope of satisfying it there and then, that held him back. In the fumes of the feast he clung to his beloved, who was feasting with him, lovelier and more alluring to him than ever; he never leaves her, gazes at her in delight, loses himself before her. This passionate thirst could even suppress for a moment not only his fear of arrest but remorse itself! For a moment, oh, only for a moment! I picture the criminal’s soul at that time in absolute, slavish subjection to three elements which had utterly overwhelmed it: first, intoxication, the fumes and uproar, the stamping of the dance, the shrill songs, and she, she, flushed with wine, singing and dancing, drunk and laughing at him! Second, a reassuring, distant dream that the fatal reckoning is still far off, at least not near—that they will not come and take him until the following day, until morning. Several hours, then: that is a great deal, a tremendous amount! Much can be thought up in several hours. I imagine his experience was something like that of a criminal being taken to execution, to the gallows: there is still a long, long street to travel, and at a walking pace, past thousands of people, then a turn into another street, and only at the end of that other street the terrible square! It seems to me that at the start of the procession the condemned man, sitting in his chariot of shame, must feel that an endless life still lies before him. But the houses slip past, the chariot keeps moving—oh, never mind, it is still so far to the turn into the second street, and he still looks briskly to right and left, and at those thousands of indifferent, curious people whose eyes are fixed on him, and he still imagines that he is a man just like them. But now they have turned into the other street—oh, never mind, never mind, there is still a whole street. And however many houses slip past, he will keep thinking: ‘There are still plenty of houses left.’ And so on until the very end, until the square itself. That, I imagine, is how it was with Karamazov then. ‘They haven’t had time there yet,’ he thinks, ‘something can still be found, oh, there will still be time to devise a defense, work out a counterattack, but now, now—now she is so lovely!’ His soul is confused and terrified, yet he finds time to set aside half his money and hide it somewhere—otherwise I cannot explain where an entire half of those three thousand rubles, which he has just taken from under his father’s pillow, could have disappeared. This is not his first visit to Mokroye; he has already spent two days carousing there. He knows that large, old wooden house, with all its sheds and galleries. I specifically suppose that part of the money was hidden then, in that very house, shortly before his arrest, in some crack or crevice, under some floorboard, somewhere in a corner, under the roof—why? What do you mean, why? The catastrophe may happen at any moment; of course we have not yet thought out how to meet it, we have no time, our head is pounding, we are drawn to her—but money? Money is necessary in every situation! A man with money is somebody everywhere. Perhaps such calculation at such a moment seems unnatural to you? But he himself assures us that a month earlier, at an equally anxious and fateful moment for him, he separated half of three thousand rubles and sewed it into a pouch, and although that is certainly untrue, as we shall now prove, the idea is nevertheless familiar to Karamazov; he has contemplated it. More than that, when he later assured the investigator that he had put fifteen hundred aside in a pouch—which never existed—perhaps he invented this pouch on the spot precisely because two hours earlier he had set aside half the money and hidden it somewhere there in Mokroye, against an emergency, until morning, simply to avoid keeping it on him, on a sudden inspiration. Two abysses, gentlemen of the jury: remember that Karamazov can contemplate two abysses, and both at once! We searched that house but found nothing. Perhaps the money is still there, or perhaps it disappeared the next day and is now with the defendant. In any event, he was arrested beside her, on his knees before her; she lay on the bed, he stretched his arms toward her, and at that moment he had forgotten everything so completely that he did not even hear those approaching to arrest him. He had not yet managed to prepare an answer in his mind. Both he and his mind were taken unawares.

“And now he stands before his judges, before those who will decide his fate. Gentlemen of the jury, in the course of our duties there are moments when we ourselves feel almost afraid in the presence of a human being, afraid for that human being too! They are the moments when we contemplate that animal terror, when the criminal already sees that all is lost, yet still struggles, still intends to struggle against you. Those are the moments when all his instincts of self-preservation rise at once and, trying to save himself, he looks at you with a piercing, questioning, suffering gaze, seizes upon and studies you, your face, your thoughts, waits to see from which side you will strike, and instantly creates a thousand plans in his trembling mind, yet is afraid to speak, afraid to give himself away! These humiliating moments of the human soul, this passage through its ordeals, this animal thirst to save itself, are terrible and sometimes make even the investigator shudder and pity the criminal! And we witnessed all this then. At first he was stunned, and in his horror several words escaped him which gravely compromised him: ‘Blood! I deserve it!’ But he quickly restrained himself. What to say, how to answer—none of this is ready yet; only one unsupported denial is ready: ‘I’m not guilty of my father’s death!’ That is our fence for the moment, and there, behind the fence, perhaps we can still put something up, some sort of barricade. Forestalling our questions, he hastens to explain his first compromising exclamations by saying that he considers himself guilty only of the servant Grigory’s death. ‘I’m guilty of that blood, but who killed my father, gentlemen, who killed him? Who could have killed him, if not I?’ Do you hear that? He asks us, us, who have come to him with that very question! Do you hear that little phrase running ahead: ‘if not I,’ that animal cunning, that naïveté, that Karamazov impatience? I did not kill him, and don’t you dare think I did: ‘I wanted to kill him, gentlemen, I wanted to,’ he hurries to confess—he hurries, oh, how terribly he hurries!—‘but all the same I’m innocent, I didn’t kill him!’ He concedes that he wanted to kill him: see for yourselves, he means, how frank I am, so believe all the more readily that I did not kill him. Oh, in such cases a criminal sometimes becomes incredibly thoughtless and credulous. And here, apparently quite by chance, the investigators suddenly asked him the most innocent question: ‘Could Smerdyakov have killed him?’ Just what we expected happened: he became terribly angry because we had forestalled him and caught him unawares before he had time to prepare, select, and seize the moment at which introducing Smerdyakov would be most plausible. True to his nature, he immediately rushed to an extreme and began assuring us with all his might that Smerdyakov could not have killed him, was incapable of killing. But do not believe him, this is merely his cunning: he is not, not abandoning Smerdyakov yet; on the contrary, he will bring him forward again, because whom can he bring forward but him? Only he will do so at another moment, because for now the thing has been spoiled. Perhaps he will bring him forward only tomorrow, or even in several days, having found a moment at which he can cry out to us himself: ‘You see, I myself ruled out Smerdyakov more strongly than you did, you remember that yourselves, but now I too am convinced: he killed him, how could it have been anyone else?’ Meanwhile he falls into a sullen, irritable denial with us, yet impatience and anger prompt him to give the clumsiest, most improbable explanation of how he looked through his father’s window and how he respectfully withdrew from it. Above all, he does not yet know the circumstances, the extent of the testimony of Grigory, who has regained consciousness. We proceed to examine and search him. The examination angers but also encourages him: the full three thousand have not been found, only fifteen hundred. And surely it is only at this moment of angry silence and denial that the idea of the pouch leaps into his head for the first time in his life. Undoubtedly he feels himself how unbelievable the invention is and agonizes, agonizes terribly, over how to make it more believable, how to compose it into a whole plausible novel. In such cases the first thing, the investigator’s principal task, is to give him no time to prepare, to catch him unexpectedly, so that the criminal reveals his cherished ideas in all their self-betraying simplicity, improbability, and contradiction. And a criminal can be made to speak only by suddenly, and apparently casually, being informed of some new fact, some circumstance of the case of stupendous significance, which until then he has never suspected and could not possibly have foreseen. We had this fact ready—oh, we had had it ready for a long time: the testimony of the revived servant Grigory concerning the open door through which the defendant had run. He had entirely forgotten that door, and never imagined that Grigory could have seen it. The effect was stupendous. He jumped up and suddenly shouted at us: ‘It was Smerdyakov who killed him, Smerdyakov!’ And so he gave away his cherished, his fundamental idea in its most improbable form, since Smerdyakov could have killed only after he had struck down Grigory and run away. When we told him that Grigory had seen the unlocked door before he fell, and, on leaving his bedroom, had heard Smerdyakov groaning behind the partition, Karamazov was truly crushed. My colleague, our respected and perceptive Nikolai Parfenovich, told me afterward that at that moment he pitied him to the point of tears. And it is at this very moment, to repair the damage, that he hastens to tell us of this notorious pouch: very well, then, hear this story! Gentlemen of the jury, I have already explained why I consider this whole invention about money sewn into a pouch a month earlier not merely nonsense but the most improbable fabrication that could have been found in these circumstances. Even if someone had set out on a wager to say or imagine the most improbable thing possible, nothing worse could have been invented. The main thing here is that the triumphant novelist can be checked and utterly demolished by details, those very details with which reality is always so rich and which these unfortunate, involuntary authors always disregard as utterly insignificant and unnecessary trifles, details that never even enter their heads. Oh, they have no time for such things at that moment; their mind is creating only a grandiose whole—and someone dares put such trifles to them! But that is exactly how they are caught! The defendant is asked: ‘Well, where did you obtain the material for your pouch, who sewed it for you?’ ‘I sewed it myself.’ ‘And where did you obtain the linen?’ The defendant is already offended, regards the trifle as almost an insult, and, would you believe it, sincerely, sincerely! But that is how they all are. ‘I tore it from my shirt.’ ‘Excellent. Then tomorrow we shall find that shirt among your linen, with a piece torn out of it.’ And consider, gentlemen of the jury: if only we really had found that shirt—and how could we have failed to find it in his suitcase or chest of drawers, if such a shirt actually existed?—that would have been a fact, a tangible fact supporting the truth of his testimony! But he cannot grasp this. ‘I don’t remember, perhaps it wasn’t from a shirt, I sewed it into my landlady’s cap.’ ‘What cap?’ ‘I took it from her, it was lying about, some old calico rubbish.’ ‘And you remember that definitely?’ ‘No, not definitely…’ And he grows angry, angry, yet imagine: how could he not remember it? At the most terrible moments of a human life, say when someone is being taken to execution, it is precisely these little things that stick in the memory. He will forget everything, but some green roof glimpsed along the way, or a jackdaw on a cross—that he will remember. After all, when he sewed his pouch he was hiding from the household; he must have remembered how humiliatingly he suffered from fear, needle in hand, lest someone come in and catch him; how at the first knock he jumped up and ran behind the partition—there is a partition in his lodgings… But, gentlemen of the jury, why am I telling you all this, all these details and trifles?” Ippolit Kirillovich suddenly exclaimed. “Precisely because the defendant stubbornly maintains all this nonsense up to this very moment! In all these two months, since that night so fateful for him, he has explained nothing, added not one real explanatory circumstance to his earlier fantastic testimony. It is all trifles, he says, take my word of honor! Oh, we are glad to believe, we long to believe, even on his word of honor! What are we, jackals thirsting for human blood? Give us, show us just one fact in the defendant’s favor, and we shall rejoice—but a tangible, real fact, not his own brother’s conclusion drawn from the expression on the defendant’s face, or a claim that when he beat his breast he must necessarily have been pointing to the pouch, and in the dark at that. We shall welcome a new fact, we shall be the first to withdraw our accusation, we shall hasten to withdraw it. But now justice cries out, and we insist, we can withdraw nothing.” Here Ippolit Kirillovich passed to his finale. He was as though feverish, crying out for the blood that had been shed, the blood of a father murdered by his son “for the base purpose of robbery.” He firmly pointed to the tragic and clamorous accumulation of facts. “And whatever you may hear from the defendant’s counsel, so famous for his talent”—Ippolit Kirillovich could not restrain himself—“whatever eloquent and moving words may sound here, aimed at your tender feelings, remember all the same that at this moment you stand in the sanctuary of our justice. Remember that you are the defenders of our truth, defenders of our sacred Russia, of her foundations, her family, of everything she holds holy! Yes, at this moment you represent Russia here, and your verdict will resound not only in this courtroom but throughout Russia, and all Russia will listen to you as her defenders and judges, and will be encouraged or dismayed by your verdict. Do not torment Russia and her hopes: our fateful troika is hurtling headlong, perhaps toward destruction. And for a long time now, throughout Russia, people have stretched out their hands and cried for this mad, unbridled gallop to be stopped. And if other nations still move aside for the troika racing headlong, perhaps it is not at all out of respect for her, as the poet wished, but simply out of terror—mark that. Out of terror, perhaps even disgust; and it is still fortunate that they move aside, for they may suddenly stop moving aside and stand like a solid wall before the charging apparition, and themselves halt the mad gallop of our lawlessness, to save themselves, enlightenment, and civilization! We have already heard these anxious voices from Europe. They are beginning to make themselves heard. Do not provoke them, do not swell their ever-growing hatred with a verdict that acquits a son of the murder of his own father!…”

In a word, although Ippolit Kirillovich got very carried away, he did finish on a note of pathos—and indeed the impression he made was extraordinary. He himself hurried out after finishing his speech and, I repeat, almost fainted in another room. The courtroom did not applaud, but the serious people were satisfied. Only the ladies were less satisfied, though even they liked the eloquence, particularly since they had no fears about the outcome and expected everything from Fetyukovich: “At last he will speak, and of course he will defeat them all!” Everyone glanced at Mitya. He had sat silently throughout the prosecutor’s speech, his hands clenched, his teeth set, his eyes lowered. Only occasionally did he raise his head and listen, particularly when Grushenka was discussed. When the prosecutor repeated Rakitin’s opinion of her, a contemptuous, malicious smile appeared on his face, and he said quite audibly: “Bernards!” But when Ippolit Kirillovich described how he had questioned and tormented him in Mokroye, Mitya raised his head and listened with terrible curiosity. At one point he even seemed about to jump up and shout something, but restrained himself and merely gave a contemptuous shrug. Later, people in our society discussed this finale of the speech, specifically the prosecutor’s exploits in Mokroye while questioning the criminal, and made fun of Ippolit Kirillovich: “The man couldn’t resist boasting of his abilities.” The hearing was adjourned, but only very briefly, for a quarter of an hour, twenty minutes at most.
