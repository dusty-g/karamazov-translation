# Book VIII — Mitya
## V. A Sudden Decision

<!-- B08-C05-P001 -->
She was sitting in the kitchen with her grandmother; both were getting ready for bed. Relying on Nazar Ivanovich, they had once again left the door unlocked from inside. Mitya burst in, flung himself at Fenya, and seized her tightly by the throat.

<!-- B08-C05-P002 -->
“Tell me this instant where she is, who she’s with in Mokroye!” he yelled in a frenzy.

<!-- B08-C05-P003 -->
Both women shrieked.

<!-- B08-C05-P004 -->
“Oh, I’ll tell you, dear Dmitri Fyodorovich, I’ll tell you everything at once, I won’t hide a thing!” the terrified Fenya cried in a breathless rush. “She’s gone to Mokroye, to the officer.”

<!-- B08-C05-P005 -->
“What officer?” Mitya roared.

<!-- B08-C05-P006 -->
“The old officer, that very one, her old one, the one from five years ago, who left her and went away,” Fenya rattled off just as breathlessly.

<!-- B08-C05-P007 -->
Dmitri Fyodorovich took his hands from her throat. He stood before her pale as a corpse and speechless, but his eyes showed that he had understood everything at once—everything, everything at once, from her first words, down to the last detail—and had guessed it all. Poor Fenya was in no position, of course, to observe at that moment whether he understood or not. She had been sitting on a chest when he came in, and there she remained, trembling all over, her hands held out in front of her as though to protect herself, frozen in that position. Her frightened eyes, their pupils dilated with terror, were fixed on him. And, to make matters worse, both his hands were smeared with blood. On his way, as he ran, he must have touched his forehead with them to wipe the sweat from his face, for red smears of blood remained on his forehead and right cheek as well. Fenya was on the verge of hysterics, while the old cook had jumped up and stared like a madwoman, almost fainting. Dmitri Fyodorovich stood there for a minute and suddenly sank mechanically onto a chair beside Fenya.

<!-- B08-C05-P008 -->
He sat, not so much thinking as frightened, as though in a kind of stupor. But everything was clear as day: this officer—he knew about him, knew all about him, knew from Grushenka herself, knew he had sent a letter a month ago. So for a month, a whole month, the affair had been going on in profound secrecy from him, right up to this new man’s arrival now, and he had not even thought of him! But how could he, how could he not have thought of him? Why had he simply forgotten this officer then, forgotten him the moment he learned about him? That question stood before him like some monster. And he contemplated that monster in real terror, chilled with terror. But suddenly he began speaking to Fenya quietly and gently, like a quiet, affectionate child, just as though he had forgotten how he had frightened, hurt, and tormented her only a moment earlier. Suddenly he began questioning her with extraordinary precision, astonishing in his condition. And Fenya, though staring wildly at his bloody hands, began answering each question with equally astonishing eagerness and haste, as if anxious to tell him the whole “true and honest truth.” Little by little she began relating every detail almost with joy, with no wish at all to torment him, but as though hurrying with all her heart and all her might to do him a service. She told him the whole day’s events down to the smallest detail: Rakitin and Alyosha’s visit, how she herself had kept watch, how her mistress had left, and how she had called from the window to Alyosha to give her love to him, Mitenka, and tell him to “remember forever how she’d loved him for one little hour.” On hearing about her greeting, Mitya suddenly smiled, and color flared in his pale cheeks. At that same moment Fenya said, no longer the least afraid of showing curiosity:

<!-- B08-C05-P009 -->
“Look at your hands, Dmitri Fyodorovich, they’re covered in blood!”

<!-- B08-C05-P010 -->
“Yes,” Mitya answered mechanically, glanced absentmindedly at his hands, and immediately forgot both them and Fenya’s question. He fell silent again. It was already some twenty minutes since he had burst in. His earlier terror had passed, but plainly some new, unbending resolve had now taken complete possession of him. Suddenly he rose and smiled thoughtfully.

<!-- B08-C05-P011 -->
“Sir, what’s happened to you?” Fenya said, again pointing to his hands. She spoke with pity, as though she were now the person closest to him in his grief.

<!-- B08-C05-P012 -->
Mitya looked at his hands again.

<!-- B08-C05-P013 -->
“It’s blood, Fenya,” he said, looking at her with a strange expression. “It’s human blood, and God, why was it shed! But… Fenya… there’s a fence here”—he looked at her as though posing a riddle—“a high fence, terrible to look at, but… tomorrow at dawn, when ‘the sun soars up,’ Mitenka will jump over that fence… You don’t understand what fence I mean, Fenya. Well, never mind… it makes no difference, tomorrow you’ll hear and understand everything… and now goodbye! I won’t get in the way, I’ll step aside, I’ll know how to step aside. Live, my joy… you loved me for an hour, so remember Mitenka Karamazov forever… She always called me Mitenka, remember?”

<!-- B08-C05-P014 -->
And with these words he suddenly left the kitchen. His leaving frightened Fenya almost more than his bursting in earlier and attacking her.

<!-- B08-C05-P015 -->
Exactly ten minutes later Dmitri Fyodorovich came to the young official, Pyotr Ilyich Perkhotin, with whom he had pawned his pistols earlier. It was already half past eight, and Pyotr Ilyich, having had tea at home, had just put his frock coat back on to go to the Capital City tavern for a game of billiards. Mitya caught him on his way out. Seeing him and his blood-smeared face, he cried out:

<!-- B08-C05-P016 -->
“Good Lord! What’s happened to you?”

<!-- B08-C05-P017 -->
“Well,” Mitya said quickly, “I’ve come for my pistols and brought you your money. With thanks. I’m in a hurry, Pyotr Ilyich, please be quick.”

<!-- B08-C05-P018 -->
Pyotr Ilyich grew more and more astonished: he suddenly noticed a mass of money in Mitya’s hands; above all, he was holding it and had walked in with it as no one ever holds money or walks in with it. He carried all the banknotes in his right hand, as though displaying them, holding his hand straight out in front of him. The official’s servant boy, who met Mitya in the hall, said afterward that he had entered the hall like that too, with the money in his hand; evidently he had carried it through the street in the same way, out in front of him in his right hand. The notes were all rainbow-colored hundred-ruble bills, held by his bloody fingers. When interested parties later asked Pyotr Ilyich how much money there had been, he stated that it was difficult to count by eye at the time: perhaps two thousand, perhaps three, but it was a large bundle, “nice and thick.” Dmitri Fyodorovich himself, as he likewise testified afterward, “seemed quite beside himself too, but not drunk, more as though in a kind of ecstasy, very distracted and at the same time apparently absorbed, as if thinking about something, trying to work it out and unable to decide. He was in a terrible hurry, answered abruptly and very strangely, yet at moments seemed not at all distressed but even cheerful.”

<!-- B08-C05-P019 -->
“But what’s happened to you, what’s happened to you just now?” Pyotr Ilyich cried again, staring wildly at his guest. “How did you get so bloody? Did you fall? Just look!”

<!-- B08-C05-P020 -->
He seized him by the elbow and put him in front of the mirror. At the sight of his blood-smeared face, Mitya started and frowned angrily.

<!-- B08-C05-P021 -->
“Oh, damn it! That’s all I needed,” he muttered furiously, quickly transferred the notes from his right hand to his left, and jerked the handkerchief from his pocket. But the handkerchief too was covered in blood—it was the one he had used to wipe Grigory’s head and face. Hardly a white spot remained, and it had not so much begun to dry as stiffened into a lump that would not unfold. Mitya angrily flung it to the floor.

<!-- B08-C05-P022 -->
“Oh, damn it! Have you got a rag of some sort… to wipe myself…”

<!-- B08-C05-P023 -->
“So you’re only stained, not wounded? Then you’d better wash,” Pyotr Ilyich replied. “Here’s the washstand. I’ll help you.”

<!-- B08-C05-P024 -->
“A washstand? That’s good… only where am I to put this?” In a now utterly strange bewilderment he indicated his bundle of hundred-ruble notes to Pyotr Ilyich, looking questioningly at him as though it were for him to decide where Mitya should put his own money.

<!-- B08-C05-P025 -->
“Put it in your pocket or lay it here on the table. It won’t disappear.”

<!-- B08-C05-P026 -->
“In my pocket? Yes, in my pocket. That’s good… No, look, this is all nonsense!” he exclaimed, as though suddenly emerging from his distraction. “Look: let’s finish this business first, the pistols. Give them to me, and here’s your money… because I need them very, very badly… and there’s no time, no time at all…”

<!-- B08-C05-P027 -->
Taking the top hundred-ruble note from the bundle, he held it out to the official.

<!-- B08-C05-P028 -->
“But I haven’t got change,” the latter observed. “Haven’t you anything smaller?”

<!-- B08-C05-P029 -->
“No,” said Mitya, looking again at the bundle, and, as though uncertain of his own words, he felt the top two or three notes. “No, they’re all the same,” he added, looking questioningly at Pyotr Ilyich again.

<!-- B08-C05-P030 -->
“But how did you get so rich?” the other asked. “Wait, I’ll send my boy to Plotnikov’s. They close late—perhaps they’ll change it. Hey, Misha!” he called into the hall.

<!-- B08-C05-P031 -->
“Plotnikov’s shop—an excellent idea!” Mitya cried too, as though a thought had struck him. “Misha,” he said, turning to the boy as he came in, “look, run to Plotnikov’s and say Dmitri Fyodorovich sends his compliments and will be there himself directly… But listen, listen: tell them to have champagne ready when he gets there, about three dozen bottles, and to pack it up just as they did when he went to Mokroye… I took four dozen from them then,” he suddenly turned to Pyotr Ilyich. “They know, don’t worry, Misha,” he turned back to the boy. “And listen: cheese, Strasbourg pies, smoked whitefish, ham, caviar, well, everything, everything they have, about a hundred or a hundred and twenty rubles’ worth, like before… And listen: don’t let them forget the treats, sweets, pears, two or three watermelons, or four—no, one watermelon’s enough—and chocolate, candy, fruit drops, toffee—everything they packed for me for Mokroye that time, three hundred rubles’ worth with the champagne… Well, exactly the same again now. And remember, Misha, if you are Misha… His name is Misha, isn’t it?” he turned to Pyotr Ilyich again.

<!-- B08-C05-P032 -->
“But wait,” Pyotr Ilyich interrupted, listening to him and watching him uneasily. “You’d better go yourself and tell them then. He’ll get it wrong.”

<!-- B08-C05-P033 -->
“He’ll get it wrong, I can see he’ll get it wrong! Oh, Misha, and I was going to kiss you for doing the errand… If you don’t get it wrong, there’s ten rubles for you. Hurry off… Champagne, above all make them bring out the champagne, and a little cognac, and red wine, and white, and all of that, like last time… They know what it was like last time.”

<!-- B08-C05-P034 -->
“But listen!” Pyotr Ilyich interrupted, impatient now. “I’m saying let him just run and change the money and tell them not to close, and you can go and tell them yourself… Give me your banknote. Off you go, Misha, and be back in a flash!” Pyotr Ilyich seemed deliberately to get Misha out as quickly as possible, for ever since stopping in front of the guest, gaping at his bloody face and blood-covered hands with the bundle of money in his trembling fingers, the boy had stood openmouthed in astonishment and fear, and probably understood little of all Mitya’s instructions.

<!-- B08-C05-P035 -->
“Now let’s get you washed,” Pyotr Ilyich said sternly. “Put the money on the table or in your pocket… That’s it. Come along. And take off your coat.”

<!-- B08-C05-P036 -->
He began helping him off with his coat, and suddenly cried out again:

<!-- B08-C05-P037 -->
“Look, your coat’s bloody too!”

<!-- B08-C05-P038 -->
“That… that’s not the coat. Only a little here on the sleeve… And this is only here where the handkerchief was. It seeped through the pocket. I sat on the handkerchief at Fenya’s, and the blood seeped through,” Mitya immediately explained with a sort of astonishing trustfulness. Pyotr Ilyich listened, frowning.

<!-- B08-C05-P039 -->
“You’ve certainly managed it. You must have been fighting with someone,” he muttered.

<!-- B08-C05-P040 -->
They began washing. Pyotr Ilyich held the jug and poured the water. Mitya was in a hurry and at first barely soaped his hands. (His hands were shaking, as Pyotr Ilyich remembered afterward.) Pyotr Ilyich immediately told him to use more soap and rub harder. At that moment he seemed to be gaining a kind of ascendancy over Mitya, increasingly so. Let us note in passing that the young man was not timid by nature.

<!-- B08-C05-P041 -->
“Look, you haven’t cleaned under your nails. Now scrub your face, here: at your temples, by your ear… Are you going in that shirt? Where are you going? Look, your whole right cuff is covered in blood.”

<!-- B08-C05-P042 -->
“Yes, in blood,” Mitya observed, examining his shirt cuff.

<!-- B08-C05-P043 -->
“Then change your shirt.”

<!-- B08-C05-P044 -->
“No time. But I’ll do this, you see…” Mitya continued with the same trustfulness, already drying his face and hands with the towel and putting on his coat. “I’ll turn back the edge of the sleeve here, and it won’t show under the coat… See!”

<!-- B08-C05-P045 -->
“Now tell me where you managed to do this. Been fighting someone? Not in the tavern again, like last time? Not the captain again, like last time, beating him and dragging him about?” Pyotr Ilyich recalled it almost reproachfully. “Who else have you beaten… or perhaps killed?”

<!-- B08-C05-P046 -->
“Nonsense!” said Mitya.

<!-- B08-C05-P047 -->
“What do you mean, nonsense?”

<!-- B08-C05-P048 -->
“Never mind,” said Mitya, and suddenly grinned. “I’ve just run over an old woman in the square.”

<!-- B08-C05-P049 -->
“Run over her? An old woman?”

<!-- B08-C05-P050 -->
“An old man!” Mitya shouted, looking Pyotr Ilyich straight in the face, laughing and shouting at him as though he were deaf.

<!-- B08-C05-P051 -->
“Oh, damn it, an old man, an old woman… Have you killed someone?”

<!-- B08-C05-P052 -->
“We made it up. We had a tussle—and made it up. Somewhere. Parted as friends. One fool… he’s forgiven me, he’s certainly forgiven me now… If he got up, he wouldn’t forgive me,” Mitya suddenly winked. “Only, you know, to hell with him, do you hear, Pyotr Ilyich? To hell with it, leave it alone! I don’t want to now!” Mitya cut him off decisively.

<!-- B08-C05-P053 -->
“All I mean is, why must you get into scrapes with everyone… like that staff captain, over nothing. You’ve had a fight and now you’re rushing off on a spree—that’s just like you. Three dozen champagne—where do you need all that?”

<!-- B08-C05-P054 -->
“Bravo! Now give me the pistols. Honestly, there’s no time. I’d like to talk to you, my dear fellow, but there’s no time. And there’s no need anyway; it’s too late to talk. Ah! Where’s the money, where did I put it?” he cried, thrusting his hands into his pockets.

<!-- B08-C05-P055 -->
“You put it on the table… yourself… there it is. Forgotten? Money really is like rubbish or water to you. Here are your pistols. Strange: you pawned them for ten rubles after five this afternoon, and now look at you, with your thousands. Two or three, I daresay?”

<!-- B08-C05-P056 -->
“Three, I daresay,” Mitya laughed, putting the money into the side pocket of his trousers.

<!-- B08-C05-P057 -->
“You’ll lose it that way. Have you got gold mines or something?”

<!-- B08-C05-P058 -->
“Mines? Gold mines!” Mitya shouted at the top of his voice and burst out laughing. “Would you like to go to the mines, Perkhotin? There’s a lady here who’ll hand you three thousand on the spot, just to get you to go. She handed it to me; that’s how she loves the mines! Do you know Khokhlakov?”

<!-- B08-C05-P059 -->
“We haven’t met, but I’ve heard of her and seen her. Did she really give you three thousand? Just handed it over?” Pyotr Ilyich looked incredulous.

<!-- B08-C05-P060 -->
“Well, tomorrow, when the sun soars up, when eternally youthful Phoebus soars up, praising and glorifying God, tomorrow go to her, to Khokhlakov, and ask her yourself whether she handed me three thousand or not. Make inquiries.”

<!-- B08-C05-P061 -->
“I don’t know your relationship… if you say so positively, she must have given it… And you’ve got the money in your paws, all three thousand, and instead of Siberia… But where are you actually going now?”

<!-- B08-C05-P062 -->
“To Mokroye.”

<!-- B08-C05-P063 -->
“To Mokroye? But it’s night!”

<!-- B08-C05-P064 -->
“Mastryuk once had everything, now Mastryuk’s got nothing!” Mitya suddenly said.

<!-- B08-C05-P065 -->
“What do you mean, nothing? With all those thousands, nothing?”

<!-- B08-C05-P066 -->
“I don’t mean the thousands. To hell with the thousands! I’m talking about woman’s nature:

> Woman’s nature is credulous,  
> And fickle, and corrupt.

I agree with Ulysses; he’s the one who says it.”

<!-- B08-C05-P067 -->
“I don’t understand you!”

<!-- B08-C05-P068 -->
“Am I drunk, then?”

<!-- B08-C05-P069 -->
“Not drunk—worse.”

<!-- B08-C05-P070 -->
“I’m drunk in spirit, Pyotr Ilyich, drunk in spirit, and enough, enough…”

<!-- B08-C05-P071 -->
“What are you doing, loading a pistol?”

<!-- B08-C05-P072 -->
“Loading a pistol.”

<!-- B08-C05-P073 -->
Mitya had indeed opened the pistol case, uncapped the powder horn, and carefully poured in and tamped down a charge. Then he took a bullet and, before rolling it in, held it up between two fingers in front of him, over the candle.

<!-- B08-C05-P074 -->
“Why are you looking at the bullet?” Pyotr Ilyich watched him with anxious curiosity.

<!-- B08-C05-P075 -->
“No reason. Imagination. Now, if you meant to put this bullet into your brain, would you look at it while you were loading the pistol, or not?”

<!-- B08-C05-P076 -->
“Why look at it?”

<!-- B08-C05-P077 -->
“It’s going into my brain, so I’m curious to see what it’s like… But that’s nonsense, a moment’s nonsense. There, it’s done,” he added, rolling in the bullet and ramming it home with tow. “Pyotr Ilyich, my dear fellow, nonsense, it’s all nonsense, and if you only knew how much nonsense! Now give me a scrap of paper.”

<!-- B08-C05-P078 -->
“Here’s some paper.”

<!-- B08-C05-P079 -->
“No, smooth, clean paper, the kind you write on. That’s it.” And Mitya seized a pen from the table, quickly wrote two lines on the paper, folded it in four, and put it in his waistcoat pocket. He put the pistols in the case, locked it with the little key, and picked it up. Then he looked at Pyotr Ilyich and gave a long, thoughtful smile.

<!-- B08-C05-P080 -->
“Now let’s go,” he said.

<!-- B08-C05-P081 -->
“Go where? No, wait… Perhaps you mean to put it into your brain, that bullet…” Pyotr Ilyich said anxiously.

<!-- B08-C05-P082 -->
“The bullet’s nonsense! I want to live, I love life! Know that. I love golden-haired Phoebus and his hot light… Dear Pyotr Ilyich, do you know how to step aside?”

<!-- B08-C05-P083 -->
“What do you mean, step aside?”

<!-- B08-C05-P084 -->
“Make way. Make way for someone you love and someone you hate. And let the hated one become dear too—that’s how to make way! And tell them: God be with you, go, pass me by, and I…”

<!-- B08-C05-P085 -->
“And you?”

<!-- B08-C05-P086 -->
“Enough, let’s go.”

<!-- B08-C05-P087 -->
“I swear I’ll tell someone,” Pyotr Ilyich said, looking at him, “to stop you from going there. Why do you need to go to Mokroye now?”

<!-- B08-C05-P088 -->
“There’s a woman there, a woman, and that’s enough for you, Pyotr Ilyich, and that’s that!”

<!-- B08-C05-P089 -->
“Listen, wild as you are, somehow I’ve always liked you… that’s why I’m worried.”

<!-- B08-C05-P090 -->
“Thank you, brother. I’m wild, you say. Savages, savages! That’s all I keep saying: savages! Ah yes, here’s Misha, and I’d forgotten him.”

<!-- B08-C05-P091 -->
Misha hurried in with the bundle of changed money and reported that at Plotnikov’s “everyone was on the move,” hauling out bottles, fish, and tea; everything would be ready in a moment. Mitya snatched a ten-ruble note and handed it to Pyotr Ilyich, then tossed another to Misha.

<!-- B08-C05-P092 -->
“Don’t you dare!” Pyotr Ilyich cried. “Not in my house, and it’s wicked spoiling besides. Put your money away, put it here, why throw it around? You’ll need it tomorrow; you’ll come back to me asking for ten rubles. Why do you keep shoving it into your side pocket? You’ll lose it!”

<!-- B08-C05-P093 -->
“Listen, my dear fellow, shall we go to Mokroye together?”

<!-- B08-C05-P094 -->
“What would I go there for?”

<!-- B08-C05-P095 -->
“Listen, shall I open a bottle now and we’ll drink to life? I want a drink, and most of all I want to drink with you. I’ve never drunk with you, have I?”

<!-- B08-C05-P096 -->
“All right, at the tavern. Come along, I’m going there now myself.”

<!-- B08-C05-P097 -->
“No time for the tavern. At Plotnikov’s shop, in the back room. Shall I give you a riddle now?”

<!-- B08-C05-P098 -->
“Go ahead.”

<!-- B08-C05-P099 -->
Mitya took the paper from his waistcoat, unfolded it, and showed him. In large, clear handwriting it said:

<!-- B08-C05-P100 -->
“I execute myself for my whole life; I punish my whole life!”

<!-- B08-C05-P101 -->
“I really will tell someone, I’ll go and tell them now,” Pyotr Ilyich said after reading the paper.

<!-- B08-C05-P102 -->
“You won’t have time, my dear fellow. Come and have a drink. March!” Plotnikov’s shop was practically one house away from Pyotr Ilyich’s, on the street corner. It was the principal grocery in our town, owned by wealthy merchants, and a very good shop in its own right. It had everything you would find in any shop in the capital, every kind of grocery: wines “bottled by the Yeliseyev brothers,” fruit, cigars, tea, sugar, coffee, and so forth. Three shop assistants were always in attendance and two errand boys running about. Though our region had grown poor, the landowners moved away, and trade declined, the grocery flourished as before, indeed better and better with every year: there was no shortage of buyers for these things. Mitya was eagerly awaited at the shop. They remembered only too well how, three or four weeks earlier, he had in just the same way bought several hundred rubles’ worth of assorted goods and wines at once for ready cash—on credit, of course, they would have trusted him with nothing. They remembered how, just as now, a whole bundle of rainbow notes had stuck out of his hands and he had flung them about recklessly, without bargaining, without considering or wanting to consider why he needed so many goods, so much wine, and so on. The whole town said afterward that when he had driven off to Mokroye with Grushenka he had “blown three thousand at a stroke in one night and the following day, and come back from his spree without a penny, naked as the day he was born.” He had roused a whole camp of Gypsies, who happened to be staying in our area, and in two days, they said, the Gypsies had extracted untold money from him while he was drunk and drunk untold quantities of expensive wine. People said, laughing at Mitya, that in Mokroye he had plied clodhopping peasants with champagne and stuffed the village girls and women with sweets and Strasbourg pies. People here, particularly at the tavern, also laughed at Mitya’s own frank and public admission at the time—not to his face, of course; laughing in his face was somewhat dangerous—that all he had got from Grushenka during the entire “escapade” was that “she let him kiss her foot and let him do nothing more.”

<!-- B08-C05-P103 -->
When Mitya and Pyotr Ilyich reached the shop, they found a troika ready at the entrance, harnessed to a carpet-covered cart with bells and jingling harness bells, and the driver Andrei waiting for Mitya. In the shop they had almost finished “getting up” one crate of goods and were only waiting for Mitya to appear before nailing it shut and loading it onto the cart. Pyotr Ilyich was surprised.

<!-- B08-C05-P104 -->
“Where did you get a troika so quickly?” he asked Mitya.

<!-- B08-C05-P105 -->
“I met him, Andrei, as I was running to your place and told him to drive straight here to the shop. No time to waste! Last time I went with Timofey, but Timofey’s gone now, gone, gone, driven off ahead of me with a certain enchantress. Andrei, will we be very late?”

<!-- B08-C05-P106 -->
“They’ll only get there an hour ahead of us, if that—not more than an hour!” Andrei answered quickly. “I got Timofey ready myself; I know how they’ll travel. Their pace isn’t our pace, Dmitri Fyodorovich, they’re no match for us. They won’t be a full hour ahead!” Andrei broke in eagerly: a driver still young, a lean, reddish-haired fellow in a fitted coat, with a peasant overcoat over his left arm.

<!-- B08-C05-P107 -->
“Fifty rubles for a drink if you’re only an hour behind.”

<!-- B08-C05-P108 -->
“We guarantee the hour, Dmitri Fyodorovich. Ah, they won’t be half an hour ahead, never mind a whole hour!”

<!-- B08-C05-P109 -->
Although Mitya bustled about giving instructions, he spoke and gave orders strangely, in bits and pieces, with no sequence. He began one thing and forgot the ending. Pyotr Ilyich found it necessary to interfere and help things along.

<!-- B08-C05-P110 -->
“Four hundred rubles’ worth, no less than four hundred, exactly like last time,” Mitya ordered. “Four dozen champagne, not one bottle less.”

<!-- B08-C05-P111 -->
“Why do you need so much, what’s it for? Stop!” Pyotr Ilyich shouted. “What’s this crate? What’s in it? Surely there isn’t four hundred rubles’ worth here?”

<!-- B08-C05-P112 -->
The bustling assistants immediately explained in honeyed voices that this first crate contained only half a dozen bottles of champagne and “the various necessities to start with”: snacks, sweets, fruit drops, and so on. But the main “consignment” would be packed and sent off separately at once, just as last time, in a separate cart also drawn by a troika, and would arrive in good time, “only about an hour after Dmitri Fyodorovich reaches the place.”

<!-- B08-C05-P113 -->
“No more than an hour, mind, no more than an hour, and put in as many fruit drops and toffees as possible; the girls there like them,” Mitya insisted eagerly.

<!-- B08-C05-P114 -->
“Toffees, fine. But why do you need four dozen? One’s enough,” Pyotr Ilyich said, now almost angry. He began bargaining, demanded the bill, would not settle down. Yet he saved only a hundred rubles. They agreed that altogether no more than three hundred rubles’ worth of goods should be delivered.

<!-- B08-C05-P115 -->
“Oh, the devil take you!” Pyotr Ilyich exclaimed, as though suddenly coming to his senses. “What’s it to me? Throw your money away if you got it for nothing!”
